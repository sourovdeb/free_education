### D152 · What 'receptive' means in ELT
**Idea —** Receptive skills are listening and reading — skills where learners receive and decode language rather than produce it. Understanding them is the first step to teaching them well.  
**Example —** A student reads a news article: she receives and processes the text. That is receptive. When she writes a response, it becomes productive.  
**Try —** List two activities you have used for listening and two for reading. Which asked for gist? Which for detail?

---

### D153 · Top-down processing: using what you know
**Idea —** Top-down processing means using prior knowledge — topic, context, genre — to make sense of a text before fully decoding it. It is fast and efficient when schema is strong.  
**Example —** A learner sees a headline: 'Central bank raises rates.' Without reading further, she knows roughly what the article will argue. That is top-down at work.  
**Try —** Give learners a headline only. Ask: 'What do you predict this article is about?' Note how much they build from schema alone.

---

### D154 · Bottom-up processing: decoding the signal
**Idea —** Bottom-up processing means building meaning word by word, sound by sound, from the raw linguistic input. It is the slower, more effortful route — and the one weaker learners over-rely on.  
**Example —** A beginner hears fast speech and tries to catch each individual word. She is processing bottom-up — no spare capacity for context or prediction.  
**Try —** Play eight seconds of audio. Ask learners to write every word they catch. Debrief: which words were clear? Which were swallowed by connected speech?

---

### D155 · The gist task: one question, whole text
**Idea —** A gist task gives learners one broad question before they read or listen. It sets a purpose, activates schema, and stops word-by-word panic. One question only — more kills gist.  
**Example —** Gist task for a short podcast on remote work: 'Is the speaker for or against working from home?' That is it. No detail, no analysis — just the big picture.  
**Try —** Write a gist question for a text you use regularly. Check: is it answerable after one fast read? If not, it is too detailed — simplify.

---

### D156 · The detail task: the second pass
**Idea —** After the gist task, learners go back through the text for specific information. The detail task should be answerable only by close reading or careful listening — not guessable from context.  
**Example —** Detail task for the same podcast: 'Name two advantages the speaker gives for remote work.' This forces a return to the text rather than a recollection of the gist.  
**Try —** Take a reading text you use. Write three detail questions. Test each: can a learner answer it without re-reading? If yes, it is not a detail question.

---

### D157 · Blocking vocabulary: what stops comprehension
**Idea —** Blocking vocabulary is a word or phrase so unfamiliar it stops comprehension of the whole text — not just a nuance, but a key meaning that collapses without it.  
**Example —** In a text about immigration law, 'asylum seeker' is probably blocking. 'Paperwork' is not — learners can infer or skip it. Teach the blocker; let the rest emerge.  
**Try —** Select a text. Highlight every unknown word. Ask: if a B1 learner skipped each one, would meaning collapse? Only those that answer yes are true blockers.

---

### D158 · Pre-teaching vocabulary: the decision rule
**Idea —** Pre-teach vocabulary only if it blocks meaning and cannot be inferred. Pre-teaching too much removes the challenge; too little leaves learners stranded mid-text.  
**Example —** For a B2 reading text, you pre-teach 'austerity' but not 'budget.' The second is guessable from context; the first is opaque and central to the argument.  
**Try —** Before your next reading lesson, identify every potential unknown word. Apply the test: block + uninferrable? Pre-teach. Everything else? Let it go.

---

### D159 · Predicting before you listen or read
**Idea —** Prediction activates schema and creates a reason to engage. A two-minute lead-in — images, a title, one question — makes the whole listening or reading more efficient.  
**Example —** Before playing a news audio clip, show the headline and ask: 'What three things do you expect to hear?' Learners engage actively from the first second.  
**Try —** Design a 90-second lead-in for a listening text you use: one image or question, one prediction prompt. Time it with a stopwatch — lead-ins should be short.

---

### D160 · Authentic vs graded recordings: the trade-off
**Idea —** Authentic recordings expose learners to real spoken English — speed, overlaps, ellipsis. Graded recordings are clear but artificial. Neither is always right; choose by aim and level.  
**Example —** A graded recording at B1 uses full sentences and careful pacing. An authentic interview at the same level trips learners with 'gonna,' false starts, and mumbled endings.  
**Try —** Listen to 20 seconds of a graded and an authentic recording on the same topic. List the differences. Which would you use for a gist task? Which for awareness of natural speech?

---

### D161 · Why native speed confuses learners
**Idea —** Connected speech — assimilation, elision, linking, weak forms — makes native-speed English sound unlike the written form. Learners who know the words still cannot parse the stream.  
**Example —** 'Did you eat yet?' becomes 'Dij'ya eet yet?' in fast speech. The words are known; the phonological reduction makes them unrecognisable. That is the real barrier.  
**Try —** Dictate 'What are you going to do?' at natural speed. Count how many catch it. Then write it on the board. Discuss the gap between what they heard and what was said.

---

### D162 · Skimming: reading fast for the big picture
**Idea —** Skimming is fast reading for global meaning — what is this text about, what is the writer's position. Learners skim by reading the first sentence of each paragraph and the conclusion.  
**Example —** Give learners 90 seconds to skim a 400-word article. Ask: 'Is the writer for or against X?' They should be able to answer without reading every word.  
**Try —** Set a timer for 60 seconds. Hand learners a new text. Timer starts. They skim. When time is up, ask one gist question. The constraint builds the habit.

---

### D163 · Scanning: reading to find one thing
**Idea —** Scanning is fast reading to locate specific information — a name, a date, a figure — without reading everything. The key is knowing exactly what to look for before you start.  
**Example —** Scanning a timetable for the next train to Lyon: eyes move quickly over the page, ignoring everything except the Lyon column. Topic knowledge directs the search.  
**Try —** Give learners a page from a programme or a menu. Set a specific item to find. Time them. Discuss what they did with their eyes.

---

### D164 · Intensive reading: close attention on short text
**Idea —** Intensive reading focuses on a short passage — one paragraph, one exchange — with close attention to language. It is the opposite of skimming: slow, forensic, deliberate.  
**Example —** Learners read one paragraph of a graded short story twice, then answer questions about vocabulary, cohesion, and implied meaning. Depth, not speed, is the goal.  
**Try —** Choose one paragraph from a text you use. Write three language-focused questions: What does 'it' refer to? What does 'moreover' signal? What tense is used and why?

---

### D165 · The lead-in: why context before text always matters
**Idea —** The lead-in gives learners a reason to engage with a text before they see it. It activates relevant vocabulary and schema, reducing the cognitive load when they reach the text itself.  
**Example —** Before a reading on urban farming, show an image of a rooftop vegetable garden. Ask: 'Have you ever grown food? Would you?' Two minutes. Now the text has a human hook.  
**Try —** Plan a lead-in for a text in tomorrow's lesson. Include: one image or question, one prediction or opinion prompt. Keep it under three minutes total.

---

### D166 · The one-question rule for gist tasks
**Idea —** One gist question sends learners in with a clear, single purpose. More than one and learners start reading for detail; the gist pass becomes confused and slow.  
**Example —** Weak: 'Read and answer: What is the article about? Who is it written for? What examples are given?' That is three tasks, not one. Write one question and trust the task.  
**Try —** Take your next listening or reading gist task. Count the questions. If more than one, cut all but the most essential.

---

### D167 · Checking answers without choral collapse
**Idea —** When you check answers as a class, choral repetition of the right answer tells you nothing — one confident student speaks, others follow. Use targeted nomination or paired comparison instead.  
**Example —** Instead of 'What's the answer to 1?' try: 'Compare with your partner. Different answers? Tell me why.' Then nominate one pair to share.  
**Try —** In your next listening lesson, check answers using paired comparison first. Give two minutes, then ask pairs to report. Notice whether you hear more student reasoning.

---

### D168 · Text selection: what makes a good reading text
**Idea —** A good reading text has a clear information load, language at or just above learner level, a topic learners can connect to, and at least one reason to read beyond the language task.  
**Example —** A genuine newspaper column on a topic the class debated last week: good. A fabricated 'model text' with artificial density and no real writer's voice: weaker.  
**Try —** Evaluate a text you plan to use: (1) level appropriate? (2) topic engaging? (3) language teachable? (4) a real reason to read? If fewer than three yes, find a different text.

---

### D169 · Grading a text: simplify without distorting
**Idea —** When adapting an authentic text, cut difficult words and complex grammar — but preserve the writer's logic, the examples, and the argument structure. Distorting meaning defeats the purpose.  
**Example —** Authentic: 'The proposal, though lauded by analysts, met fierce resistance.' Graded: 'Analysts liked the plan. Others opposed it strongly.' The argument survives; the complexity falls away.  
**Try —** Take a paragraph from an authentic article above your class level. Rewrite it for B1. Check: is the main argument still intact? Did you remove an example? Put it back.

---

### D170 · Schema theory: what learners bring to a text
**Idea —** Schema is the network of prior knowledge a learner activates when reading or listening. Strong schema compensates for weak language; weak schema makes even familiar language confusing.  
**Example —** A learner with no knowledge of baseball reads a match report. Even basic past-tense sentences confuse her — she has no schema to anchor the events.  
**Try —** Before your next reading lesson, identify what schema learners need. Spend two minutes activating it: a photo, a question, a fact. Then notice whether comprehension changes.

---

### D171 · Jigsaw reading: split the text, share the meaning
**Idea —** In jigsaw reading, different groups read different parts of a text, then regroup to share what they know. The information gap creates a real communicative reason to speak.  
**Example —** Group A reads about the causes of a problem; Group B reads about the effects. They regroup in A+B pairs to build a complete picture — neither has it alone.  
**Try —** Take a 300-word text. Split it into two equal sections. Give each to a different group. Set a comprehension task for each part. Then regroup. Did the discussion feel purposeful?

---

### D172 · Reading for pleasure: why it belongs in class
**Idea —** Extensive reading — reading for pleasure, without tasks or comprehension checks — builds vocabulary incidentally, increases reading speed, and raises motivation. Nation's research supports it strongly.  
**Example —** A learner who reads one graded reader per month at her level for a year will encounter high-frequency vocabulary hundreds of times in natural context — no flashcard required.  
**Try —** Recommend one graded reader at the right level to each learner today. No comprehension task — just 'What did you think of it?' is enough.

---

### D173 · The 'read and discuss' trap
**Idea —** Giving learners a text and immediately asking 'What do you think?' skips the comprehension stage entirely. Learners end up discussing what they brought to the text, not what is in it.  
**Example —** After a text on artificial intelligence, the teacher asks: 'Do you think AI is dangerous?' Learners share opinions they held before reading. The text became decoration.  
**Try —** After your next reading task, add one question that can only be answered by the text: 'What specific danger does the author name in paragraph 3?' Then open the discussion.

---

### D174 · Listening for attitude: reading between the lines
**Idea —** Attitude tasks ask learners to infer how the speaker feels — interested, sceptical, enthusiastic — from tone, word choice, and hedging, not just from the literal content.  
**Example —** A speaker says 'Well, it is an interesting approach...' with a falling tone and a pause. Literal meaning: positive. Implied meaning: doubt.  
**Try —** Play 30 seconds of interview audio. Ask: 'Does the speaker seem convinced? Find the word or moment that tells you.' Compare answers in pairs before class discussion.

---

### D175 · Stress and weak forms in real listening
**Idea —** In natural speech, content words carry stress and are clear; function words — articles, auxiliaries, prepositions — are reduced to weak forms. Learners who do not know this miss them completely.  
**Example —** 'Can you tell me what time it is?' becomes 'k'n y' tell me w't time it'z?' in fast speech. The unstressed syllables become schwa. Train the ear explicitly.  
**Try —** Write four sentences. Say each at natural speed. Underline the stressed syllables. Ask learners to compare what they heard with what you wrote.

---

### D176 · Transcription tasks: a tool, not a crutch
**Idea —** Transcription — writing out what you hear word for word — is powerful for raising phonological awareness, but overused as a main listening task it trains caution, not fluency.  
**Example —** Transcribe 10 seconds of authentic speech: it reveals every gap between written and spoken forms. But a whole lesson of transcription slows listening speed rather than building it.  
**Try —** Use a ten-second transcription task at the end of a listening lesson, not the start. Debrief: where did connected speech fool them? That is the teaching point.

---

### D177 · Using images as a pre-listening scaffold
**Idea —** Images activate schema and emotion before learners hear a word. A photo of the speaker, the setting, or the topic gives context that reduces the cognitive load of the first listen.  
**Example —** Before playing an interview with a chef, show a photo of a busy restaurant kitchen. Ask: 'What is this person's job like?' Two minutes of prediction; the listening becomes more manageable.  
**Try —** Choose an image connected to your next listening text. Show it for 60 seconds. Ask two questions: 'What do you see?' and 'What do you predict you will hear?' Then play the audio.

---

### D178 · The follow-up task: text to learner life
**Idea —** The follow-up task connects what learners read or heard to their own experience. It ensures the lesson ends with the learner as subject, not the text — and gives speaking or writing practice.  
**Example —** After reading about remote work, the follow-up is: 'Would you prefer to work from home? Tell your partner two reasons.' The text sparked the topic; now learners own it.  
**Try —** Take a recent reading lesson. What was the final task? Was it text-based or learner-centred? Rewrite it to start with 'In your experience...' or 'Have you ever...'

---

### D179 · Choosing the right sub-skill for your lesson aim
**Idea —** Match the sub-skill to the aim. A lesson on scanning should not start with an intensive comprehension task — that is a different skill entirely.  
**Example —** Aim: 'Learners can locate specific data in a table.' Sub-skill: scanning. Task: find three figures in the table without reading the surrounding text.  
**Try —** Write your lesson aim for tomorrow's reading lesson. Then ask: what sub-skill does that aim require? Is your main task actually practising that sub-skill? Adjust if not.

---

### D180 · Staging a receptive skills lesson end-to-end
**Idea —** A complete receptive lesson has five clear stages: lead-in, pre-teach blocking vocab, gist task, detail task, follow-up. Remove any stage and comprehension or motivation suffers.  
**Example —** Lead-in (2 min) → pre-teach two blockers (3 min) → gist question, first listen (4 min) → detail task, second listen (6 min) → discuss with partner (5 min). Total: 20 minutes.  
**Try —** Map your next reading lesson onto those five stages. Where does yours deviate? Which stage is longest? Is the follow-up productive or does it end with answer-checking?
