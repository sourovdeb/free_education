### D152 · What 'receptive skills' means
**Idea —** Receptive skills — reading and listening — are how learners take in language. They receive meaning without producing it. Both require active processing, not passive absorption.
**Example —** A learner reads a news article without speaking a word, yet the brain is predicting, inferring, and checking meaning throughout.
**Try —** Think of the last time you learned something in a foreign language. Were you reading or listening? What made it easy or hard?

---

### D153 · Reading vs listening — two different challenges
**Idea —** Reading is self-paced; learners can re-read. Listening is real-time — the sound disappears. Each demands different sub-skills and different lesson staging.
**Example —** A B2 reader who handles a newspaper article fluently may still struggle with the same article read aloud at natural speed.
**Try —** List one skill a reader needs that a listener does not, and one a listener needs that a reader does not.

---

### D154 · Top-down processing — using what you already know
**Idea —** Top-down processing means using prior knowledge, context, and expectations to predict and understand a text. Learners draw on what they know about the world to fill gaps.
**Example —** Seeing the headline 'Earthquake hits Japan' activates schema: location, damage, rescue. A learner predicts the article before reading a word.
**Try —** Show learners a headline only. Ask: what will this text say? What do you already know about this topic?

---

### D155 · Bottom-up processing — building meaning from words
**Idea —** Bottom-up processing starts with individual sounds or words and builds meaning upward. Learners decode the text piece by piece rather than using context or schema.
**Example —** A learner who doesn't know the topic uses bottom-up processing to decode an unfamiliar medical text, word by word, sentence by sentence.
**Try —** Give learners a short text with no title or images. Can they still understand it? Notice where bottom-up processing works hardest.

---

### D156 · Interactive processing — when both work together
**Idea —** Skilled readers and listeners use top-down and bottom-up processing simultaneously, switching as needed. Teaching both builds real comprehension rather than reliance on one strategy.
**Example —** A learner hears 'syringe' (bottom-up) and immediately activates a hospital schema (top-down). The two feed each other.
**Try —** In your next lesson: where are learners using top-down strategies? Where are they stuck on words (bottom-up)?

---

### D157 · The gist task — one question before they read
**Idea —** A gist task asks for the general idea of a text, not specific detail. Set it before learners read or listen. One question. Whole text. First pass only.
**Example —** Before listening: 'What is the main topic of this conversation?' Not: 'What time does the flight leave?' That is detail, not gist.
**Try —** Write one gist question for a text you use regularly. Check: does it require the whole text, or just one paragraph?

---

### D158 · Scanning — finding specific information fast
**Idea —** Scanning means searching a text for a specific piece of information — a name, a number, a date — without reading everything. It is a real-world skill, not a test trick.
**Example —** Scanning a train timetable for the 09:15 departure: eyes move quickly, most of the text is ignored. That is the skill in action.
**Try —** Give learners a timetable or menu. Set a time limit. How fast can they find three specific details?

---

### D159 · Skimming — getting the main idea fast
**Idea —** Skimming means reading quickly for the general idea, not detail. Readers move fast, taking in headings, first sentences, and key words. Not every text rewards it equally.
**Example —** Skimming a news article in 30 seconds to decide if it is worth a full read — most of the text is deliberately skipped.
**Try —** Give learners 45 seconds with a long text. Ask: what was it about? They should not have time to read it fully.

---

### D160 · Intensive reading — reading every word
**Idea —** Intensive reading means slow, careful reading for full comprehension: every word matters. It is the basis of detailed comprehension tasks and language analysis work.
**Example —** Reading a contract clause or a grammar explanation: one missed word can change the meaning entirely. That is intensive reading.
**Try —** Give learners a short, dense paragraph. Ask them to find two unknown words and guess meaning from context. Then check.

---

### D161 · Pre-teaching vocabulary — which words to unblock first
**Idea —** Pre-teach only the vocabulary that would block comprehension — two to four words maximum. Do not pre-teach everything unusual; that removes the inferring work learners need.
**Example —** For a text about surgery, pre-teach 'anaesthetic' if it is key to the gist. Leave every technical term for learners to infer.
**Try —** Read through a text you use. Identify the one or two words without which learners could not complete the gist task.

---

### D162 · Lead-in activities — switching on the schema
**Idea —** A lead-in activates what learners already know about a topic before they meet the text. It primes top-down processing, generates interest, and sets context. Keep it short.
**Example —** Before a text about smartphones: 'What did you use your phone for this morning? Three things. Thirty seconds.' That is a lead-in.
**Try —** Write a one-minute lead-in for a text you teach. Make it personal and spoken, not written.

---

### D163 · Setting context before a text
**Idea —** Context is the situation around a text: who wrote it, for whom, why, and where it appears. Setting context helps learners engage appropriately and reduces processing load.
**Example —** Before a listening: 'Two friends are deciding where to eat. One has a food allergy.' That is context — not a spoiler.
**Try —** For a listening you use, write one sentence of context. Does it give the situation without giving away the gist-task answer?

---

### D164 · True / False / Not Given — the tricky third option
**Idea —** 'Not Given' is not 'False.' False means the text contradicts the statement. Not Given means the text is silent on it. Learners confuse these constantly.
**Example —** Text: 'The museum opens at 9.' Statement: 'The museum closes at 5.' Answer: Not Given — the text says nothing about closing time.
**Try —** Write one True, one False, and one Not Given statement for a text you use. Test a colleague. Where do they stumble?

---

### D165 · Multiple-choice traps — why learners guess wrong
**Idea —** Multiple-choice distractors often use words from the text in wrong combinations. Learners pattern-match on familiar words instead of reading for meaning.
**Example —** Text: 'The train was delayed by fog.' Option C: 'The train was cancelled due to fog.' Learners who skimmed often tick C.
**Try —** Write one multiple-choice item for a text you use. Include one distractor that borrows text vocabulary but alters the meaning.

---

### D166 · Types of listening — casual, selective, attentive
**Idea —** We listen in three main ways: casually (background awareness), selectively (targeting one thing), and attentively (full focus, active processing). Match your task type to the listening purpose.
**Example —** Podcast while cooking = casual. Flight number in a noisy airport = selective. A lecture you will be tested on = attentive.
**Try —** Think of one classroom listening task. Which type does it require? Is that the hardest type for your learners?

---

### D167 · Listening for gist — the first listen
**Idea —** The first listen should always be for gist — one question, whole recording. Do not pause, rewind, or clarify during this listen. Learners need to build tolerance for ambiguity.
**Example —** First listen: 'What is the relationship between the two speakers?' Not: 'What did the woman order?' That is detail work.
**Try —** Set a gist task for a listening you normally use for detail work. What changes in how learners approach it?

---

### D168 · Listening for detail — the second listen
**Idea —** After the gist listen, set a detailed task for the second listen. Learners now search for specific information and may need a third listen or a pause.
**Example —** Second listen: 'What three things does the man suggest? Write them down.' This demands focused attention and note-taking.
**Try —** Design a detail task — three to four specific questions — for a listening you already use. Make sure every answer is clearly stated in the audio.

---

### D169 · Note-taking while listening
**Idea —** Note-taking is a sub-skill, not a natural reflex. Teach learners to write key words, not full sentences, and to keep listening even while writing.
**Example —** A learner who writes 'The meeting is on Thursday at three o'clock in the afternoon' in full misses the next two sentences.
**Try —** Play 60 seconds of a talk. Ask learners to take notes using key words only. Then compare notes in pairs.

---

### D170 · Predicting content before you play the recording
**Idea —** Prediction activates top-down processing and reduces cognitive load. Give learners the topic and task before playing anything. They arrive at the text with expectations already working.
**Example —** Before a job-interview listening: 'What questions do interviewers usually ask? Tell a partner.' Then play the recording.
**Try —** Add 60 seconds of prediction to your next listening lesson before pressing play. Notice whether comprehension improves.

---

### D171 · Bottom-up listening problems — fast speech and weak forms
**Idea —** Learners trained on clear, slow audio struggle with natural speech: words run together, vowels weaken, sounds drop. These are features of spoken English, not mistakes.
**Example —** 'What are you going to do?' in natural speech sounds like 'Wotchagonado?' Learners who haven't met this hear noise.
**Try —** Say 'I want to go to the cinema' at natural speed. Can your learners transcribe it? Play it three times and see.

---

### D172 · Connected speech — helping learners hear the joins
**Idea —** Connected speech features — linking, elision, assimilation, weak forms — make natural English hard to decode. Explicit teaching of these features improves listening measurably.
**Example —** Linking: 'pick it up' → /pɪkɪtʌp/. Elision: 'next day' → 'nex day'. Teach the pattern, not just the example.
**Try —** Take one sentence from a listening you use. Mark where sounds link or drop at natural speed. Teach that one pattern explicitly.

---

### D173 · Using transcripts — when and how
**Idea —** A transcript is a support tool, not a first resort. Use it after two listens. Let learners read while the audio plays, not instead of it. The comparison reveals what they missed.
**Example —** After two listens, learners follow the transcript while the audio plays. They hear the gaps. Then teach the connected speech that caused them.
**Try —** Use a transcript in your next listening lesson — but only after two full listens without it. Note what learners discover.

---

### D174 · Authentic vs graded listening texts
**Idea —** Authentic texts use real-world speech: overlaps, hesitations, accent variation, natural speed. Graded texts simplify for level. Both have a place; neither should dominate a course.
**Example —** A B1 class with a slow, clear dialogue: graded. The same class with a podcast excerpt at natural speed: authentic.
**Try —** Take an authentic clip you like. What would you pre-teach to make it accessible at your learners' level?

---

### D175 · Authentic vs graded reading texts
**Idea —** Authentic texts are written for native speakers: idiomatic, complex, dense. Graded texts trade authenticity for accessibility. Use both; do not let graded readers become the only diet.
**Example —** A graded reader at B1 simplifies Dickens. The original is authentic but likely inaccessible. The graded version builds reading habits and confidence.
**Try —** Find one authentic text at your learners' level — a menu, a sign, a social media post. What makes it accessible?

---

### D176 · The jigsaw reading task
**Idea —** In a jigsaw task, different learners read different texts then share information. Everyone has something the others need. It creates a genuine communication purpose for speaking after reading.
**Example —** Group A reads about climate causes. Group B reads effects. Group C reads solutions. Then they share to complete a joint task.
**Try —** Take a long text you use. Split it into three sections. Assign one section per group. Can they reconstruct the whole from each other?

---

### D177 · Staging a reading lesson — the full sequence
**Idea —** Reading lesson order: lead-in → set context → pre-teach two key words → gist task → check → detail task → check → language focus or productive follow-up.
**Example —** Skip the lead-in and learners arrive cold. Skip the gist task and learners go straight to detail, missing the overall meaning first.
**Try —** Map your next reading lesson against this sequence. Which stage do you most often cut? What do learners lose without it?

---

### D178 · Staging a listening lesson — the full sequence
**Idea —** Listening lesson order: lead-in → context → pre-teach one or two words → gist listen → check → detail listen → check → transcript if needed → follow-up.
**Example —** Teachers who skip gist and go straight to detail send learners in without a frame. They search for specifics in a fog.
**Try —** Plan one listening lesson from lead-in to follow-up. Write the exact task for each stage, not just the label.

---

### D179 · Post-reading and listening tasks — into production
**Idea —** After comprehension, learners should do something with the content. A productive follow-up — discussion, writing, roleplay — consolidates language and creates a genuine communication purpose.
**Example —** After a job-interview listening, learners roleplay their own interview using vocabulary from the audio. Comprehension becomes the springboard.
**Try —** Take a reading task you use. If there is no productive follow-up, design one that draws on language from the text.

---

### D180 · Six common receptive-skills lesson mistakes
**Idea —** Common errors: skipping lead-in; pre-teaching too many words; asking for detail before gist; replaying audio immediately when learners struggle; using transcripts as a shortcut; no productive follow-up.
**Example —** Playing audio three times straight without a task trains dependence on repetition, not listening strategy. Learners stop trying after one listen.
**Try —** Review your last reading or listening lesson. How many of these six errors appeared? Pick one to address next time.
