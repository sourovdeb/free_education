# Page 1 — Mechanism

## What is it?

Competitive victimhood is comparative.

One group feels harmed.
Another group feels harmed.

Then comparison begins.

“Our suffering was greater.”
“Our losses mattered more.”
“Our side was targeted.”
“Their suffering gets attention.”

The harms may exist.
The comparison adds something.

Recognition becomes scarce.
Moral standing feels scarce.
Sympathy feels scarce.
Power may feel scarce.

Researchers call this competition.

It differs from victimhood.
Victimhood can be factual.
Competitive victimhood is relational.

It asks “who suffered more?”

## Core mechanism

Conflict creates injury.

Injury creates grievance.
Grievance enters group identity.

Then identity gets threatened.

An accusation may trigger this.
Another group gains recognition.
One’s group feels displaced.

Defensive comparison can follow.

The group recalls losses.
It highlights its victims.
It discounts rival suffering.

That can reduce guilt.
It can restore morality.
It can restore agency.

It can also harden conflict.

## Experimental evidence

Sullivan tested moral threat.

Participants faced group accusations.

Men faced sexism claims.
Women faced reverse claims.
Students faced staff claims.

Accusations increased competition.

Group suffering became salient.

Later experiments tested function.

Competitive claims reduced guilt.
They reduced stigma threat.

That supports defense mechanisms.

The finding matters.

Victim claims can soothe.
They can protect identity.

That does not invalidate harm.
It explains one function.

## Conflict evidence

Noor studied Northern Ireland.

The sample reached 318.
Catholics and Protestants participated.

Competitive victimhood predicted forgiveness.
The relationship was negative.

Identity strength mattered.
Outgroup trust mattered.

Earlier Chile studies converged.

Competitive victimhood predicted less forgiveness.

The 2012 review followed.

It synthesized conflict research.

The authors identified functions.
Power restoration was one.
Moral restoration was another.

## Replication evidence

A 2025 study revisited.

Four conflicts were tested.
Total N reached 860.

Direct conflicts appeared stronger.
Structural conflicts appeared weaker.

Competitive victimhood predicted forgiveness.
Again, the link was negative.

Identity pathways were refined.

This supports generalization.
It does not prove universality.

Another 2025 study mattered.

Two groups were followed.
Context changed over time.

Victimhood levels also changed.

The relative pattern flipped.

This challenges fixed narratives.

Power alone cannot explain.
Threat and context matter.

## Why this unsettles

People value compassion.

We expect suffering shared.
We expect sympathy expands.

Yet recognition can compete.

Another victim can threaten.
Another grievance can displace.
Another history can offend.

This feels morally ugly.

But denial worsens it.

The mechanism stays human.
It is not partisan.
It is not biological.
It is not destiny.

## The disturbing truth

**The disturbing truth:** Suffering can become status.

## What this excludes

**What this does NOT prove:** Victim claims are false.

Real harms remain real.
Historical crimes remain real.
Power differences still matter.
Evidence still matters most.

The mechanism concerns comparison.
It concerns moral competition.
