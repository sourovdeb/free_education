# Human Nature Dossier

## Schadenfreude and justice

**Date:** 2026-09-23 10:25 +04:00  
**Series:** Human Nature, Law, Science  
**Folder:** `2026-09-23-1025-schadenfreude-justice`

> Central question:
> Does punishment reward observers?

Schadenfreude means felt pleasure.
Another person suffers misfortune.
The pleasure feels improper.
So people often hide it.

Justice complicates this emotion.
Punishment can feel deserved.
Deservingness changes emotional responses.
Punishment can also reward.
Those processes can overlap.

**The disturbing truth:** Punishment can feel rewarding.

**What this does NOT prove:** Justice equals cruelty.

This dossier separates them.
Emotion is not verdict.
Feeling is not action.
Brain activity proves little.
Institutions can constrain impulses.

# Page 1 — The mechanism

## 1. Psychology lens

### What is happening?

Several routes exist.
They can operate together.

**Route A: deservingness.**  
Someone breaks a rule.
Observers judge them blameworthy.
Misfortune then feels fitting.
Pleasure can follow punishment.

Van Dijk studied deservingness.
Responsibility increased schadenfreude.
Perceived deservingness mediated this.
The study used scenarios.
That limits realism.

**Route B: social comparison.**  
Someone outranks us.
Their advantage feels threatening.
Their fall reduces comparison.
Relief can become pleasure.

Takahashi tested this route.
Participants imagined comparison targets.
Envy tracked self-relevance.
Misfortune followed those targets.
Striatal responses then increased.
Self-reported schadenfreude also increased.

This was neuroimaging work.
Samples were not large.
fMRI needs cautious interpretation.
Reward regions are multifunctional.
No brain region proves motives.

**Route C: group rivalry.**  
Groups create social competition.
Rivals become comparison targets.
Their losses can please.

Cikara and Fiske tested this.
Participants viewed social targets.
Some targets seemed competitive.
Some targets seemed high-status.
Misfortunes changed emotional responses.
Facial activity also shifted.
Cooperation reduced schadenfreude.
Status changes also mattered.

That result matters.
The response was conditional.
It was not fixed.
Context changed the emotion.

**Route D: justice alignment.**  
A person violates trust.
Another person punishes them.
Observers may feel pleasure.
They may support punishers.

Yam and colleagues tested this.
They ran four experiments.
Trust games created violations.
Punishment followed those violations.
Deservingness predicted schadenfreude.
Schadenfreude predicted alignment.

Experiment Four was preregistered.
It used larger samples.
The pattern appeared again.
That strengthens the claim.
It still uses experiments.
Real courts differ greatly.

### The basic loop

1. A norm breaks.
2. Blame gets assigned.
3. Deservingness gets inferred.
4. Punishment becomes expected.
5. Misfortune then occurs.
6. Pleasure may appear.
7. Pleasure feels justified.
8. Judgment may harden.

This creates confusion.
Justice and reward merge.
The observer feels righteous.
The observer also benefits.
That benefit stays hidden.

### Why uncomfortable?

Our self-image differs.
We prefer cleaner motives.
We imagine justice dispassionately.
Evidence suggests emotional involvement.

Punishment can restore order.
It can protect others.
It can deter conduct.
It can express condemnation.

It can also gratify.
Those functions can coexist.
That coexistence feels taboo.

### Counterargument

Schadenfreude can support norms.
That need not corrupt justice.
Some pleasure tracks fairness.
Some punishment protects cooperation.

Yam supports this possibility.
Their participants favored punishers.
Especially after trust violations.
This can stabilize norms.

But another problem follows.
Deservingness is itself judged.
Those judgments can drift.
Group identity can distort them.
Envy can distort them.
Dislike can distort them.

So emotion has functions.
It also creates risk.

# Page 2 — Law, investigation, history

## 2. Criminal-investigation lens

Investigators need working theories.
Suspects also attract judgments.
Those judgments can harden.

NIJ studied wrongful convictions.
Its research identified tunnel vision.
Early errors became self-reinforcing.
Systems sometimes failed correction.

Schadenfreude research adds caution.
It does not prove causation.
No cited study shows this:

> Schadenfreude causes wrongful convictions.

That claim goes unsupported.
The implication is narrower.

A suspect seems blameworthy.
Punishment then feels deserved.
Confirming evidence feels coherent.
Disconfirming evidence feels disruptive.

This combination needs safeguards.
Independent review can help.
Blind procedures can help.
Recorded interviews can help.
Evidence separation can help.

### Investigation implication

Do not reward certainty.
Reward disconfirmation attempts.
Document rival hypotheses.
Separate guilt from dislike.
Separate evidence from punishment.

The emotional sequence matters.
Punishment pleasure comes later.
Investigative neutrality comes earlier.
The stages should separate.

## 3. Legal conflict

Law demands constrained punishment.
Evidence sets factual boundaries.
Culpability sets moral boundaries.
Sentencing adds legal boundaries.

Human emotion ignores compartments.
Harm can intensify outrage.
Outrage can intensify punishment.
Deservingness can feel self-evident.

Darley reviewed punishment research.
Moral outrage often arrives quickly.
Reasoning can follow afterward.

Salerno reviewed legal emotion.
Experienced emotion affects factfinding.
Expressed emotion can matter.
Effects vary by context.

### One concrete conflict

`Payne v. Tennessee` matters.
The Court addressed victim-impact evidence.
The majority allowed consideration.
The dissent stressed arbitrariness risks.

That dispute stays relevant.
Emotion can convey harm.
Emotion can also skew.
Both concerns are legal.

Schadenfreude adds another layer.
Punishment can reward observers.
That reward remains legally invisible.

Law judges external evidence.
It rarely measures pleasure.
That is probably necessary.
Thoughts are not crimes.
Emotions are not verdicts.

But institutions need structure.
Structure limits emotional leakage.

## 4. Political-history lens

One example needs caution.
Emotion cannot be reconstructed.
Historical behavior can be documented.
Inner pleasure usually cannot.

The Holocaust Encyclopedia documents humiliation.
Nazi authorities staged punishments.
Public humiliation became political theater.
Ordinary citizens sometimes participated.
Police also participated.
Military personnel also participated.

These events taught ideology.
They displayed state power.
They degraded targeted people.

This does **not** prove schadenfreude.
No archive reads minds.
The historical relevance differs.

Punishment became public spectacle.
Spectacle invites audience emotion.
Group hierarchy framed deservingness.
Institutional power reinforced framing.

That structure resembles experiments.
The moral stakes differ.
The evidence types differ.

Documented fact remains separate.
Psychological interpretation remains tentative.

## 5. Nietzsche lens

Nietzsche studied punishment genealogically.
He rejected simple origins.
Punishment served many functions.

In *Genealogy of Morals*,
he links punishment with satisfaction.
He describes creditor power.
He describes sanctioned cruelty.

That is philosophical interpretation.
It is not neuroscience.
It is not experimental evidence.

His useful question remains:

> Who benefits emotionally?

Modern research partly echoes this.
Punishment can produce pleasure.
But Nietzsche proved nothing experimentally.

His claims exceed evidence.
Some historical claims remain disputed.
Use him as provocation.
Not as scientific authority.

## 6. Robert Greene lens

Greene writes strategically.
He studies envy explicitly.
His framework emphasizes hidden motives.
He warns about fragile egos.

That can illuminate schadenfreude.
A rival falls publicly.
Status pressure then falls.
Pleasure may get moralized.

But Greene is interpretive.
His examples are selective.
His books are not trials.
They are not meta-analyses.

Research supports narrower claims.
Envy can predict schadenfreude.
Deservingness can predict schadenfreude.
Competition can amplify schadenfreude.

Research does **not** support:

> Everyone secretly wants ruin.

That would overreach badly.

Does status regulation dominate?

Measurement is another problem.
Schadenfreude is socially undesirable.
People may underreport it.
Facial measures add information.
Brain measures add information.
Neither reveals pure motives.

Context also changes effects.
Sports rivalry differs legally.
Court punishment differs socially.
Political conflict differs institutionally.

Culture may matter.
Samples remain geographically limited.
Student samples remain common.
Vignettes simplify consequences.
Laboratory punishment stays artificial.

Replication evidence exists.

Still, broad generalization fails.
No universal rate exists.

# Practical safeguards

These are design principles.
They are not accusations.

- Separate investigation stages.
- Record initial hypotheses.
- Seek disconfirming evidence.
- Blind reviewers where possible.
- Separate factfinding roles.
- Explain sentencing reasons.
- Use proportionality checks.
- Review emotionally loaded evidence.
- Track alternative explanations.
- Avoid public humiliation.
