# Page 2 — Five Lenses

## 1. Psychology

Moral circles are flexible.

They can widen.

They can shrink.

Threat often narrows them.

Distance can narrow them.

Deservingness can narrow them.

Similarity can widen them.

Rights framing can widen them.

Moral exclusion stays conditional.

No study proves universality.

### Method limits

Many samples are narrow.

Many outcomes use vignettes.

Self-report remains common.

Definitions overlap nearby constructs.

Exact replication remains limited.

Conceptual replication is stronger.

The mechanism remains useful.

Its borders remain debated.

---

## 2. Criminal investigation

Homicide work needs prioritization.

That creates a risk.

Some victims seem familiar.

Others seem socially distant.

Some witnesses seem credible.

Others seem less worthy.

This remains an implication.

It is not established fact.

Police integrity studies matter.

NIJ surveyed thousands.

Reporting norms varied sharply.

Codes of silence appeared.

Group culture changed responses.

That does not prove exclusion.

It shows boundary effects.

NIJ source:
https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/measurement-police-integrity-final-report

A 2022 OJP study helps.

Academy socialization increased silence.

Misconduct seriousness also mattered.

Control systems mattered too.

OJP source:
https://www.ojp.gov/library/publications/breaking-code-silence-importance-control-systems-and-empathy-toward-outgroups

### Investigation implication

Do not rank humanity.

Rank evidence instead.

Use fixed triage rules.

Track neglected leads.

Record contrary evidence.

Review closed hypotheses.

Use independent review.

Use blinding where possible.

Forensic bias review:
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2019.01.016

## 3. Political history

Consider Executive Order 9066.

It came in 1942.

Security fears shaped policy.

Japanese Americans were removed.

Many were American citizens.

No individual charges existed.

Ordinary protections narrowed.

The National Archives documents this.

Nearly 70,000 were citizens.

The government later apologized.

Redress followed in 1988.

Archive source:
https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/executive-order-9066

### Documented facts

Exclusion orders were enforced.

Incarceration followed ancestry.

Civil liberties were lost.

### Interpretation

Moral exclusion fits conceptually.

It may explain normalization.

It cannot prove motives.

War fear also mattered.

Racism also mattered.

Institutions also mattered.

History shows outcomes.

Psychology suggests mechanisms.

Those levels stay separate.

## 4. Nietzsche

Nietzsche questioned moral universality.

He studied moral boundaries.

He studied herd judgment.

He studied power claims.

His lens asks questions.

Who defines deservingness?

Who defines moral membership?

Who benefits from exclusion?

This is philosophy.

It is not evidence.

Primary text:

## 5. Robert Greene

Greene emphasizes group interest.

He emphasizes social masks.

He emphasizes status conflict.

His lens predicts redrawing.

Rules may follow interest.

Insiders may gain exceptions.

Outsiders may face strictness.

Research partly supports selectivity.

Greene generalizes much further.

His claims lack controls.

They remain interpretation.

# Legal conflict

Law assumes equal protection.

Psychology predicts selective concern.

That creates tension.

Yick Wo matters here.

A neutral rule existed.

Enforcement became discriminatory.

The Court rejected that.

Court text:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/118/356

Selective prosecution adds difficulty.

Unequal enforcement is unlawful.

Proof standards remain high.

Intent often needs proof.

Comparison cases also matter.

Cornell overview:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/selective_prosecution

## The conflict

Science studies hidden gradients.

Law needs provable elements.

Bias can stay implicit.

Legal intent may stay absent.

Harm can still differ.

Science cannot decide liability.

Law cannot detect everything.

Procedures bridge that gap.
