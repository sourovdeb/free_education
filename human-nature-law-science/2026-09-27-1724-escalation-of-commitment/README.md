# Human Nature Dossier
## Escalation of Commitment

**Date:** 2026-09-27 17:24 +04:00  
**Central question:** What buried human trait creates conflict between scientific evidence, legal assumptions, moral judgment, and social self-image?

### One-line answer
Past investment can become identity.

### The disturbing truth
Investment can make error feel honorable.

### What this does NOT prove
Persistence is not irrational by default.

## Definition

Escalation of commitment starts simply. A person chooses a path. Time gets spent. Money gets spent. Reputation gets attached. Then bad news appears. The rational question is future value. The human question often changes. Stopping now feels wasteful. Stopping can also feel humiliating. So another investment follows. The extra investment creates more pressure. The cycle can repeat. This is related to sunk costs. It is not identical. Sunk costs are past losses. Escalation includes continued commitment. Research shows both patterns. A meta-analysis found clear sunk-cost effects across many studies. Another meta-analysis examined escalation drivers. Context changed effect sizes. Personal responsibility mattered. Project structure mattered. Social pressure mattered. The effect is not universal. Sometimes persistence is rational. New investment can rescue projects. The error appears when past investment drives current choice. Future costs then lose weight. Future evidence can lose weight too.

## Counterargument

Persistence can be rational. New evidence may support continuation. Some investments create learning. Some projects need patience. Past investment alone proves nothing.

## Psychology

Waste aversion matters. Self-justification matters too. Withdrawal can make losses feel final. Continuing preserves recovery hopes. Four experiments found more escalation when continuation looked like action. Development adds another clue. Young children often ignore sunk costs. Adults expect sunk costs to shape choices. Researchers still debate boundaries. Some tasks show weak effects. Time investments differ. Completion effects can look similar. Experts are not always protected. The safest claim stays narrow. Past investment can influence present choice.

## Criminal investigation

Investigations create commitment quickly. A suspect gets identified. Interviews begin. Teams spend hours. A theory forms. Contrary evidence may appear. NIJ research describes tunnel vision. It can stop systems self-correcting. This does not prove sunk-cost bias. Confirmation bias may dominate. Case pressure may matter. Escalation adds one question. How much was invested? Kyles v. Whitley matters. Favorable evidence must be disclosed. Prosecutors must account for police-held evidence. The law demands correction. Psychology predicts correction costs.

## Political history

Concorde became a metaphor. Britain and France began development in 1962. Cost estimates rose sharply. UK Parliament records show this. Estimates reached £970 million by 1972. Concorde entered service in 1976. Treaty obligations also mattered. Jobs mattered too. Prestige mattered too. Technical hopes remained. These facts block simple claims. Continuation was not only sunk costs. The case still illustrates rising reversal costs.

## Nietzsche lens

Nietzsche is philosophical here. He questions fixed conviction. He does not test sunk costs. His lens asks one question. When does admitting error threaten identity? Science answers separately.

## Robert Greene lens

Greene emphasizes irrationality and defensiveness. This can describe face-saving. It cannot replace evidence. Research identifies several mechanisms. Ego is one candidate.

## Replication limits

The effect is supported. Its size varies. A 2015 meta-analysis reviewed 98 effects. Decision type changed results. Field studies can fail. Time costs are less stable. Completion effects can look similar. Direct justice evidence stays limited.

## Legal conflict

Law requires reassessment. Psychology predicts resistance. Kyles v. Whitley matters. Favorable evidence requires disclosure. Case commitment cannot excuse suppression.

## Investigation implication

Track contrary evidence. Separate review teams. Predefine stopping rules. Reopen suspect models. Ignore unrecoverable effort.
