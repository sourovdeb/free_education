# Pages 1–2

## The mechanism

Defensive attribution shifts blame.  
Threat changes causal judgment.  
Bad outcomes invite blame.  
Similarity can change blame.  
Difference can change blame.  
Perceived vulnerability also matters.  
Blame can restore control.  
That control can mislead.

The core pattern follows:

1. Something harmful happens.
2. Observers feel vulnerability.
3. They seek causal order.
4. They compare themselves.
5. They allocate responsibility.
6. Blame can reduce fear.

This mechanism feels rational.  
Sometimes it is rational.  
Evidence can justify blame.  
But threat can intrude.

### What studies found

Walster varied accident severity.  
Behavior stayed otherwise matched.  
Severe outcomes drew blame.  
The effect concerned judgment.  
Carelessness ratings changed less.

Shaver added personal relevance.  
He tested defensive attribution.  
Similarity changed some judgments.  
Severity also mattered.

Later tests were mixed.  
Fincham found no similarity effect.  
Severity still changed blame.  
That matters for replication.

Gilbert reviewed crisis studies.  
Severity showed small effects.  
The pattern was significant.  
Context changed effect sizes.

Crash research found asymmetries.  
Victims favored external causes.  
Others favored personal causes.  
Workplace studies found parallels.

Rape-blame research adds nuance.  
Similarity sometimes reduces blame.  
Sometimes it changes vulnerability.  
Gender can alter pathways.  
Context remains decisive.

### What scientists dispute

Evidence remains mixed.  
Similarity effects replicate unevenly.  
Severity effects look steadier.  
Motivation remains disputed.

One account is defensive.  
Fear drives blame shifts.  
Another account is cognitive.  
People infer from outcomes.

Brewer challenged motivational accounts.  
She proposed information processing.  
Outcome information alters probabilities.  
No self-protection is required.

Both routes may operate.  
Studies rarely separate them.  
Most use short vignettes.  
Samples can lack diversity.  
Real cases add complexity.

### Why this unsettles

People value fair judgment.  
People also fear randomness.  
Random harm feels intolerable.  
Blame creates perceived order.

That creates tension.  
We want impartiality.  
We also want safety.

Similarity adds discomfort.  
A victim resembles us.  
That can reduce blame.  
Or increase defensive distance.

The result feels moral.  
It may be protective.

### Counterargument

Blame can track evidence.  
Severe harm may matter.  
Law often values outcomes.  
Public anger can be justified.

Also, similarity effects weaken.  
Some replications fail them.  
Defensive motives remain inferred.  
No universal pattern exists.

So use caution.  
Do not psychologize disagreement.
