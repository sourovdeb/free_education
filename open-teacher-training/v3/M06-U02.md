# M06-U02 — Check a summary

Version 3.0.0. Complete sample; educator review pending. Original fictional teaching material. These are not real study findings.

Training estimate: 35 minutes. Classroom illustration: 25 minutes. Level: B1–B2, provisional; diagnose before use.

Teacher outcome: model source comparison without treating fluent summaries as evidence.

Learner outcome: reconcile two source accounts without removing their limitations.

## Source A — Learning-centre trial note, 6 May

[1] Eighteen volunteers attended four lunchtime reading sessions. The sessions combined short articles, discussion and a ten-minute writing task. Fourteen volunteers completed both the opening and closing quizzes. Their average scores rose from six to eight correct answers out of ten.

[2] The quizzes used different articles. Staff did not check whether those articles had the same difficulty. There was no comparison group. Six volunteers said discussion helped them. Others did not answer that question. The centre will repeat the trial before making a decision about expansion.

## Source B — Coordinator’s email, 8 May

[3] We are pleased that participants returned. The quiz results are encouraging, but they cannot show which activity caused the change. Some volunteers had attended English classes elsewhere during the same month. We have not tested whether improvements last.

[4] Next time, we will record attendance and ask about other learning. We also need a task using an unfamiliar article. The current results should not be advertised as proof that ten minutes of writing improves everyone’s reading.

## Worked decision

A claim about score changes is supported by A[1]. A claim about writing causing improvement is not supported. A[2] and B[3] provide alternative explanations.

## Training sequence

5 minutes: diagnose. 7: model. 10: attempt. 8: compare and revise. 5: independent check and return planning. Total: 35 training minutes.

## Tasks

### T1 — Identify the sample

How many attended? How many completed both quizzes? Why does the difference matter?

### T2 — Audit the summary

Underline unsupported claims:

> Eighteen learners proved that ten minutes of writing causes a lasting reading improvement. Everyone preferred discussion, so the centre will expand immediately.

### T3 — Rewrite with attribution

Write 70–100 words. State the result, sample and two limits. Name the source of one caution.

### T4 — Explain an uncertainty

Tell a partner which finding would change your confidence. Write one follow-up question.

## Answers

T1: Eighteen attended; fourteen completed both quizzes. The score change describes completers, not necessarily every attendee.

T2: Eighteen completers is wrong. Only fourteen completed both. Proved, causes, lasting, everyone and expand immediately are unsupported or contradicted. A[2] and B[3–4] explain why.

T3: Include fourteen completers, the six-to-eight result, and at least two limitations: unmatched difficulty, no comparison, other learning or no follow-up. Attribute the coordinator’s caution. Do not imply either causal success or causal failure.

T4: Accept a matched comparison, comparable quiz difficulty or delayed unfamiliar-text task. Satisfaction alone cannot establish causation.

One possible response:

> The centre reports that fourteen volunteers completed both quizzes, with average scores rising from six to eight out of ten. Eighteen people attended the sessions overall. The result does not establish what caused the change: article difficulty was not matched and there was no comparison group. The coordinator also notes that some people attended other English classes. No follow-up test established whether the change lasted. The centre plans another trial before deciding whether to expand.

## Feedback and access

Ask which source supports each clause. Keep a useful result without overclaiming. Do not replace unjustified optimism with unjustified pessimism.

Read sources separately, then together. Explain average, comparison group and lasting. Allow first-language planning. Reduce output length before deleting limitations.

## Return without AI

Use after 2–7 days, retaining access accommodations.

A library tried a reminder message for a writing group. Twenty members received it. Twelve came that week, compared with eight the previous week. It rained heavily during the earlier meeting. The library did not ask whether members read the message. The organiser wrote: “Attendance rose, but we cannot identify the cause from these two meetings.”

Correct this claim: “The reminder made four extra people attend.” Write 50–70 words preserving the observed change and two limitations.

Teacher key: attendance changed from eight to twelve. This does not establish four specific additional people or a reminder effect. Weather differed; receipt/reading was unverified; no comparison group or repeated matched observations.

## Criteria

Accuracy: preserves sample and result. Attribution: identifies the caution’s source. Qualification: separates association and cause. Independence: explains the rewrite without AI.

The proposed pilot rubric uses 0, 1 or 2 for not demonstrated, partial or demonstrated in this task. It is not a validated level score.
