### D182 · What Are Productive Skills?
**Idea —** Speaking and writing are productive skills — learners generate language, not receive it. Both require simultaneous control of form, meaning, and discourse.
**Example —** Answering a question aloud demands grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, and real-time meaning-making all at once. Writing adds the demands of accuracy and organisation.
**Try —** Think of one speaking task and one writing task you use. For each, list three things learners must control simultaneously.

---

### D183 · Fluency vs. Accuracy
**Idea —** Fluency means communicating smoothly without excessive pausing or breakdown. Accuracy means using correct forms. Neither is superior — different tasks prioritise one.
**Example —** A role-play values fluency: finishing the task matters. A grammar drill values accuracy: the form is the point. Mixing criteria in feedback confuses learners.
**Try —** Observe a learner speaking for 60 seconds. Record only errors for 30 seconds, then only hesitations. Discuss what each list reveals.

---

### D184 · Controlled Speaking Practice
**Idea —** Controlled practice keeps language use tightly constrained — learners produce target forms with minimal creative choice. It builds form confidence before fluency work.
**Example —** Substitution drills, prompt-and-response, and dialogue repetition with one variable changed are all controlled. Learners can fail or succeed on the target item only.
**Try —** Design a two-minute controlled speaking activity for 'used to + verb.' What is the single decision learners make each turn?

---

### D185 · Freer Speaking Tasks
**Idea —** Freer tasks give learners a communicative goal with minimal language constraint. Success means achieving the goal, not producing a specific target form.
**Example —** 'Plan a weekend trip with your partner. Budget: £50.' The task is real; the language is the learner's choice.
**Try —** Write a freer speaking task for your current class. State the goal, the constraint, and the time limit. What makes it communicative, not just conversation?

---

### D186 · Designing a Discussion Task
**Idea —** A good discussion task has a genuine information or opinion gap — learners must talk because they don't all hold the same answer.
**Example —** 'Is social media good or bad?' has no gap. 'You read different articles — what does yours argue?' creates a real reason to speak.
**Try —** Take a discussion topic you use. Add a real gap: give half the class one viewpoint, the other half the opposing one.

---

### D187 · Role-Play in Speaking
**Idea —** Role-play gives learners a persona and a situation, separating their language performance from their social identity. It reduces inhibition and produces target register.
**Example —** 'You are returning a faulty phone. Your partner works in the store.' The task has purpose, stakes, and a clear register.
**Try —** Design a role-play for a B1 class. Write both cards. What information does each learner have that the other needs?

---

### D188 · Information-Gap Activities
**Idea —** In an information-gap activity, two learners hold different information and must exchange it to complete the task. Neither can finish without the other.
**Example —** Student A has a timetable with gaps; Student B has the same timetable with different gaps. They must speak — they cannot show each other.
**Try —** Create a simple information-gap using two versions of a weekly schedule. What language do learners need to complete the exchange?

---

### D189 · Feedback on Speaking
**Idea —** Feedback on speaking is either immediate (on-the-spot correction) or delayed (after the task). In freer tasks, delay is usually preferable — it protects fluency.
**Example —** During a role-play, collect errors without interrupting. After, write them on the board anonymously and ask the class to correct them.
**Try —** During a speaking task, note five errors. Put them on the board at the end. Which do students spot? Which do they self-correct?

---

### D190 · Monitoring Speaking Tasks
**Idea —** While learners speak, the teacher monitors: listening for errors, noting effective language, ensuring the task is working. Monitoring is not hovering.
**Example —** Good monitoring means moving quietly, listening briefly, moving on — not standing over one pair for three minutes.
**Try —** During your next speaking task, monitor for two things only: are they on task? and what is the commonest error? Note both.

---

### D191 · Pronunciation in Productive Work
**Idea —** Pronunciation matters for intelligibility — can the listener understand? Not native-likeness, but enough accuracy to prevent communication breakdown.
**Example —** A mispronounced vowel ('ship'/'sheep') may cause confusion; a non-native accent rarely does. Teach what blocks communication.
**Try —** Choose three pronunciation points relevant to your learners' L1. For each, identify one minimal pair. Drill chorally, then individually.

---

### D192 · Dealing with Silence
**Idea —** Silence in a speaking task is not always a problem. It may signal thinking or genuine engagement. Diagnose before intervening.
**Example —** A three-second pause before a complex question is processing time, not failure. Jumping in too fast removes that time — and the learning.
**Try —** Set a speaking task. Commit to waiting five seconds before intervening when a learner goes quiet. Note how often they resume without help.

---

### D193 · Eliciting Student Talk
**Idea —** Eliciting means drawing language from learners rather than presenting it. The teacher asks, waits, and lets learners arrive at the answer.
**Example —** 'This word is the opposite of generous. What do you think it might be?' beats telling. The cognitive effort of retrieval aids retention.
**Try —** Choose five words you would normally present directly. Write an eliciting question for each — one that leads learners to the answer via context or contrast.

---

### D194 · Speaking and Writing Together
**Idea —** Speaking and writing prepare each other: talk generates ideas; writing organises them. A three-minute discussion before a writing task fills the page.
**Example —** Ask learners to discuss a topic for three minutes, then write a paragraph from the discussion. The talk provides content; the writing adds precision.
**Try —** Use a three-minute pair discussion as a pre-writing stage next lesson. Compare output quality with a version where you skip the speaking.

---

### D195 · Writing Sub-Skills
**Idea —** Writing involves distinct sub-skills: generating ideas, organising, drafting, revising for content, editing for language. Good tasks target specific sub-skills.
**Example —** Brainstorming targets idea generation. Re-ordering targets organisation. Reformulation targets editing. Each does different cognitive work.
**Try —** Take a writing task you use. Identify which sub-skill it primarily trains. Which sub-skill does it neglect entirely?

---

### D196 · Process Writing
**Idea —** Process writing treats writing as cyclical — draft, get feedback, revise, draft again. The focus is on improving the text through multiple iterations.
**Example —** First draft for meaning → peer feedback → revision for organisation → teacher feedback → final draft for accuracy. Each pass does different work.
**Try —** Run a short process writing cycle in one lesson. Draft 10 min → swap and respond to content only → revise 10 min. What changed?

---

### D197 · Product Writing
**Idea —** Product writing focuses on the final text — its genre conventions, organisation, and accuracy. Learners study a model, then produce one like it.
**Example —** Analyse a letter of complaint: opening, problem statement, evidence, demand, close. Then write one using the same structure.
**Try —** Choose a text type your learners need. Extract three structural features from a model. Ask learners to produce a text using those features.

---

### D198 · Genre-Based Writing
**Idea —** Genre-based writing teaches that every text type has social purpose and cultural conventions. Learning a genre means learning its purpose, audience, and form.
**Example —** A job application has a purpose (get an interview), an audience (hiring manager), and conventions (formal register, quantified achievements).
**Try —** With learners, analyse one genre they will need. List its purpose, audience, and three conventions. Then draft a short example.

---

### D199 · Writing for a Real Audience
**Idea —** Writing for a genuine reader produces better work. When someone outside the classroom reads the text, learners attend to clarity and register more carefully.
**Example —** A letter to a local newspaper, a review posted online, a recipe shared with another class — all create a real communicative purpose.
**Try —** Plan one writing task this term where learners write for a reader who is not the teacher. What changes in their preparation?

---

### D200 · Guided Writing
**Idea —** Guided writing provides scaffolding — sentence starters, paragraph frames, content prompts — reducing the language load so learners can focus on meaning.
**Example —** 'First, … . One reason for this is … . However, …' guides a B1 learner through a paragraph without dictating the content.
**Try —** Write a guided writing frame for a short opinion paragraph. Use three sentence starters. Remove them in the next task and compare the output.

---

### D201 · Free Writing Tasks
**Idea —** Free writing gives learners maximum autonomy — topic, form, length, and language are all their choice. It is fluency practice transferred to paper.
**Example —** 'Write anything you want to say. Ten minutes. I will not correct the language.' Purpose: break the fear of the blank page.
**Try —** Use five minutes of free writing to open a lesson this week. Ask learners: what did you find difficult? What did you enjoy?

---

### D202 · Feedback on Writing
**Idea —** Written feedback is most useful when it targets what the learner can improve next — not a comprehensive correction of everything wrong.
**Example —** 'Your ideas are strong. This lesson, focus on: check every present simple for third-person -s.' One precise focus beats ten ignored corrections.
**Try —** Mark a set of writing using two codes only: C (content worth keeping) and one language focus. Discuss the difference this makes to revision.

---

### D203 · Peer Feedback in Writing
**Idea —** Peer feedback trains learners to read critically, builds editing skills, and removes dependence on the teacher as the sole source of response.
**Example —** A checklist with three criteria — Is the purpose clear? Are ideas in logical order? Underline one thing you liked — prevents vague comments.
**Try —** Create a three-item peer-feedback checklist for a writing task next lesson. Run it, then ask: was the feedback specific enough to act on?

---

### D204 · Error Correction in Writing
**Idea —** Correction in writing can be direct (give the form), indirect (mark the error), or metalinguistic (name the error type). Indirect is often more effective.
**Example —** Underlining an error forces the learner to find and correct it — more cognitive work than receiving the answer. More work, more retention.
**Try —** Mark one set of writing using only underlining. Ask learners to self-correct. Note which errors they can fix and which they cannot.

---

### D205 · Planning a Writing Lesson
**Idea —** A writing lesson has three stages: pre-writing (generate ideas, activate genre knowledge), writing (draft), and post-writing (feedback, revise). Skip one and quality drops.
**Example —** Skipping pre-writing produces thin, poorly organised drafts. Skipping post-writing removes the feedback loop and the learning opportunity.
**Try —** Plan a 50-minute writing lesson for a genre your learners need. Allocate time to each stage. What gets the most time, and why?

---

### D206 · Writing Integrated with Reading
**Idea —** Reading models provide genre exemplars. Learners read to analyse, then write to replicate — analysis feeds production.
**Example —** Read a film review: identify structure, stance markers, recommendation. Write a review of something recently watched using the same structure.
**Try —** Choose a short text type (100–200 words). Use it as both a reading text and a writing model in the same lesson. What do learners notice?

---

### D207 · Paragraph Structure
**Idea —** A clear paragraph has a topic sentence, supporting detail or evidence, and a concluding link. Teaching this explicitly helps learners at B1 and above.
**Example —** 'Social media causes anxiety. A 2023 study found… . This suggests that…' — topic, evidence, implication: three moves, one paragraph.
**Try —** Give learners a jumbled paragraph — topic sentence, three supports, a conclusion. Ask them to reconstruct it in the correct order. Discuss the reasoning.

---

### D208 · Cohesion and Coherence
**Idea —** Cohesion is the grammatical and lexical links between sentences — pronouns, connectors, reference chains. Coherence is the logical progression of ideas. Both matter.
**Example —** 'She arrived late. Therefore, the meeting started without her.' Cohesion (Therefore) plus coherence (causal logic) — both are present.
**Try —** Find a learner text with poor cohesion. Add only connectors and pronouns. Does it now read as coherent? If not, what else is missing?

---

### D209 · Academic Writing at B2+
**Idea —** Academic writing requires formal register, hedging, evidence-based claims, and explicit structure. Teaching it begins with register awareness, not essay format.
**Example —** 'It seems likely that…' hedges. 'The data suggests…' is evidential. 'This essay will argue…' is structural. All three must be taught explicitly.
**Try —** Take a learner paragraph at B2. Ask them to add one hedge, one evidential phrase, and one linking sentence. Compare before and after.

---

### D210 · Creative Writing in ELT
**Idea —** Creative writing builds vocabulary range, sentence variety, and risk-taking. Low-stakes tasks — poems, micro-fiction, dialogue — produce language learners own.
**Example —** A six-word story forces lexical precision. A poem starter ('I remember…') unlocks personal language that coursebook tasks rarely reach.
**Try —** Give learners: 'Write a story in exactly six words.' Allow five minutes. Share. Discuss the vocabulary choices made.

---

### D211 · Speaking in Task-Based Learning
**Idea —** In task-based learning, speaking emerges from the task, not from a pre-taught structure. Learners use whatever language they can to achieve a real goal.
**Example —** 'Plan a community event with a budget of £200.' Nobody teaches the language first — the post-task focus teaches what they needed.
**Try —** Set a TBL speaking task next lesson. Monitor for language that emerged naturally. Use that language in the post-task focus stage.

---

### D212 · Month 7 Review
**Idea —** This month covered productive skills: fluency vs. accuracy, controlled and freer tasks, speaking feedback, the writing process, and integrating reading with writing.
**Example —** Process writing = cyclical revision. Product writing = study a model, then reproduce. Information-gap = two learners hold different data and must exchange it.
**Try —** Without looking back: define one of the three concepts above in your own words. Check your answer against the example.
