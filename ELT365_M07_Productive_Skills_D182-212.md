### D182 · What 'productive' means: speaking and writing
**Idea —** Productive skills are speaking and writing — skills where learners generate language rather than receive it. They require learners to retrieve, organise, and produce form and meaning simultaneously.  
**Example —** A learner answers an email in English: she chooses vocabulary, applies grammar, and structures her argument. All three at once — that is the productive challenge.  
**Try —** List one speaking task and one writing task you have used this month. For each, note what learners had to do simultaneously: grammar, vocabulary, organisation, audience awareness.

---

### D183 · Fluency vs accuracy: the tension at the heart of production
**Idea —** Fluency means communicating meaning without interruption; accuracy means using form correctly. Every speaking and writing task sits somewhere on the scale — and you design where.  
**Example —** A five-minute conversation task with no interruption for error correction: fluency-focused. A grammar exercise producing written sentences checked immediately: accuracy-focused.  
**Try —** Think of your last speaking task. Did you intervene on errors during the task? If yes, was the task fluency-focused? What would you do differently now?

### D184 · The information gap: why learners actually talk
**Idea —** An information gap means Learner A holds information Learner B needs, and vice versa. It creates a genuine communicative purpose — they must speak to complete the task.  
**Example —** Student A has the departure times; Student B has the platform numbers. Neither can answer the task alone. They must exchange information. That is an information gap.  
**Try —** Take a conversation task from a coursebook. Ask: is there a real gap? If both learners have the same information, redesign it so they do not.

### D185 · Controlled practice: keeping the focus tight
**Idea —** Controlled practice gives learners little freedom — they produce a specific form in a predictable context. It belongs right after presentation: build accuracy before you open the task.  
**Example —** After presenting reported speech, learners complete sentences: 'She said she ___.'' They choose the right verb form. Controlled — the target structure is the only variable.  
**Try —** Write one controlled practice sentence for a grammar point you are teaching this week. Check: is the target structure the only decision the learner makes?

---

### D186 · Free practice: opening the gate
**Idea —** Free practice sets a communicative task where learners choose their own language. The teacher monitors but does not intervene. The aim is fluency and consolidation.  
**Example —** After a lesson on giving advice, learners discuss a realistic dilemma in pairs: 'My neighbour plays music at 2 a.m. What should I do?' They may or may not use 'you should' — that is fine.  
**Try —** Design a two-minute free practice task for a language point you recently taught. Ensure the task works even if learners avoid the target structure.

---

### D187 · Monitoring during speaking tasks
**Idea —** During speaking tasks, the teacher circulates without intervening. Note language use — good and problematic — for delayed feedback. Sitting at the desk kills momentum.  
**Example —** During a five-minute roleplay, you move quietly from pair to pair. You note 'they said *he go* twice' and 'good use of hedging.' You collect material for the feedback stage.  
**Try —** In your next speaking task, carry a notepad. Write two or three examples of strong language and two or three errors. At the end, put them on the board without attribution.

---

### D188 · Error correction in fluency tasks: the delayed rule
**Idea —** During fluency tasks, correct errors after the task, not during. Immediate correction interrupts communication, raises anxiety, and trains learners to speak in short, safe sentences.  
**Example —** A learner is mid-story about her weekend when you stop her to fix 'I seen.' She loses her thread, the story dies, and the class learns: speak carefully, not freely.  
**Try —** Practice delayed feedback: write two student errors during a speaking task. After the task, write them on the board. Ask: 'Anything wrong here?' Let learners self-correct.

---

### D189 · Reformulation: the most natural correction
**Idea —** Reformulation means restating what a learner said, correctly, without flagging an error. It models the right form while keeping the conversation moving.  
**Example —** Learner: 'I goed to Paris.' Teacher: 'Oh, you went to Paris! When was that?' The correct form is in the response; the lesson moves on. Clean, low-anxiety, communicatively honest.  
**Try —** In your next class, try reformulation instead of explicit correction three times. Did the learner notice? Did they repeat the correct form in their next turn?

### D190 · Oral drilling: choral before individual
**Idea —** Drilling establishes pronunciation and form. Choral drilling — whole class together — lowers anxiety and lets learners rehearse without exposure. Individual drilling checks and consolidates.  
**Example —** Teacher: 'I haven't seen him.' (gesture) All together: 'I haven't seen him.' Then nominate: 'Maria.' Then: 'David.' Build confidence before putting individuals on the spot.  
**Try —** Drill one phrase from your next lesson: say it, gesture for choral, then nominate three individuals. Ensure each gets it before you move on. The whole sequence takes under 90 seconds.

### D191 · The principle: language emerges from the task
**Idea —** In task-based learning, learners do a communicative task first, then focus on language that arose from it. The task generates a real need; the language focus answers it.  
**Example —** Learners plan a weekend trip in pairs, then share their plan. Errors with future forms appear naturally. Now the grammar lesson is answering a question the learners already have.  
**Try —** Set a task before the grammar input in your next lesson. Monitor for the target form errors. After the task, present the language as a solution to what went wrong.

---

### D192 · Discussion tasks: build in a clear outcome
**Idea —** Discussion without a clear outcome trails off. Give learners a decision to reach, a list to rank, or a recommendation to make — something that requires the discussion to produce something.  
**Example —** Weak: 'Discuss the advantages of social media.' Strong: 'Agree on the single biggest risk of social media and be ready to defend it.' A decision forces collaboration.  
**Try —** Rewrite a discussion question from your coursebook. Add: 'Agree on...' or 'Rank these in order and be ready to explain your top choice.' Does the discussion feel more purposeful?

---

### D193 · Roleplay: building a context learners believe
**Idea —** Roleplay works when the scenario is specific and plausible. Vague roles produce vague language. Specific roles with real stakes generate genuine negotiation.  
**Example —** Compare: 'Roleplay a complaint' vs 'You ordered a birthday cake. It arrived late and the wrong flavour. The bakery is offering 10% off. You want a full refund.' The second has stakes.  
**Try —** Take a roleplay from a coursebook. Rewrite the scenario with three concrete details: the product, the problem, the gap between what each party wants. Compare the language generated.

### D194 · Pair work: setting up the information gap properly
**Idea —** For an information-gap pair task, each partner needs a different information card. The setup takes 90 seconds: distribute cards, check they do not show each other, demonstrate, set the outcome.  
**Example —** Partner A: train schedule for Monday. Partner B: train schedule for Tuesday. Task: find a day and time you are both free to travel. Neither can see the other's card.  
**Try —** Set up the next pair task with physical information cards if possible. Run the four-step setup: distribute, check cards are hidden, demonstrate, set the outcome.

---

### D195 · Reaching a decision: a task structure that works
**Idea —** Decision-making tasks — 'agree on X' — have a natural endpoint, require genuine exchange of views, and generate negotiation language without a script.  
**Example —** 'Agree on which three items you would take to a desert island, and explain your choices.' Learners must justify, listen, and compromise. The language of discussion appears because the task demands it.  
**Try —** Design a decision-making task for your class this week. Criteria: everyone must contribute; the answer is not obvious; defending your position requires real reasoning.

### D196 · Presentations in EFL: shorter is better
**Idea —** Long presentations overwhelm both speaker and audience. A two-to-three minute talk with one main point and one example is hard enough at B1 and generates better language than a ten-minute ramble.  
**Example —** Task: 'Prepare a two-minute talk about one thing you have changed your mind about. Include one reason and one example.' Focused, manageable, and personally meaningful.  
**Try —** Design a presentation task for B1+ learners. Cap it at two minutes. Require one main idea and one example. Give five minutes preparation. Does the constraint improve the output?

---

### D197 · Writing as a classroom activity: why it belongs here
**Idea —** Classroom writing is not homework. In-class writing allows the teacher to monitor process, give real-time feedback, and use errors as teaching material.  
**Example —** Learners write a paragraph in class while you circulate. You see three learners struggling with cohesion; you address it now. At home, you would only see the polished or the abandoned draft.  
**Try —** Set a five-minute in-class writing task next lesson. Circulate and note two patterns — one strength, one problem. Use both in feedback.

---

### D198 · Process writing vs product writing
**Idea —** Process writing focuses on how a text is built — planning, drafting, revising. It mirrors how real writers work and raises awareness of writing as a recursive skill.  
**Example —** Product: 'Write a formal email.' (submit and mark) Process: plan → draft → peer feedback → revise → submit. The second teaches writing; the first mainly tests it.  
**Try —** Take one writing task from your coursebook. Add a planning stage (5 min) and a peer-review stage (5 min) before learners write their final version. Compare the quality of the finished texts.

---

### D199 · The model text: what it is and how to use it
**Idea —** A model text shows learners what a good version of the target text looks like before they write their own. It sets the standard and provides language they can adapt — not copy.  
**Example —** Before asking learners to write a formal complaint email, you read one together, highlighting the opening, the problem statement, the request, and the close.  
**Try —** Choose a model text for a writing task you are planning. Read it with learners first. Ask: 'What does the writer do at the start? In the middle? At the end?' Extract the structure before they write.

---

### D200 · Analysing a model: form before function
**Idea —** When analysing a model text, look at the form first — what the text does in each section — before asking learners what they think of it. Jumping to opinion skips the language focus entirely.  
**Example —** For a formal letter model: first map the structure (greeting, purpose, detail, close). Then note key phrases. Opinion last — 'Would you write this way?'  
**Try —** With your next model text, write three form-focused questions before one opinion question: 'What does the writer use to open? How does the second paragraph begin? What is the final request?'

### D201 · Planning a piece of writing: why bother?
**Idea —** Planning forces learners to separate content decisions from language decisions. Without it, writers spend working memory on both at once and produce thin, underdeveloped paragraphs.  
**Example —** A learner given five minutes to plan a discursive essay lists her three points before writing. Her draft has a clearer argument and stronger development than one written without planning.  
**Try —** Set a three-minute planning task before your next writing activity: list main points, order them, pick one example for each. Compare the planning-first draft to one done without.

---

### D202 · Paragraph structure for B1+ learners
**Idea —** A functional paragraph has three parts: a topic sentence that states the main idea, supporting sentences that develop it with examples or reasons, and an optional closing sentence.  
**Example —** Topic: 'One advantage of remote work is flexibility.' Support: 'Employees can start earlier or later, which reduces commuting at rush hour.' The example earns the claim.  
**Try —** Give learners a mixed-up paragraph: topic sentence, two support sentences, one irrelevant sentence. Ask them to remove the irrelevant one and reorder the rest. Debrief: how did they decide?

---

### D203 · Cohesion: connectors are not enough
**Idea —** Learners often over-rely on connectors while ignoring other cohesive devices: pronoun reference, lexical chains, and ellipsis. Connectors alone make writing mechanical.  
**Example —** Weak: 'Firstly, dogs are loyal. Moreover, they are good for health. In conclusion, people should own dogs.' Strong: 'they,' 'these animals,' 'this relationship' build a coherent chain.  
**Try —** Take a student's written paragraph. Remove all connectors. Read what remains. Does it hold together? If not, identify what is missing — often it is reference or repetition of key lexis.

---

### D204 · Giving written feedback: less is more
**Idea —** Marking every error overwhelms learners and teaches them to feel bad, not to improve. Choose one or two patterns — ideally the ones that most affect meaning or reflect today's teaching aim.  
**Example —** A B1 essay has fifteen errors. You mark three: all missing articles in the first paragraph. 'Check articles — see three examples I have marked. Try to self-correct.'  
**Try —** Try focused feedback on one error type only in your next marking session. Write what you focused on at the top of the page. Note whether learners engage differently.

---

### D205 · Peer feedback: setting it up so it works
**Idea —** Peer feedback fails when learners just say 'it is good.' It works when they have a specific criterion: 'Does the first sentence tell you the main idea? Can you find one example?'  
**Example —** Learner A reads B's paragraph and answers: 'What is the main idea? Is there an example? Does the last sentence link forward?' Now feedback is a task, not a compliment.  
**Try —** Write three peer-feedback criteria for a writing task you will set this week. Make each a yes/no question with a follow-up. Pilot the peer-review stage.

---

### D206 · Editing vs proofreading: a distinction that matters
**Idea —** Editing is improving content and structure — does the paragraph make sense, is the argument clear? Proofreading is checking form — spelling, punctuation, grammar. Teach them as separate stages.  
**Example —** First pass (edit): 'Is the main idea clear? Is there an example?' Second pass (proofread): 'Check every verb ending. Check articles. Check spelling.'  
**Try —** After your next in-class writing task, ask learners to edit first then proofread. Does the sequence change how they read their own work?

---

### D207 · Writing to a real reader: the motivation fix
**Idea —** Writing for a teacher-examiner is motivationally thin. Writing for a real or imagined audience gives learners a reason to make the text work.  
**Example —** Instead of 'Write a review of a film,' try 'Write a review for the school newsletter. It will be read by students choosing what to watch next weekend.' The audience sharpens every choice.  
**Try —** Redesign a writing task by specifying: who the reader is, where the text will appear, and what the reader needs from it. Does learner investment change?

### D208 · The 4-3-2 technique for speaking fluency
**Idea —** In the 4-3-2 technique, learners tell the same story three times: four minutes, then three, then two. Repetition forces fluency — they stop translating and start retrieving. Nation developed this.  
**Example —** Round 1 (4 min): the learner stumbles and searches. Round 2 (3 min): smoother, fewer pauses. Round 3 (2 min): often the most fluent. Same content; building automaticity.  
**Try —** Try 4-3-2 with a topic learners know well. After round 3, ask: 'Was it easier the third time? Why?' The metacognitive debrief is part of the technique.

### D209 · The dictogloss: where listening meets writing
**Idea —** A dictogloss is a listening-writing task: the teacher reads a short text at natural speed twice; learners reconstruct it from notes. The reconstruction reveals what they know and what they are missing.  
**Example —** Text (read twice at natural speed): 'Migration has always shaped cities. London, Paris, and Sydney grew because people moved there looking for work.' Learners compare their reconstructions in pairs.  
**Try —** Write a six-sentence dictogloss text on a topic your class knows. Read it at natural speed twice. Learners reconstruct in pairs. Compare with the original. Focus feedback on the language gaps.

---

### D210 · Integrating skills: the natural approach
**Idea —** Real communication always integrates skills. Reading sparks speaking; listening generates writing; speaking leads to reading. Design tasks that mirror this rather than isolating skills artificially.  
**Example —** Read an article → discuss your reaction → write a response → share it with a partner who reads and gives feedback. All four skills, one topic, one hour.  
**Try —** Map your next lesson's main activity: which skill does it target? Now add one more skill stage before and one after. Does the lesson feel more cohesive?

---

### D211 · Staging a productive skills lesson end-to-end
**Idea —** A complete productive skills lesson has four stages: lead-in, model and analyse, prepare, produce, feedback. Rushing the prepare stage collapses the quality of production.  
**Example —** Lead-in (2 min) → read model and analyse structure (5 min) → prepare your own (5 min) → produce (8 min) → content feedback, then language feedback (5 min). Total: 25 minutes.  
**Try —** Map your next speaking or writing lesson to those four stages. Which stage do you typically cut? If it is 'prepare,' restore it — that is where language moves from passive to active.
