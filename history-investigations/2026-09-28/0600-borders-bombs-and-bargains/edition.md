## 1. Oil and fear converged

Oil production could restart. **Scholarly interpretation.** Oil and anticommunism intertwined. Iranian actors remained decisive. Clerics, officers, and monarchists acted. Crowds also acted. **Counterevidence and limits.** Tudeh influence was real. Yet immediate takeover seemed unlikely. Mosaddeq also weakened parliament. His referendum drew criticism. Domestic conflict preceded intervention. CIA support never acted alone. Declassified records prove involvement. They show planned pressure. They do not prove omnipotence. Later revolutionary anger had sources. The coup shaped that anger. It cannot explain everything.

## 6. Primitive Echo: punishment

**Question:** Why punish free-riders?

**Documented evidence.** Public-goods experiments test cooperation. People receive shared stakes. Some participants contribute less. Others can punish them. Punishment often costs punishers. Cross-cultural results show variation. Low contributors often face sanctions. High contributors sometimes face them. Elected monitors gain compliance. Random monitors gain less. Field experiments confirm context. **Evolutionary interpretation.** Sanctions can deter free-riding. They may protect shared goods. Reputation may reward enforcers. Cultural learning shapes norms. Institutions can centralize enforcement. **Current echo.** Online groups shame defectors. Workplaces police effort. Nations punish tax evasion. Communities also punish dissent. **Benefits.** Sanctions can stabilize cooperation. Legitimacy reduces needed coercion. **Harms.** Punishment can become antisocial. Envy can target contributors. Group power can silence minorities. **Counterevidence and limits.** Games simplify real societies. Samples differ across studies. Laboratory costs stay small. Culture explains much variation. Genes remain unproven here. Punishment is not automatically adaptive. It works within institutions. Without legitimacy, retaliation grows.
