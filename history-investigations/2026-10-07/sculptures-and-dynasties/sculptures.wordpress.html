Evidence

Read the marks

How did people shape stone? Anna Serotta examines tool marks. Her experiments compare tools. Pounding and abrasion shaped surfaces. Abrasives helped saws and drills. Different operations can leave similarities.[S01]

A surface can erase evidence. Polishing may remove earlier marks. Surviving marks require interpretation. Experiments help test proposed techniques. They cannot identify every workshop. Scale does not establish technology.[S01]

Interpretation

Who commissioned the Sphinx?

Mark Lehner favours Khafre. Quarry layers match temple blocks. Their sequence links the structures. The attribution rests on context. This differs from direct proof.[S04]

Khufu's involvement remains a hypothesis. He might have initiated planning. Construction evidence could change attribution. Rock layers also weather differently. Geological comparisons require matching materials. Erosion alone cannot establish date.[S04][S05]

Alternatives concern sequence and geology. AERA supplies both research accounts. These accounts share research dependencies. They cannot count as independence.[S04][S05]

Evidence → interpretation

An Amarna drillhole

Serotta examined an Amarna fragment. Scientist Federico Carò joined her. Their specimen retained corundum. Bronze corrosion also remained. They infer bronze-tube drilling. They infer corundum abrasion.[S02]

The specimen dates to Amarna. Its context: 1353–1336 BCE. The observation concerns one object. It cannot establish every workshop. The mineral source remains unresolved. Imports and local sources compete. Mineral comparisons could distinguish them.[S02]

Experiment → hypothesis

Could water assist transport?

Abdoulaye Fall and colleagues experimented. Their tests measured sliding friction. Water can reduce sand friction. Quantity and grain distribution matter. Overwatering reverses the benefit. Capillary bridges explain the mechanism.[S03]

This supports a hauling hypothesis. It does not reconstruct journeys. Routes and loads require evidence. Sledges, surfaces and practices vary. Only the abstract was inspected.[S03]

Evidence → interpretation

Kailasa: excavation creates architecture

At Ellora, carvers removed rock. Kailasa emerged through excavation. Jayaram Poduval describes downward cutting. He distinguishes five excavation phases. Regional traditions informed the architecture.[S06]

Rashtrakuta patronage explains its context. Attribution draws upon copper-plate evidence. No site inscription names patrons. Patron assignments involve interpretation. Planning and additions can coexist.[S06][S07]

Dating indicates the eighth century. Later additions complicate completion claims. Workforce and duration remain uncertain. Online tonnage figures need verification. Architectural comparisons help assess sequence.[S07]

Evidence

Chola bronzes: casting and worship

Sculptors modelled figures in wax. Clay enclosed each wax model. Heating removed the wax. Metal filled the resulting mould. Breaking it released the sculpture.[S08]

Processions brought images among worshippers. Rulers supported temples and images. Craft served worship and patronage. Workshop recipes could still vary. Casting cannot identify every maker.[S08]

Questions

What would change these accounts?

A hypothesis proposes an explanation. Evidence must distinguish competing accounts. Inscribed dates could clarify patronage. Residue analysis could identify materials. Tool experiments could test feasibility. Quarry records could constrain labour.[S01][S02][S04][S06]

Uncertainty does not establish alternatives. Each claim needs its evidence. Drawings illustrate mechanisms, not discoveries.
