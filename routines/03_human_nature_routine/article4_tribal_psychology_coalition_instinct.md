# The Coalition Instinct: Why Your Brain Treats Group Loyalty as Survival

## Hook

In the summer of 1954, psychologist Muzafer Sherif sent twenty-two eleven-year-old boys to a summer camp at Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma. None knew the camp was an experiment. The boys were split into two groups, kept apart for a week so each could bond internally, and only then introduced to one another as rivals in a tournament of games.

Within days, the groups — who named themselves the Eagles and the Rattlers — were raiding each other's cabins, burning the other side's flag, and refusing to eat in the same hall. Boys who had been ordinary, cooperative children a week earlier were now hurling insults at kids they had never met and would have been friends with under different circumstances. Sherif had not needed ideology, scarcity, or years of grievance to manufacture intergroup hatred. He needed a label, a competition, and six days (Sherif et al., 1961).

The finding that followed — replicated and refined for seventy years — is that the human mind does not require a reason to form a tribe. It requires only a boundary. Once the boundary exists, loyalty, suspicion, and moral double standards follow automatically, underneath conscious deliberation. This is the coalition instinct, and it is arguably the single most exploited feature of human psychology in the history of politics, advertising, and war.

## The Mechanism: Minimal Groups, Maximal Loyalty

Henri Tajfel's minimal group experiments in the early 1970s pushed Sherif's finding to its logical extreme. Tajfel assigned British schoolboys to groups based on a coin flip — literally arbitrary, and the boys knew it. Even so, when given the chance to allocate rewards, the boys consistently favored their own randomly assigned group over the other, sometimes even when doing so reduced their own group's absolute payoff, so long as it widened the gap over the outgroup (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). Tajfel called the resulting framework Social Identity Theory: people derive part of their self-concept from group membership, and will protect the group's relative status even at personal cost.

Evolutionary psychologists Peter DeScioli and Robert Kurzban later argued this is not a side effect of tribalism but its engine. Their coalitional psychology model holds that alliance-formation was a survival necessity in ancestral environments where physical safety depended on having allies who would fight on your behalf (DeScioli & Kurzban, 2013). A mind that hesitated to pick a side, or that applied identical moral scrutiny to ally and rival alike, was a mind that lost fights. The lesson plan objective evolution appears to have optimized for was never "be accurate" — it was "be loyal, fast."

Mark Van Vugt and Justin Park's "male warrior hypothesis" extends this further: intergroup threat reliably activates ingroup cohesion and outgroup hostility in males specifically, a pattern visible across warfare, sports fandom, and corporate rivalry (Van Vugt & Park, 2009). None of this requires a villain. It requires only two labeled groups and a perceived contest.

## Historical Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Rwandan Radio Campaign

Between 1993 and 1994, Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines (RTLM) in Rwanda ran a sustained campaign recoding the Tutsi minority as "cockroaches" and "snakes" — dehumanizing vocabulary that functioned as **blocking vocabulary**, foreclosing any receptive frame in which a listener could process a Tutsi neighbor as an individual rather than a category. Scholars of the genocide, notably Scott Straus (2007), have documented that RTLM alone cannot explain the genocide's scale, but it demonstrably lowered the threshold for participation by ordinary Hutu civilians in communities where the broadcasts were heard, converting an existing ethnic boundary into an active kill list. The coalition instinct did not need to be created from nothing; it needed only its ordinary ingroup-preference setting redirected toward extermination.

### Pattern 2: Wartime Propaganda and the "Loyalty Test" Structure

Historian and media scholar Jacques Ellul, in *Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes* (1965), observed that effective state propaganda rarely tries to persuade through argument. Instead it re-frames dissent itself as evidence of outgroup loyalty. Ellul's core claim was that propaganda works best on the educated, not the ignorant, because educated audiences are more skilled at generating self-justifying reasons for a conclusion their group identity has already supplied. Gustave Le Bon had anticipated part of this mechanism decades earlier in *The Crowd* (1895), describing how individual judgment dissolves inside a crowd identity, replaced by a shared, simplified, emotionally charged consensus.

The consistent structural feature across 20th-century propaganda campaigns — Allied and Axis alike — was the instructional checking question repurposed as a loyalty test: not "do you understand the policy" but "are you with us." A population trained to answer instructional checking questions with group affirmation rather than independent verification is a population whose productive knowledge — its capacity to generate independent judgment — has been substituted with receptive compliance to the coalition's script.

## Modern Echoes

Social media architecture reproduces the minimal-group paradigm at a speed Tajfel and Sherif never had access to. Christopher Bail's research on partisan "echo chambers" found that exposing partisans to opposing views on social media did not reduce polarization — it increased it, because the exposure was read as an intergroup threat rather than new information (Bail et al., 2018). The platform's engagement incentive and the coalition instinct point in the same direction: outrage at the outgroup drives more reliable engagement than nuanced agreement, so the algorithm and the ancestral alliance-detection circuitry reinforce one another without any coordinated design.

Jonathan Haidt and Jean Twenge's more recent work on adolescent social media use links the sharpest rise in political and identity-based tribalism among teenagers to the specific affordances of platforms built around public, quantified ingroup signaling — likes, shares, and follower counts that function as continuously updated coalition-loyalty scores (Haidt, 2024). The mechanism is Sherif's tournament, running permanently, for an audience of billions who never leave camp.

## Implications & Self-Reflection

The uncomfortable finding across seventy years of this research is not that propagandists are unusually skilled. It is that the raw material they work with — your own coalition instinct — requires almost no manipulation to activate. A label, a scoreboard, and a rival is sufficient. The Eagles and the Rattlers needed nobody's malice, only a name and a tournament.

This has a practical corollary: the feeling of moral clarity you experience toward an outgroup is not reliable evidence that the group deserves it. Tajfel's schoolboys felt clarity too, over a coin flip. The mechanism cannot distinguish a coalition formed around a genuine atrocity from one formed around a summer-camp cabin assignment; both are processed by the same circuitry, at the same speed, with the same felt certainty.

Practically, this suggests treating any freer practice of your own political or tribal judgment — the moments where you reason without a scripted conclusion already installed — as a discipline rather than a default. Ask, deliberately, whether your certainty tracks evidence or tracks group membership: would you still hold this view if the labels on the two groups were swapped? Notice when disagreement inside your own coalition gets punished faster than falsehood does — that asymmetry is the signature of the mechanism operating, not of the group being right.

Journal prompt: Recall a disagreement where you felt certain the other side was not just wrong but bad. Strip away which side was "yours." Does the argument still hold?
