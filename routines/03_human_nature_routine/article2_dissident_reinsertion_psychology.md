# The Shadow Institutions: Why We Abandon Our Best Thinkers and the Evolutionary Trap Behind It

## Hook

In 1962, James Watson and Francis Crick received the Nobel Prize for the structure of DNA. Rosalind Franklin, whose X-ray crystallography work — specifically Photo 51 — was the empirical cornerstone of their discovery, received nothing. She had died four years earlier, aged 37, of ovarian cancer. She never knew her data had been shared with Watson without her knowledge or consent.

Watson, years later, made racist statements about genetic intelligence differentials. He was stripped of his honorary titles, his name removed from buildings. The scientist who had been erased from her own discovery received posthumous tributes.

Neither outcome is heroic. Both are structurally predictable.

The question is not whether institutions are run by bad people. The question is: what evolutionary dynamic systematically *produces* the erasure of excellence, and what does it reveal about the coalition-management instincts we inherited from our ancestors on the savannah?

---

## The Hidden Driver: The Tall Poppy Reflex

Hunter-gatherer societies — our ancestral template for roughly 95% of human evolutionary history — were profoundly egalitarian, and not by accident. Christopher Boehm's 2012 work *Moral Origins* documents across hundreds of ethnographic studies that forager bands maintained equality through active suppression of dominance: ridicule, ostracism, and in extreme cases, execution of individuals who accumulated disproportionate resources or social power.

This is sometimes called **reverse dominance hierarchy** — the group collectively punishing those who try to rise above it. It is not weakness; it was survival-critical. A forager band that allowed one person to monopolise food, mates, or decision-making became fragile. The suppression of excellence — specifically, the suppression of *visible* excellence — was the social immune system.

The problem is that this mechanism was calibrated for bands of 30–150 people (Dunbar's number), living in zero-sum resource environments. It was never designed for institutions of thousands managing non-zero-sum knowledge production.

When that evolutionary hardware runs inside a modern university, a media organisation, or a government agency, it produces **tall poppy syndrome** at institutional scale: the systematic pressure to cut down the individual who grows too distinctive, too accurate, too credible. The target need not be wrong. In fact, the more correct they are, the more threatening they become to the status quo of the coalition.

This is what Jungian analyst James Hollis calls **institutional shadow** — the collective refusal of a group to acknowledge the parts of itself that threaten its self-image. The institution projects onto the deviant individual exactly the pathology it is most committed to not seeing in itself: narcissism, self-promotion, instability.

## Historical Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Socratic Execution and Its Institutional Logic

In 399 BCE, the city-state of Athens executed Socrates for impiety and corrupting youth. He was 70 years old. His accusers included prominent democratic politicians. His crime, stripped of rhetoric, was epistemological: he demonstrated, publicly and repeatedly, that the city's most respected figures did not know what they claimed to know.

The Athenian democracy — then in its post-Peloponnesian War trauma, having just emerged from oligarchic coup and counter-revolution — was extraordinarily sensitive to destabilising figures. Socrates was not killed because he was wrong. He was killed because his method of inquiry was *structurally incompatible with coalition maintenance*. He could not be bought, flattered, or absorbed.

The modern equivalent is not execution. It is defunding, deplatforming, and the coordinated erosion of institutional affiliations. The result — the removal of the individual from the knowledge-production infrastructure — is functionally identical.

What policymakers, from Pericles to modern communications directors, understood intuitively is this: **the credibility of the messenger is the weapon**. You do not need to refute the argument. You need to destroy the career. Once the career is destroyed, the argument loses its institutional amplification and dies of neglect.

### Pattern 2: The Soviet Lysenko Affair — When the State Selects for Compliance

From 1940 to 1964, Trofim Lysenko dominated Soviet biology. He rejected Mendelian genetics, insisted on the heritability of acquired characteristics, and had scientists who disagreed imprisoned, sent to gulags, or killed. Hundreds of Soviet biologists were destroyed. Soviet agricultural science fell decades behind the West.

Lysenko was not an ideological aberration. He was the *optimal product* of an incentive structure that selected for political loyalty over empirical accuracy. Stalin needed genetics to be wrong because Mendelian heredity implied fixed traits — which conflicted with the Marxist vision of the transformable human. The institution rewarded the scientist who provided the answer the power structure required.

The lesson is devastating in its simplicity: **institutions do not intrinsically select for truth**. They select for fitness to the institution. In healthy epistemic environments, these two things roughly align. Under conditions of ideological pressure, resource scarcity, or external threat, they diverge catastrophically.

The marketers and propagandists of the 20th century absorbed this lesson with precision. Edward Bernays — Freud's nephew — founded the discipline of public relations explicitly on the insight that mass behaviour could be engineered by targeting the institutional structures through which people form beliefs. His 1928 book *Propaganda* was used as a manual by both commercial advertisers and, later, by the Nazi propaganda ministry.

The CIA's influence campaigns, documented by journalist Tim Weiner in *Legacy of Ashes* (2007), operated on the same principle: you do not change the population's mind directly. You change the credibility infrastructure through which the population forms beliefs — the editors, the academic chairs, the foundation boards — and the population follows.

## Modern Echoes

Eric Weinstein's 2026 remarks identify something that social science has been reluctant to study directly: **the systematic underinvestment in reinstating dissident thinkers who have been vindicated**.

The economics are straightforward. A chaired professorship at a major US university costs $5–15 million to endow. This is within the reach of any serious philanthropist. And yet the vindicated dissidents — the scientists who were correct about lab leaks, about the statistical problems in nutritional epidemiology, about the replication crisis, about the flaws in climate model sensitivity estimates — are not being reinserted. They are being left in the parallel institutions: podcasts, Substack, independent conferences.

This is not accidental. The tall poppy reflex runs in reverse when the excluded thinker is outside the walls: it is now the institution that feels threatened by the possibility of reinsertion. To reinstate Jordan Peterson to a Cambridge chair would be an institutional admission that the process that removed him was wrong. Institutions, like individuals, have egos. Institutional ego defence is one of the most robust and undertheorised phenomena in organisational psychology.

The policy implication — which Weinstein raises but does not fully resolve — is that **parallel institutions are insufficient**. A podcast with five million listeners does not produce the next generation of researchers. A conference in an event venue does not train PhD students. The knowledge infrastructure that Western civilisation spent five centuries building is not replicable outside the citadel; it can only be recaptured inside it.

The mechanism of recapture is the endowed chair. The mechanism is embarrassingly mundane. It requires money and willingness.

## Implications & Self-Reflection

Here is the evolutionary psychology of your own institution — your workplace, your social group, your family:

Every coalition you belong to has a suppression mechanism. Every group has its list of things that cannot be said, people who cannot be praised, conclusions that cannot be reached. You have already internalised most of these constraints so thoroughly that you experience them not as constraints but as your own judgment.

The individuals who are excluded from your coalition — who were perhaps exiled, ridiculed, or quietly defunded — may include people who were simply too correct, too clearly able to expose the group's blind spots. The tall poppy reflex does not distinguish between the destabilising malcontent and the inconveniently accurate truth-teller. It just cuts.

Boehm's ethnographic data suggests the reflex can be overridden — forager bands had elders who explicitly moderated the suppression impulse, recognising that certain kinds of excellence were coalition-enhancing rather than coalition-threatening. The question for modern institutions is whether any equivalent mechanism can be built.

The first step is individual: recognising the reflex as an evolutionary artifact rather than a moral judgment. The person your institution is currently excluding: are they wrong, or are they simply inconvenient?
