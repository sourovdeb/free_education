# Does Depression Come From a "Chemical Imbalance"? What the Evidence Actually Shows

**Claim audited:** Depression is caused by low serotonin levels in the brain ("chemical imbalance"), the rationale long used to explain how SSRI antidepressants work.

For decades, patients were told depression stems from a "chemical imbalance" — too little serotonin in the brain. This framing, widely used in SSRI marketing and patient materials since the 1990s, became conventional wisdom.

In 2022, Joanna Moncrieff and colleagues (UCL) published the first systematic "umbrella review" pooling decades of research on serotonin and depression — body-fluid metabolites, receptor/transporter imaging, tryptophan-depletion trials, and gene-association studies (*Molecular Psychiatry*, 2022). Their conclusion: no consistent evidence links lowered serotonin to depression. The review had no dedicated funding; one co-author disclosed having since co-founded a company helping patients taper off antidepressants — a disclosed interest, but one pointing *away* from pharmaceutical revenue, not toward it.

A 31-author rebuttal, "A leaky umbrella has little value" (*Molecular Psychiatry*, 2023), argued Moncrieff mixed incompatible study types and misread tryptophan-depletion and 5-HT1A imaging data. Notably, many of its authors disclosed extensive pharmaceutical ties — honoraria from Janssen, Lundbeck and Sunovion, equity in psychedelic-drug companies, and one author employed part-time by Lundbeck — a clear commercial stake in defending a biological, drug-treatable model of depression.

Independent experts polled by the UK's Science Media Centre landed in between: nearly all agreed the old "simple deficiency" pitch is outdated, but split — without clean funding lines — on how much newer imaging evidence still implicates serotonin.

**Verdict:** the simplistic chemical-imbalance claim doesn't survive scrutiny and was never well supported. The narrower question of serotonin's *some* role remains genuinely contested — but the loudest pushback comes from researchers financially tied to the drugs whose rationale is being questioned.
