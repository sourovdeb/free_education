# Research Audit: "Power posing" changes your hormones and makes you more confident

**Routine:** 03_psychology_marketing_mental_health_audit_routine
**Date:** 2026-07-05
**Domain:** Psychology / neuroscience / marketing & self-help influence

---

## 1. The claim

Popularized by Amy Cuddy's 2012 TED talk (now 75M+ views, the 3rd most-watched TED talk ever) and her 2015 bestselling book *Presence*: standing in an expansive "power pose" (e.g., the "Wonder Woman" stance) for as little as two minutes measurably **raises testosterone, lowers cortisol, and makes you take more risks and feel more powerful**. The claim spread widely into corporate training, job-interview coaching, and public-speaking marketing as a science-backed confidence hack.

## 2. Primary sources

| Study | Design | Sample | Venue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010) | 1-minute high- vs. low-power poses; pre/post saliva testosterone & cortisol; gambling risk-taking task | **N = 42** | *Psychological Science* |
| Ranehill, Dreber, Johannesson, Leiberg, Sul & Weber (2015) | Close conceptual replication, larger and more balanced sample, no outlier exclusion, preregistered-style rigor | **N = 200** (98 women, 102 men) | *Psychological Science* |
| Simmons & Simonsohn (2017), "P-Curving the Evidence" | Statistical p-curve analysis testing whether the body of power-posing studies has "evidential value" | 33 studies | *Psychological Science* |
| Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse (2018), reply to Simmons & Simonsohn | Expanded p-curve analysis, broader inclusion criteria | 55 studies | *Meta-Psychology* |

**Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010):** Reported high-power posers showed testosterone increases and cortisol decreases, plus greater self-reported feelings of power and more risk-taking in a gambling task, versus low-power posers.

**Ranehill et al. (2015):** With a sample nearly 5x larger, replicated the *self-reported feelings of power* effect but found **no significant effect on testosterone, cortisol, or actual risk-taking behavior** — the two headline physiological/behavioral claims did not replicate.

**Simmons & Simonsohn (2017):** Concluded the power-posing literature as a whole lacked "evidential value" — the pattern of p-values across studies looked more consistent with p-hacking/selective reporting than a real effect.

**Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse (2018):** Using a larger, more inclusive set of studies, found evidential value for effects on *self-reported feelings* (confidence, mood) but did not rehabilitate the hormonal or risk-tolerance claims that made the original finding famous.

## 3. Funding / conflict-of-interest check

- **Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010):** Internal Columbia Business School / Harvard Business School research funds; no pharmaceutical or industry funding disclosed. However, the **commercial stakes emerged after publication**: Cuddy's subsequent TED talk and *Presence* (500,000+ copies sold, reached #3 on the NYT bestseller list) turned the power-pose effect into the financial and reputational foundation of her speaking and publishing career — a direct commercial conflict of interest in continuing to defend the finding, even though it didn't exist at the time of the original study.
- **Dana Carney (co-author), 2016 public statement:** Carney published a signed statement disavowing her own study, citing small sample size, researcher degrees of freedom in the statistical analysis, and her own view that she does "not believe" the effect on hormones/risk-taking is real. She had nothing financially to gain from this retraction and a personal/professional cost in publicly contradicting a famous paper — which makes her disavowal a relatively strong, low-bias data point.
- **Ranehill et al. (2015):** Academic funding via Swedish and Swiss university/research-council channels (University of Zurich, Stockholm School of Economics collaborators); no commercial stake in the outcome either way.
- **Simmons & Simonsohn:** Independent methodologists (University of Pennsylvania/Wharton) with no financial stake in power posing specifically; their broader professional reputation is built on detecting exactly this kind of shaky effect, which is a mild motivated-reasoning consideration but not a financial conflict.
- **Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse (2018):** Authored by the original claim's principal commercial beneficiary — the clearest conflict-of-interest source in this set.

## 4. Bias pattern assessment

The pattern of results tracks commercial stake closely: the analysis authored by the researcher with the greatest financial and reputational investment in the effect (Cuddy) is the one still defending it, while every analysis from researchers with no stake in the outcome — the large independent replication (Ranehill et al.), the independent statisticians (Simmons & Simonsohn), and eventually Cuddy's own original co-author (Carney) — found the striking, marketable claims (hormones, real risk-taking) did not hold up. The one piece of the claim that survives across camps (self-reported feelings of confidence) is also the piece most vulnerable to demand characteristics, since participants can guess what response researchers expect.

## 5. Contested vs. robust

- **Robust (settled against the popular claim):** No reliable evidence that brief power posing changes testosterone, cortisol, or actual risk-taking behavior — a 5x-larger direct replication failed, and the original lead author has publicly retracted confidence in that part of the finding.
- **Contested:** Whether power posing produces a genuine causal effect on self-reported feelings of confidence/power, versus reflecting participants' expectations of the "correct" answer. Both later p-curve analyses (Simmons & Simonsohn vs. Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse) reach opposite headline conclusions from overlapping data, and the dispute hasn't been resolved with a decisive follow-up replication.

## 6. Verdict

The version of "power posing" that went viral — a two-minute pose measurably shifting your hormones and making you take more risks — does not survive scrutiny. It failed a large, well-powered direct replication, and the study's own lead author now disavows it. The much more modest surviving claim (some effect on how confident people *say* they feel) remains genuinely disputed among independent statisticians, with no financial stake in either direction. The strongest continued public defense of the original, dramatic claim comes from the researcher whose book and speaking career depend on it being true — a conflict of interest worth weighing heavily against the popular version of this story.

## Micro-blog post (for publication)

**Title:** Power Posing: What Survived the Replication, and What Didn't

Amy Cuddy's 2012 TED talk — the third most-watched of all time — claimed a two-minute "power pose" measurably raises testosterone, lowers cortisol, and makes you take more risks. It fueled a bestselling book and a corporate-training industry. The original study (Carney, Cuddy & Yap, 2010, *Psychological Science*) had just 42 participants.

In 2015, Ranehill et al. ran a close replication with nearly five times the sample (N=200) and more rigorous controls. They replicated the self-reported "I feel more powerful" effect — but found **no effect on testosterone, cortisol, or actual risk-taking**, the two claims that made the talk go viral. In 2016, original co-author Dana Carney published her own statement saying she no longer believes the hormone/risk-taking effects are real, citing small samples and flexible statistical choices. She had nothing to gain, and something to lose, by saying so.

Independent statisticians Simmons and Simonsohn (2017) then found the whole power-posing literature showed no statistical "evidential value." Cuddy fired back in 2018 with her own reanalysis of a larger study set, finding support for effects on self-reported feelings — but not for hormones or behavior. Notably, the person still defending the strongest version of the claim is the one whose book and speaking career were built on it; the people finding it doesn't hold up have no comparable stake either way.

**Bottom line:** the dramatic, physiology-changing version of power posing that made this famous does not hold up under independent replication. A weaker "you might feel a bit more confident" effect is still genuinely contested. Treat commercial confidence coaching built on the hormone claim with real skepticism.
