# Claim Audit: "Depression Is Caused by a Chemical Imbalance (Low Serotonin)"

**Audit date:** 2026-07-04
**Domain:** Neuroscience / Mental health treatment
**Verdict:** Contested — the specific "low serotonin causes depression" claim is not supported by the current evidence base, but this does not mean antidepressants don't work for many patients; it means the *mechanism* long used to justify prescribing them is unproven.

---

## 1. The core claim

For decades, depression has been popularly explained — including by drug marketing and by some clinicians — as being caused by a "chemical imbalance," specifically low serotonin, and SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) work by correcting it.

## 2. Primary source and methodology

- Moncrieff, J., Cooper, R.E., Stockmann, T., Amendola, S., Hengartner, M.P., Horowitz, M.A. (2022). "The serotonin theory of depression: a systematic umbrella review of the evidence." *Molecular Psychiatry*. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-022-01661-0 (open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618090/)
- Design: umbrella review (a review of reviews) covering 17 sources — 12 systematic reviews/meta-analyses, a large collaborative meta-analysis, a meta-analysis of large cohort studies, a narrative systematic review, a genetic association study, and one prior umbrella review — spanning serotonin/5-HIAA fluid concentrations, 5-HT1A receptor binding, serotonin transporter (SERT) imaging and post-mortem studies, tryptophan depletion studies, and SERT gene/gene-environment studies.
- Finding: no consistent evidence that depression is associated with lowered serotonin concentration or activity, across any of these lines of evidence.

## 3. Funding and conflicts of interest

**Moncrieff et al. (2022):** No specific funding for the review; one author's fellowship is funded by an NHS trust. Disclosed conflicts: the lead author and a co-author receive royalties from books critical of psychiatric drugs, and one co-founded a company that helps patients taper off antidepressants — a commercial interest aligned with the paper's conclusion, which readers should weigh alongside the data.

**The main rebuttal:** a Comment signed by 36 researchers, led by Sameer Jauhar and Carmine Pariante (King's College London IoPPN), published in *Molecular Psychiatry* (2023): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-023-02093-0 — argues the umbrella review used selective inclusion criteria, downplayed tryptophan-depletion evidence in people with a depression history, and mischaracterized receptor/imaging findings. Notably, co-author Carmine Pariante discloses research funding and consortium ties to Janssen, GSK, Lundbeck and Pfizer (via the Wellcome-Trust-backed NIMA consortium) and paid speaking engagements for Lundbeck — direct pharmaceutical-industry financial relationships on the side defending the drug-treatment rationale.

**Broader antidepressant efficacy literature:** independent audits have found industry involvement is common and correlated with more favorable conclusions. A review of 185 antidepressant meta-analyses (2007–2014) found 79% had some industry link, and industry-affiliated meta-analyses were ~22x less likely to report negative statements about a drug (Ebrahim et al., 2016, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26399904/). A 2006 study found industry-sponsored psychiatric drug trials reported favorable outcomes 78% of the time vs. 48% for independently funded trials (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16199844/).

## 4. Bias pattern check

- The critical-psychiatry side has a modest commercial/reputational interest (book royalties, a deprescribing company) in the "no chemical imbalance" conclusion.
- The pro-drug-treatment rebuttal's most prominent co-author has substantial, disclosed pharmaceutical-industry funding ties, which is a stronger and more direct financial interest in defending the serotonin-based treatment rationale.
- Independent of either side, industry-wide meta-research shows a consistent, quantifiable pattern of industry-sponsored antidepressant research skewing toward favorable conclusions — this is the strongest "neutral" evidence of directional bias in the broader literature.

## 5. Comparison with opposing/independent research

Both the 2022 review and its 2023 rebuttal have been through peer review, and clinical trial data independently confirm SSRIs produce small but statistically real symptom-reduction effects versus placebo in acute major depressive disorder (e.g., Cipriani et al. 2018 network meta-analysis in *The Lancet*: https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(17)32802-7/fulltext). That efficacy finding is separate from, and does not depend on, the serotonin-deficiency mechanism — which is the specific point Moncrieff et al. dispute. The claim that "SSRIs work by fixing low serotonin" and the claim "SSRIs measurably reduce depressive symptoms for some patients" are not the same claim, and conflating them is where most of the public confusion (and marketing) originates.

## 6. Micro-blog (~260 words) — News Item

> **"Chemical imbalance" depression theory: what the evidence actually shows**
>
> For 30+ years, "depression is caused by low serotonin" has been the default public explanation for why antidepressants work. A 2022 umbrella review in *Molecular Psychiatry* by Moncrieff et al. — synthesizing 17 systematic reviews and meta-analyses covering serotonin levels, receptor binding, transporter imaging, tryptophan depletion, and gene studies — found **no consistent evidence** linking depression to lowered serotonin activity ([Nature/Molecular Psychiatry](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-022-01661-0), [free full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618090/)).
>
> That's not the end of the story. A rebuttal signed by 36 researchers, led by Sameer Jauhar and Carmine Pariante of King's College London, argues the review cherry-picked and misread key studies ([Molecular Psychiatry, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-023-02093-0)). Worth knowing: Pariante discloses funding and consulting ties to Janssen, GSK, Lundbeck and Pfizer — a direct financial stake in defending the serotonin-based treatment narrative. Meanwhile, Moncrieff and a co-author have their own smaller commercial interest: book royalties and a company that helps patients taper off antidepressants.
>
> Zooming out, independent industry audits (not tied to either side) found that meta-analyses with pharma involvement are ~22x less likely to report anything negative about a drug ([Ebrahim et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26399904/)) — real, quantified bias in this literature.
>
> **Bottom line:** the "low serotonin causes depression" mechanism looks unsupported. That's separate from whether SSRIs help some patients — trial data (e.g. [Cipriani et al., *Lancet* 2018](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(17)32802-7/fulltext)) show a small real effect over placebo. The marketing claim and the treatment-efficacy claim have been conflated for decades; they shouldn't be.
