# Claim Audit: "Antidepressants Are Clearly Effective for Depression"

**Routine:** 03_psych_research_audit_routine
**Date:** 2026-07-05
**Category:** Mental Health Treatment / Psychiatry
**Tags:** antidepressants, SSRIs, publication bias, pharmaceutical funding, depression treatment, evidence-based medicine
**SEO title:** Do Antidepressants Work? Auditing the Funding and the Evidence
**Meta description:** A funding-and-methodology audit of the antidepressant efficacy debate — what FDA data, independent meta-analyses, and industry-funded trials actually show.

---

## 1. Core claim

Pharmaceutical marketing, many clinical guidelines, and popular press routinely state that SSRI/SNRI antidepressants are clearly and substantially effective for major depressive disorder. This audit checks that claim against the primary trial data, the funding behind it, and the strongest independent counter-analyses.

## 2. Primary sources and methodology

- **Turner et al., *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2008.** Compared 74 FDA-registered trials of 12 antidepressants (12,564 patients) against what was actually published in the literature.
  https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa065779
  - 94% of *published* trials appeared positive; only 51% of the *complete FDA dataset* was positive.
  - 31% of FDA-registered trials (3,449 participants) were never published — nearly all of the unpublished trials had null/negative results.
  - Publication bias inflated the apparent drug-class effect size by ~32%.

- **Kirsch et al., *PLoS Medicine*, 2008.** Independent, FOIA-obtained FDA data (not company-supplied) for the 6 most-prescribed antidepressants.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582668/
  - Overall drug–placebo effect size = 0.32 (Cohen's d), below the UK NICE clinical-significance threshold of 0.50 in place at the time.
  - Meaningful drug–placebo separation appeared only at the most severe end of the depression-severity scale.

- **Cipriani et al., *The Lancet*, 2018.** The largest network meta-analysis to date: 522 trials, ~116,000 participants, 21 antidepressants.
  https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(17)32802-7/fulltext
  - Found every antidepressant studied statistically outperformed placebo — the evidence most often cited to settle the debate in favor of efficacy.
  - Funding: National Institute for Health Research (Oxford Health Biomedical Research Centre) and the Japan Society for the Promotion of Science — independent of drug manufacturers.
  - Caveat acknowledged even by supporters: 73% of the 522 pooled trials were rated moderate risk of bias, 9% high risk; only 18% were low risk.

## 3. Funding and conflict-of-interest check

- The **Cipriani (2018) study itself** was independently funded (NIHR + a Japanese public research body), not by pharmaceutical companies — a genuine point in its favor.
- However, the **underlying 522 trials it pooled** were mostly run and funded over decades by the drug manufacturers whose products were being tested. A separate meta-epidemiological analysis found industry-sponsored antidepressant trials are roughly **30% more likely to report favorable results** than independently funded trials, a pattern not explained by other measured sources of bias.
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9808270/
- Broader review of psychiatric drug meta-analyses found that when **study authors were employees of the drug's manufacturer**, the resulting analyses were roughly 22-fold less likely to include any negative statement about the drug, versus meta-analyses without that conflict.
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7140556/
- Turner (2008) and Kirsch (2008) sidestepped this problem entirely by using **FDA-submitted trial data** (obtained via regulatory filing / FOIA) rather than only published, company-selected results — which is precisely why their findings diverge from the published-literature consensus.

## 4. Bias pattern assessment

The pattern is consistent across all three sources: study conclusions correlate with how close the researchers were to the trial's original funder. Company-run, company-published trials skew positive; FDA-derived, independently-analyzed data skew more modest; and even a well-funded independent meta-analysis (Cipriani) inherits bias from the underlying company-sponsored trial base it must pool from, because so much of the antidepressant trial literature was generated and controlled by manufacturers in the first place.

## 5. Robustness verdict

**Partially contested, not resolved.** The claim "antidepressants beat placebo" is well replicated (Cipriani 2018 is strong evidence for this narrow claim). The stronger marketing claim — that antidepressants produce a *large, clinically obvious* benefit for the average depressed patient — is not well supported once publication bias (Turner 2008) and clinical-significance thresholds (Kirsch 2008) are accounted for. Benefit appears concentrated in severe depression and modest-to-negligible for mild-moderate cases, which is the range in which most prescriptions are written.

>
> The claim, repeated in drug marketing and many treatment guidelines, is that antidepressants are clearly effective for depression. The evidence is more contested than that framing suggests.

(*PLoS Medicine*, 2008), using the same FDA data via FOIA — independent of company reporting — found a drug–placebo difference below the UK NICE threshold for clinical significance (d = 0.32 vs. a 0.50 cutoff), with real benefit concentrated in the most severe cases. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582668/))
>
> The strongest counter-evidence, Cipriani et al. (*The Lancet*, 2018; 522 trials, ~116,000 patients), found all 21 antidepressants studied beat placebo. > **Verdict:** statistical superiority over placebo is well replicated. The *size* of that benefit for average-severity depression, and how much of the trial base was shaped by manufacturer funding, remains a genuine, unresolved dispute — not settled by any single study.

## 7. Disclosure and limits of this audit

This audit relied on publicly available, peer-reviewed sources and their disclosed funding statements; it did not independently verify author financial disclosures beyond what each paper itself reports, and it did not conduct new statistical re-analysis of raw trial data. At least two independent sources are cited (an NIHR/Japan-funded Lancet study, an FDA-FOIA-based PLoS Medicine study, and an independent NEJM analysis), and their funding sources are disclosed above. Readers making treatment decisions should consult a qualified clinician; this is a research-transparency audit, not medical advice.
