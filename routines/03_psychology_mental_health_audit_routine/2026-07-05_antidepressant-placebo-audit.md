# Research Audit: "Antidepressants barely beat placebo"

**Routine:** 03_psychology_mental_health_audit_routine
**Date:** 2026-07-05
**Domain:** Mental health treatment / psychopharmacology

---

## 1. The claim

A recurring claim across wellness media and social platforms: *"SSRIs and other antidepressants perform barely better than sugar pills — except maybe in severe depression."* It's used both by anti-psychiatry commentators (to argue the drugs don't work) and, in a milder form, by clinicians explaining that effect sizes are modest.

## 2. Primary sources

| Study | Design | Sample | Venue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turner, Matthews, Linardatos, Tell, Rosenthal (2008) | Compared FDA-registered trial results to what was actually published in journals | 74 FDA-registered trials, 12,564 patients, 12 antidepressants | *New England Journal of Medicine* |
| Kirsch, Deacon, Huedo-Medina, Scoboria, Moore, Johnson (2008) | Meta-analysis of the same FDA submission data, examining effect size vs. baseline severity | Same FDA dataset (12 drugs) | *PLoS Medicine* |
| Cipriani et al. (2018) | Network meta-analysis, 21 antidepressants vs. placebo/each other | 522 trials, ~117,000 patients | *The Lancet* |

**Turner et al. findings:** 31% of FDA-registered trials (23/74, 3,449 patients) were never published. Of 38 FDA-positive trials, 37 were published as positive. Of 36 negative/questionable trials, only 3 were published as negative — 22 vanished, 11 were published framed as positive. Net effect: published literature made drugs look ~94% positive; the FDA's own data put it at 51%. Selective publication inflated apparent effect size by ~32% on average (11–69% per drug).

**Kirsch et al. findings:** Using the same FDA data, drug-placebo separation was negligible-to-small for mild/moderate depression, reaching conventional clinical significance only at the most severe end — and that gap was driven mainly by *reduced placebo response* in severely depressed patients, not increased drug response.

**Cipriani et al. findings:** All 21 antidepressants studied outperformed placebo (this is the largest, most methodologically current synthesis, incorporating unpublished data recovered for 52% of trials).

## 3. Funding / conflict-of-interest check

- **Turner et al. (2008):** Authors affiliated with Oregon Health & Science University / Portland VA Medical Center. Lead author's disclosed conflict was a prior role as an FDA medical reviewer — no pharmaceutical industry funding disclosed.
- **Kirsch et al. (2008):** Academic authors (Hull, Connecticut, et al.); no industry funding disclosed. Note: Kirsch is a long-standing, publicly critical voice on antidepressant efficacy — a source of potential bias in the opposite direction (motivated skepticism), worth flagging even though no financial conflict exists.
- **Cipriani et al. (2018):** Authors (Cipriani, Furukawa, Salanti, Ioannidis, et al.) declared no personal competing interests. However, **409 of 522 trials (78%) feeding the analysis were industry-funded** — the underlying evidence base, not the authors, carries the conflict.
- **Background evidence on the bias pattern:** Lundh et al., Cochrane systematic review (2017), found industry-sponsored drug/device trials are ~30% more likely to report favorable efficacy results and ~87% more likely to report favorable harms results than independently-funded trials.

## 4. Bias pattern assessment

The funding pattern here is indirect but real: the two studies finding *modest* effects (Turner, Kirsch) are independently/government-affiliated with no industry money; the study finding *robust, uniform* superiority over placebo (Cipriani) is built on a corpus that is 78% industry-funded, in a research area where industry funding is empirically linked to more favorable results (Lundh et al.). That doesn't make Cipriani wrong — it's a rigorous synthesis using recovered unpublished data — but it means "the biggest study says antidepressants clearly work" and "the industry-funded literature says antidepressants clearly work" are not fully separable claims here.

## 5. Contested vs. robust

- **Robust:** publication bias inflated the *apparent* efficacy of antidepressants in the literature prior to ~2008 (Turner et al. is not seriously disputed).
- **Contested:** whether the true drug-placebo gap is clinically negligible in mild-to-moderate depression (Kirsch's position) or consistently meaningful across severity (Cipriani's position, plus later reanalyses like Munkholm et al.). No consensus resolution as of the most recent literature.

## 6. Verdict

Neither "antidepressants are basically placebo" nor "antidepressants are unambiguously proven effective" survives scrutiny as a flat claim. The honest position: publication bias measurably inflated the evidence base for years; the best current synthesis (Cipriani 2018) still finds a real effect, but it draws on a trial base that is majority industry-funded in a field where that funding source correlates with more favorable results. Readers should weight "antidepressants clearly work across the board" claims against that provenance, and weight "antidepressants don't work" claims against the fact that even severity-skeptical reanalyses (Kirsch) found a real, if narrower, effect in severe depression.

## Micro-blog post (for publication)

**Title:** Do Antidepressants Beat Placebo? What the Funding Trail Adds

A common claim in wellness and anti-psychiatry media: "SSRIs barely beat placebo, except in severe depression." The evidence is more contested than either side lets on — and funding is part of the story.

Turner et al. (*NEJM*, 2008) compared FDA-registered trial data (74 trials, 12,564 patients) against what actually got published, and found selective publication: 94% of published trials looked positive, versus 51% in the FDA's own data — inflating apparent efficacy by ~32%. Kirsch et al. (*PLoS Medicine*, 2008), reanalyzing that same FDA dataset, found drug-placebo differences were small to negligible except at the most severe end of depression. Neither team disclosed pharmaceutical funding; both are independent academic/government-affiliated analyses.

The counterweight is Cipriani et al.'s 2018 *Lancet* network meta-analysis — the largest, most rigorous synthesis to date (522 trials, ~117,000 patients) — which found all 21 antidepressants studied beat placebo. Its authors reported no personal competing interests, but 78% of the trials in the dataset were themselves industry-funded, which matters: a 2017 Cochrane review (Lundh et al.) found industry-sponsored drug trials are roughly 30% more likely to report favorable results than independently funded ones.

**Verdict:** neither "antidepressants don't work" nor "antidepressants clearly work" holds up as a blanket claim. Publication bias genuinely inflated the pre-2008 evidence base. The strongest current synthesis still shows a real effect, but rests on a majority industry-funded trial pool — a documented source of bias in this literature. Treat sweeping claims from either side skeptically.
