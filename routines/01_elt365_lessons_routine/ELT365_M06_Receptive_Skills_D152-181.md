### D152 · Gist vs Detail: the Two Listening Modes
**Idea —** Every listening task needs a purpose. Gist tasks ask learners to get the overall message; detail tasks ask them to locate specific information. Mixing them up in the same task kills both.  
**Example —** Play a news clip. Gist task: what is the topic? Detail task (second listen): what three facts does the speaker give?  
**Try —** Take a listening you use regularly. Write one gist question and one detail question. Run them as separate listens next lesson.

---

### D153 · The Text Is Not the Task
**Idea —** Comprehension of a listening text and completion of a task are different things. Learners can score poorly on a task even when they understood the recording — because the task was badly designed.  
**Example —** A learner answers true/false by guessing from key words, not from understanding. The task measured test-taking, not listening.  
**Try —** Review your next listening task before class. Ask: could a learner get full marks without actually understanding the text? If yes, redesign.

---

### D154 · Pre-teaching Vocabulary: When It Helps
**Idea —** Pre-teach only blocking vocabulary — words whose absence will stop learners completing the task. Pre-teaching everything removes the challenge; pre-teaching nothing leaves learners lost.  
**Example —** Text contains: *deforestation, habitat, biodiversity*. Pre-teach all three before a gist task — learners just need to know the topic is about forests.  
**Try —** Pick a listening for this week. List every unknown word. Cross off any that won't block task completion. Pre-teach only what's left.

---

### D155 · Top-Down Processing: Using Context to Predict
**Idea —** Top-down processing means using prior knowledge, context, and expectations to interpret a text. Activate it before learners hear a word and comprehension improves.  
**Example —** Show a photo of two people arguing in an office. Ask: who are they? What might they be saying? Then play the dialogue. Learners already have a frame.  
**Try —** Before your next listening, spend 60 seconds eliciting context: topic, speakers, setting. Notice whether it changes how well learners follow the audio.

---

### D156 · Bottom-Up Processing: Decoding the Signal
**Idea —** Bottom-up processing is the decoding work: recognising phonemes, words, and chunks in the stream of speech. Learners who struggle with connected speech need bottom-up practice, not just more listens.  
**Example —** Dictation and 'phoneme spotting' tasks build bottom-up skill. 'Listen and mark the stress' is another. These are distinct from comprehension tasks.  
**Try —** Design one bottom-up task for a text you already use: a short dictation, a stress-marking exercise, or a 'how many words do you hear?' count.

---

### D157 · Jigsaw Listening: One Text, Two Groups
**Idea —** In a jigsaw task, different learners hear different parts of a recording. They then share information to complete the whole picture. The information gap creates a real communicative need.  
**Example —** Group A hears the first half of an interview; Group B hears the second. Each group answers their own questions, then pairs up to reconstruct the full account.  
**Try —** Identify a recording that divides naturally into two parts. Write separate task sheets for each group. Plan the share phase as a structured speaking task.

---

### D158 · While-Listening Tasks That Actually Work
**Idea —** Good while-listening tasks focus attention without demanding so much cognitive load that learners stop listening. Multiple-choice with four options, long-form note-taking, and reading dense text while listening all compete for processing.  
**Example —** Simple ticking tasks, ordering, and single-word gap fills work well. They guide attention without hijacking it.  
**Try —** Audit one while-listening task you use. Count how many words learners must read while also listening. If it's more than 30, redesign it.

---

### D159 · Post-Listening: From Comprehension to Production
**Idea —** After checking answers, a post-listening stage connects the text to learners' language use. Without it, the lesson ends at comprehension — a passive skill.  
**Example —** After a listening about a job interview, learners role-play their own interview using language from the recording.  
**Try —** Add a post-listening stage to your next lesson. It can be as short as two minutes: a discussion question, a written sentence, or a brief role-play.

---

### D160 · Authentic Audio vs Graded Recordings
**Idea —** Authentic audio exposes learners to natural speech rate and connected speech. Graded recordings control input for clarity. Neither is always right.  
**Example —** A1 learners need graded input to build confidence. B2 learners benefit from authentic material to close the gap with real-world listening.  
**Try —** Next time you choose a listening, ask: is the challenge from the language, the task, or the speech rate? Only the first two are pedagogically useful.

---

### D161 · Grading the Task, Not the Text
**Idea —** A B2 authentic text can be used with A2 learners if the task asks only for top-level gist. The text provides exposure; the task sets the achievable challenge.  
**Example —** Play a C1-level podcast to A2 learners. Task: how many different speakers do you hear? Learners don't need to understand every word to answer.  
**Try —** Choose one text above your class's level. Write a gist task they can genuinely complete.

---

### D162 · Skimming: Reading for the Main Idea
**Idea —** Skimming is reading quickly to identify the general topic or main point. It is a skill, not a natural behaviour — learners need to practise ignoring detail to get the overview.  
**Example —** Give learners 45 seconds to read a 200-word text and answer: what is it about in one sentence?  
**Try —** Time your learners on one skimming task this week. 45–60 seconds for 200 words is the right pressure.

---

### D163 · Scanning: Locating Specific Information
**Idea —** Scanning means moving across a text quickly to locate a specific piece of information, ignoring everything else.  
**Example —** Learners scan a restaurant menu to find: 'Which dish contains nuts?' They shouldn't read every word.  
**Try —** Give learners a task with a specific target: a name, a number, a date. They have 30 seconds to find it.

---

### D164 · Intensive Reading: Every Word Counts
**Idea —** Intensive reading asks learners to process a short text very carefully: every word, every grammatical signal, every implication.  
**Example —** A legal clause or a poem — learners read once, then answer detailed inference questions that require tracking meaning across the whole text.  
**Try —** Select a text of 60–80 words. Write three questions that cannot be answered without reading every sentence carefully.

---

### D165 · Schema Activation Before Reading
**Idea —** Schema is prior knowledge. Activating it before reading speeds up comprehension and reduces cognitive load.  
**Example —** Before reading a news article about climate change, ask: what do you already know? What do you expect to read?  
**Try —** Write three pre-reading questions for your next text. They should activate relevant knowledge, not test it.

---

### D166 · While-Reading Tasks: Guiding Attention
**Idea —** While-reading tasks should guide learners through the text, not test them after the fact.  
**Example —** Learners read and underline every phrase that expresses the writer's opinion. The annotation is the task.  
**Try —** Design a while-reading task that uses annotation, ordering, or matching rather than traditional questions.

---

### D167 · Post-Reading: Moving from Text to Talk
**Idea —** A post-reading stage extends the text into production. Without it, reading ends at passive understanding.  
**Example —** After reading an opinion piece about social media, learners write one sentence agreeing or disagreeing, then share and discuss.  
**Try —** Add one post-reading activity: a 2-minute discussion, a written sentence, or a mini-debate.

---

### D168 · Unknown Words: Reader Strategies
**Idea —** Learners who stop at every unknown word never build reading fluency. Teaching strategies for handling unknown vocabulary is as important as teaching the vocabulary itself.  
**Example —** Strategy one: skip and read on. Strategy two: use context clues. Strategy three: look for word parts.  
**Try —** Teach one strategy explicitly. Give learners a text with three unknown words and ask them to use the strategy before checking a dictionary.

---

### D169 · Selecting Reading Texts for Class
**Idea —** A good classroom reading text is appropriately levelled, genuinely interesting, and generates language worth noticing.  
**Example —** Criteria: is the topic relevant to learners' lives? Does it contain target language? Is it short enough to complete in class time?  
**Try —** Before choosing your next reading text, answer: why this topic? What language will learners notice? What will they do with it after?

---

### D170 · Authentic vs Graded Readers
**Idea —** Graded readers simplify vocabulary and syntax. Authentic texts keep the full complexity. Both have a place.  
**Example —** A B1 learner reads a graded crime novel for fluency. The same learner reads an authentic news article with teacher support to see how real English looks.  
**Try —** Plan one graded reader task and one authentic text activity for the same class this month.

---

### D171 · Reading Aloud: When It Helps and When It Doesn't
**Idea —** Round-the-class reading aloud is usually pronunciation practice at best and a source of anxiety at worst.  
**Example —** Use teacher read-aloud to model prosody. Use learner read-aloud only for short, rehearsed, meaningful tasks.  
**Try —** Replace one round-the-class reading with silent reading plus a task. Compare learner comprehension.

---

### D172 · Extensive Reading: What It Is and How to Start
**Idea —** Extensive reading means reading large quantities at a comfortable level for pleasure and fluency, not for study. Nation's research suggests it is one of the most effective vocabulary acquisition routes.  
**Example —** Learners choose a graded reader at or below their level, read outside class, and report back in 90 seconds. No comprehension test.  
**Try —** Research one free extensive reading resource for your learners' level. Plan how you would introduce it in under two minutes.

---

### D173 · Listening for Attitude and Opinion
**Idea —** Beyond facts and details, proficient listeners track how a speaker feels about what they are saying.  
**Example —** Play a speaker discussing a topic they clearly dislike. Ask: how does the speaker feel? How do you know?  
**Try —** Add one question about attitude, not content: 'How does the speaker feel about X?' Discuss how learners know.

---

### D174 · Note-Taking While Listening
**Idea —** Note-taking while listening is a productive skill, but it competes with comprehension at lower levels. Teach it explicitly.  
**Example —** Learners listen and note only numbers: dates, quantities, prices. Second listen: fill in what the numbers refer to.  
**Try —** Design a two-stage note-taking task: first listen (one category), second listen (context).

---

### D175 · Inferencing from Context
**Idea —** Inference means working out what is implied but not stated. It is a higher-order skill worth teaching explicitly.  
**Example —** A speaker says 'That was certainly… interesting' about a meal. Did they enjoy it? The pause and stress tell us — not the word itself.  
**Try —** Find a 30-second clip where the speaker implies something. Ask: what did they say? What did they mean?

---

### D176 · Predicting Before Listening
**Idea —** Prediction activates schema, reduces anxiety, and gives learners a purpose for listening.  
**Example —** Show the title of a podcast and a photo of the guest. Ask: what do you think they will talk about? Then play.  
**Try —** Add a one-minute prediction stage before your next listening. Notice whether it changes engagement.

---

### D177 · Checking Comprehension Without 'Did You Understand?'
**Idea —** Asking 'Did you understand?' is useless: learners who didn't understand don't know they didn't.  
**Example —** Instead: 'What three things did the speaker say about the project timeline?' The answer reveals real comprehension.  
**Try —** Write two specific comprehension questions for your next listening — neither answerable with yes or no.

---

### D178 · Using Video in Class
**Idea —** Video adds a visual channel to listening. Watch without sound first to build prediction; then watch with sound.  
**Example —** Play a 60-second clip without sound. Ask: what is happening? Play again with sound. What did you miss?  
**Try —** Sequence it: silent watch → predict → watch with sound → confirm or revise.

---

### D179 · Connected Speech and Listening
**Idea —** Native speakers link, reduce, and swallow sounds in ways that standard syllabi don't cover. Learners trained on careful speech are unprepared for real connected speech.  
**Example —** 'What are you going to do?' becomes 'Whaddya gonna do?' in fast speech.  
**Try —** Take one sentence from authentic audio. Transcribe it as it sounds. Teach learners to identify the connections.

---

### D180 · L1 Interference in Listening
**Idea —** L1 phonological systems can interfere with listening in English. Learners may systematically mishear phoneme contrasts that don't exist in their first language.  
**Example —** Japanese learners may consistently mishear /l/ and /r/. Arabic speakers may struggle with short vowel distinctions.  
**Try —** Research one phoneme contrast difficult for your learners' L1. Design a minimal pairs task for a warm-up.
