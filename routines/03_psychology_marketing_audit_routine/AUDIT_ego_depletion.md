# Research Audit: "Ego Depletion" (Willpower as a Limited Resource)

**Routine:** 03_psychology_marketing_audit_routine
**Date:** 2026-07-12
**Method:** Primary source review + funding check + replication comparison (steps 1–6 of the audit brief)

---

## 1. The core claim

"Ego depletion": exercising self-control on one task temporarily uses up a limited
psychological resource, leaving less willpower for the *next* task. This became the
scientific basis for a huge amount of pop-psychology and productivity advice — "willpower
is like a muscle," "do your hardest task first before you deplete," "don't make big
decisions when you're tired."

## 2. Primary source

- Baumeister, R. F., Bratslavsky, E., Muraven, M., & Tice, D. M. (1998). *Ego depletion:
  Is the active self a limited resource?* Journal of Personality and Social Psychology,
  74(5), 1252–1265. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599441/)
- Four small lab experiments (radish-vs-chocolate task is the famous one); people who
  resisted eating chocolate cookies in favor of radishes quit an unsolvable puzzle faster
  than people who weren't asked to resist anything.
- Follow-on theorizing added a **glucose/"muscle model"** mechanism (self-control runs on
  blood glucose, like a muscle runs out of fuel).

## 3. Funding check

- The original 1998 paper was supported by two **NIH/NIMH grants**: MH-51482 and
  MH-57039 (public U.S. federal funding, not industry money).
- No pharmaceutical, supplement, or marketing-industry funding is disclosed on the paper.
- **Conclusion on funding:** no obvious commercial conflict of interest in the founding
  study. This is a case where the problem isn't "who paid for it" — it's ordinary academic
  incentives (see below).

## 4. Bias / methodology assessment

- Small per-cell sample sizes (typical of 1990s social psychology), a single lab/research
  group, and — per later critiques — a research environment with flexible analysis choices
  and a strong incentive to publish positive, novel results (publication bias).
- Carter & McCullough (2014) meta-analyzed the ego-depletion literature and found strong
  evidence of publication bias; correcting for it shrank the estimated effect toward zero.
  ([summary via HAL](https://hal.science/hal-03561895v1/document))
- The glucose-mechanism claim specifically failed to replicate the drop in blood glucose
  that the theory required. ([Replicability-Index](https://replicationindex.com/2016/04/18/rr1egodepletion/))

## 5. Independent replication attempts

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Hagger et al. (2010) | Meta-analysis of existing literature | Large pooled effect (before bias correction) |
| **Hagger et al. (2016)**, *Registered Replication Report*, Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573 | Pre-registered protocol, 23–24 independent labs, **2,141 participants** | **d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]** — indistinguishable from zero ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616652873), [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/07/160729143034.htm)) |
| Vohs et al. (2021) follow-up multi-lab replication | 36 labs, 3,531 participants | d = 0.06 — again close to zero |
| Carter & McCullough (2015) | Bias-corrected meta-analysis | Effect shrinks sharply once publication bias is accounted for |

Baumeister and Vohs publicly disputed the 2016 replication's protocol choices, arguing the
project coordinators had rejected the original lab's preferred task — a real and
substantive objection, but one addressed by the fact that the *second*, larger (36-lab)
replication using a broader range of protocols found the same near-zero result.

## 6. Verdict — does it hold up?

**Contested, and the weight of large-scale, pre-registered replication now points to an
effect that is much smaller than originally claimed, possibly zero.** The theory isn't a
industry-funded myth — it's a case study in how small-sample psychology + publication bias
can manufacture a "landmark finding" that a discipline treats as settled for over a
decade before multi-lab registered replications correct the record. Advice built directly
on the strong version of ego depletion ("protect your willpower like a battery") should be
treated as folklore, not settled science.

## Micro-blog post (published as a WordPress draft — see `publisher/`)

**Title:** Is "Ego Depletion" Real? What the Failed Replications Actually Show

For 15 years, "ego depletion" — the idea that willpower is a fuel tank that empties with
use — was social psychology's most-cited finding on self-control. Baumeister, Bratslavsky,
Muraven & Tice's original 1998 study (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*,
funded by two public NIH grants, not industry money) reported that resisting cookies in
favor of radishes made people quit a puzzle faster. It spawned a "muscle model" of
willpower, a glucose-depletion theory, and a cottage industry of productivity advice
("do the hard task first, before you run out").

Then came the checks. A 2010 meta-analysis found a large pooled effect — but a 2016
**Registered Replication Report**, pre-registered across 23–24 independent labs and 2,141
participants, found essentially nothing: d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15], statistically
indistinguishable from zero. A follow-up 36-lab replication (3,531 participants) found
d = 0.06. A separate bias-corrected meta-analysis found the original literature was
riddled with publication bias, shrinking the "true" effect toward zero once corrected.

No pharma or marketing money is implicated — the original funding was public. This looks
like ordinary academic incentives at work: small samples, flexible analysis choices, and a
bias toward publishing positive results, not a funder-driven conclusion. **Verdict:**
contested, likely far smaller than claimed, possibly zero. Treat "protect your willpower"
advice as folklore, not science.
