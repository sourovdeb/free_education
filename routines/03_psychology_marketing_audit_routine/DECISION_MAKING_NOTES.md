## 8. How do we actually make decisions? (childhood, parents, education, trauma, ideology, society)

Decision-making isn't one process — it runs on two interacting systems (Kahneman's
dual-process framework, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*):

- **System 1** — fast, automatic, emotional, pattern-matching. This is where most
  moment-to-moment choices actually happen.
- **System 2** — slow, effortful, deliberate. Used sparingly because it's metabolically
  and attentionally expensive.

The *content* System 1 draws on is built early and socially:

- **Childhood/parents/attachment** — early attachment patterns (Bowlby, Ainsworth) shape
  a lifelong template for trust, risk tolerance, and how threat/reward is read in
  ambiguous situations. Children internalize decision heuristics by watching caregivers
  ("modeling," Bandura's social learning theory) before they can articulate any reasoning.
- **Education** — teaches explicit System-2 tools (probability, cost-benefit reasoning)
  but only in the domains actually taught; most real-world decisions never receive that
  training and default back to heuristics.
- **Trauma** — recalibrates the threat-detection system (amygdala reactivity, HPA-axis
  sensitivity), which biases decisions toward loss-aversion and short-horizon,
  safety-first choices even in objectively low-risk situations.
- **Ideology/culture/society** — supplies the "default options" and social proof cues
  people fall back on under uncertainty; group identity is one of the strongest known
  predictors of belief-consistent decision-making, often overriding raw evidence.

**Bottom line:** a "decision" is rarely a fresh, from-scratch calculation. It's usually a
System-1 pattern match built from childhood attachment, modeled behavior, cultural
defaults, and prior threat exposure — with System 2 invoked only when the stakes are high
enough, or the person is prompted, to justify the cost of deliberate thought.

## 9. Marketers spend billions — what are they actually buying?

Global advertising spend is forecast to pass **$1 trillion for the first time in 2026**
(~$1.17T), digital advertising alone accounting for roughly $780–836B of that
([Statista](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1446002/advertising-marketing-spending-worldwide/),
[eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/worldwide-ad-spending-forecast-2026),
[Dentsu](https://www.dentsu.com/news-releases/global-ad-spend-set-to-surpass-one-trillion-for-the-first-time-in-2026-as-the-algorithmic-era-redefines-growth)).
Very little of that is trying to win an individual, deliberate purchase decision on the
spot. What it's actually buying:

1. **Mental availability / "share of memory"** — the goal (per Byron Sharp's evidence-based
   marketing research) is to be the brand that comes to mind first, in the most buying
   situations, later — not to argue anyone into a purchase today. Advertising is mostly
   priming System 1, not persuading System 2.
2. **Habit and default status** — repeated exposure builds automatic, low-thought
   repurchase behavior (the "mere exposure effect": familiarity itself increases liking,
   independent of any argument made).
3. **Emotional/identity association** — linking a brand to an emotion or in-group identity
   so the *choice itself* becomes a form of self-expression or belonging, which resists
   price and rational-comparison shopping.
4. **Long-run pricing power** — a brand people default to without comparison-shopping can
   sustain higher margins; that pricing power, compounded over years, is what the ad spend
   is actually amortized against, not any single sale.

**Why "spending money for no reason" happens:** most purchases aren't reasoned at all —
they're primed defaults, emotional-identity signals, or responses to engineered scarcity/
urgency cues (Cialdini's scarcity and social-proof principles). The spend isn't wasted from
the marketer's point of view; it's aimed at exactly the automatic, low-scrutiny decision
system described in Q8 and Q10 below — which is a *rational* target for a marketer even
though it produces choices the buyer wouldn't endorse under System-2 reflection.

## 10. Why do humans default to easy, low-effort options? Evolution, or something else?

Most likely **both**, and they reinforce each other:

- **Evolutionary/metabolic**: the brain is ~2% of body mass but consumes a
  disproportionately large share of resting energy — conserving cognitive effort was
  historically an actual survival advantage, not laziness. Deliberation is expensive;
  organisms that reserve it for genuinely high-stakes situations out-compete ones that
  deliberate constantly.
- **Cognitive-architecture ("cognitive miser")**: Herbert Simon's concept of **bounded
  rationality** and **satisficing** — people don't seek the optimal choice, they seek the
  first "good enough" option, because the cost of gathering and weighing more information
  usually exceeds the expected benefit of a marginally better choice.
- **Heuristics-and-biases**: Tversky & Kahneman's research catalogued the specific
  shortcuts (availability, representativeness, anchoring) that substitute for full
  calculation — these are efficient most of the time and only look like "errors" in the
  artificial cases psychologists construct to expose them.

So it isn't simply "laziness" — low-effort decision-making is close to an optimal strategy
given finite time, finite energy, and a world where most decisions are low-stakes. The
problem is that this same efficient machinery is exactly what marketing, propaganda, and
manipulative design are built to exploit (see Q9 and Q11).

## 11. Why are we easier to persuade when cognitively overloaded? What's overloading us now?

This is well-supported by the **Elaboration Likelihood Model** (Petty & Cacioppo): persuasion
happens along two routes — a *central* route (evaluating the actual argument, System 2) and
a *peripheral* route (relying on surface cues — source credibility, emotion, scarcity,
repetition — System 1). **Cognitive load pushes people from the central route to the
peripheral route** because System 2 capacity is limited and gets crowded out first. An
overloaded brain doesn't stop deciding — it just decides on cheaper cues, which are exactly
the cues persuasion/advertising techniques supply.

Current, well-documented sources of everyday overload:

- **Notification volume**: U.S. smartphone users report roughly 46–80 push notifications
  a day and check their phones on the order of 100+ times daily
  ([Speakwise](https://speakwiseapp.com/blog/notification-overload-statistics)).
- **Interruption cost**: research finds a ~2.8-second interruption can double the odds of a
  subsequent error, and refocusing after an interruption can take over 20 minutes — while
  cognitive-load theory shows that even *ignored* notifications add "extraneous load" that
  competes with whatever task is in front of you
  ([PsychUniverse](https://psychuniverse.com/cognitive-cost-of-notifications/)).
- **Infinite/algorithmic feeds and multitasking**: continuous, algorithmically-optimized
  content streams remove natural stopping cues and keep working memory perpetually
  partially occupied, leaving less spare System-2 capacity for whatever else is being
  decided (a purchase, an argument, a vote) at the same time.
- **Choice volume itself**: the popular claim that people make "35,000 decisions a day" (or
  "200 food decisions a day") traces back to Cornell's Food and Brand Lab (Wansink &
  Sobal) — a lab that later had **more than a dozen papers retracted** and was found to have
  used flawed/extrapolated methodology
  ([Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2006/12/mindless-autopilot-drives-people-underestimate-food-decisions),
  [PMC methodological critique](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7050813/)).
  **This number should not be repeated as verified fact** — the underlying qualitative
  point (we make far more small decisions than we're aware of) is plausible, but the
  specific figure is not reliable.

**Bottom line:** overload doesn't need to be dramatic to work — routine notification volume
and endless-feed design are enough to keep System 2 chronically under-resourced, which is
precisely the state in which peripheral-route persuasion cues (scarcity, social proof,
authority, repetition) do their heaviest lifting.
