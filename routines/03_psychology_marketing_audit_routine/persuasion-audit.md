## Claim 1: Decision-making is shaped by childhood, parenting, trauma, ideology, and society

**Finding.** The CDC-Kaiser Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study (Felitti et al., 1998, *American Journal of Preventive Medicine* 14(4):245–258) surveyed over 17,000 adult HMO patients and found a graded relationship between the number of adverse childhood experiences (abuse, neglect, household dysfunction) and adult health and behavioral outcomes, including substance use and depression. This is a tier-2 peer-reviewed source with a large sample.

Separately, decades of social-psychology research (Bowlby and Ainsworth on attachment; Asch on conformity; Milgram on obedience to authority) establish that early relationships and social context shape adult decision patterns, though several classic obedience and conformity findings have themselves faced replication and ethics scrutiny in recent decades and should not be treated as unqualified law.

**Funding and conflict of interest.** The ACE study was funded by the US Department of Health and Human Services, the CDC Foundation, and the Kaiser Permanente Garfield Memorial Fund — a government body plus the HMO whose patients were studied. This is a disclosed, government/nonprofit funding structure, not an industry-funded persuasion study, though Kaiser has an institutional interest in demonstrating the value of preventive screening within its own system.

**Alternative interpretation.** A 2019 critical review, "The Relation Between Adverse Childhood Experiences and Adult Health: Turning Gold into Lead" (PMC6220625), argues the widely used aggregate "ACE score" oversimplifies a complex, non-linear relationship and risks deterministic messaging ("your childhood determines your future") that the original data do not support. Ideology's role specifically (via frameworks like Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory) is more contested at the theory level: critics note MFT is not grounded in an explicit theory of cooperation and its list of "foundations" is somewhat ad hoc (Behavioral Scientist critique; Wikipedia summary of the academic debate). This session's search did not locate a documented funding conflict specific to Moral Foundations Theory; that absence is reported as "not found," not as "no conflict exists."

## Claim 2: What billions in ad spend are actually buying

**Finding.** Two bodies of evidence point in different directions. Blake, Nosko and Tadelis (2015, *Econometrica* 83(1):155–174), using a large-scale randomized field experiment on eBay, found that because ad clicks and purchase intent are correlated, non-experimental estimates overstate paid-search returns; branded keyword ads in particular showed no measurable short-term benefit, and average returns for frequent users were negative once ad spend was accounted for. This is an independently funded, peer-reviewed field experiment (tier 2).

By contrast, the IPA Effectiveness Databank, underlying Les Binet and Peter Field's widely cited "60/40" rule (60% brand-building, 40% short-term activation, from "The Long and the Short of It," 2013, and refined in "Effectiveness in Context," 2018) is built from roughly 1,000–1,500 campaign case studies submitted to the IPA's own effectiveness awards.

**Funding and conflict of interest.** The IPA is the UK advertising industry's own trade association; its databank is composed of award-entry case studies submitted by agencies with an incentive to report success, and Binet and Field's work is published through the IPA itself. Similarly, the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science, the most-cited academic source for "how brands grow" claims, is funded by corporate sponsorships starting at USD $70,000/year from more than 60 companies including PepsiCo, Nestlé, Unilever, Procter & Gamble, and Mars — most of its research findings are shared exclusively with paying sponsors before wider release. By the sourcing hierarchy, both are tier 5 (industry-association publications), useful for describing industry consensus but not as neutral, independent verification that advertising spend delivers the returns claimed.

**Alternative interpretation.** The eBay field experiment is the strongest independently funded counter-evidence: it demonstrates that at least one major, well-measured category of ad spend (branded search) can have close to zero causal effect despite looking effective in the uncontrolled data most marketers use to justify budgets. The honest synthesis is that advertising effectiveness evidence is dominated by industry-funded sources, and the few independently funded, randomized field experiments available suggest real effects are frequently much smaller than industry figures imply — this is a genuinely contested area, not one where either side should be reported as settled.

## Claim 3: Is preferring the low-effort option an evolved trait?

**Finding.** Petty and Cacioppo's Elaboration Likelihood Model (1986) is a well-replicated dual-process framework: people are "cognitive misers" who default to low-effort, peripheral-cue-based judgment unless they have both the motivation and the cognitive capacity for effortful central-route processing. This is consistent with a much older observation, Zipf's "principle of least effort" (1949), and with Kahneman's System 1/System 2 framework — all pointing to effort-minimization as a stable, broadly evidenced feature of human cognition, plausibly consistent with evolved resource-conservation pressures, though "evolved" causal claims are harder to test directly than the behavioral pattern itself.

**Funding and conflict of interest.** No commercial funding conflict was identified for the ELM or Zipf's foundational work; these are long-standing academic psychology and linguistics findings, not industry-commissioned studies.

**Alternative interpretation — the cautionary case.** A related and frequently cited claim, "ego depletion" (that willpower is a finite resource that gets used up, Baumeister et al. 1998), illustrates why "evolved trait" claims about effort and self-control need scrutiny before publication. A 2016 multilab preregistered replication (Hagger et al., 23 laboratories, over 2,000 participants) found no evidence of the depletion effect, despite 22 of 23 labs predicting beforehand that they would replicate it. Follow-up commentary (PMC4987495, PMC5329006) argues the replication's specific manipulation may have been too weak to invoke real depletion in all participants, so the debate is not fully closed — but the episode is a documented instance of a popular, "obviously evolutionary" self-control claim collapsing under preregistered multilab testing. The lesson generalizes: claims about low-effort preference being a fixed evolved mechanism should be held to the same preregistration and replication standard, and most have not yet been tested at that scale.

## Claim 4: Why cognitive overload makes people easier to persuade

**Finding.** The Elaboration Likelihood Model's central mechanism directly predicts this: when cognitive resources are scarce (overload, time pressure, distraction), people shift from scrutinizing arguments to relying on peripheral cues (source attractiveness, message length, social proof, authority signals) — a mechanism, not merely a correlation, that is well established in the persuasion literature (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986, and subsequent decades of replication).

Documented modern sources of overload include: information volume (Bawden & Robinson, "The Dark Side of Information," and their 2020 "Information Overload: An Overview," Oxford Encyclopedia of Political Decision Making — the information-overload research literature is decades old and largely academic, not industry-funded); and platform design choices with a commercial engagement motive — the 2021 Wall Street Journal "Facebook Files," based on documents disclosed by whistleblower Frances Haugen, showed Facebook's 2017 ranking change weighted "angry" reactions as five times a "like," and that internal researchers warned this could increase "proportionately higher amounts of controversial content," a change Facebook nonetheless kept in place for a period after being warned. That is a documented internal mechanism (leaked primary company records, tier 4), not an inference of hidden intent beyond what those documents state.

**Funding and conflict of interest.** Bawden and Robinson's information-overload research is independent academic work (no industry funding identified). The Facebook Files evidence is adversarial to the company whose behavior it describes — Meta did not commission or endorse this disclosure, which strengthens rather than weakens its evidentiary value, though it should be read as one whistleblower's document set, not an audited third-party review.

**Alternative interpretation.** The widely repeated statistics that "average attention span fell from 2.5 minutes to 47 seconds" and that "adults make 35,000 decisions a day" are treated here as **unverified**: this session's search traced them mainly to secondary statistics-aggregator pages rather than a single, clearly identified peer-reviewed original. Gloria Mark's (UC Irvine) published attention-span research is the more credible underlying academic source and should be checked directly rather than relying on the popularized numbers before republishing them as fact. Similarly, the judicial "decision fatigue" finding (Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso, 2011, *PNAS*) — often cited as proof that overload degrades real-world high-stakes decisions — has a documented, substantive rebuttal: Glöckner's (2016) simulation argues the reported effect size is inflated by a statistical artifact related to how favorable and unfavorable cases are ordered and how long each type of ruling takes to process, not necessarily mental depletion. The original authors partially rebutted this critique but conceded the residual effect size is smaller and contested. This is a genuinely open empirical question, not a settled one.

## Micro-blog post (for publication)

# What Actually Moves Your Decisions: Childhood, Ad Spend, and a Tired Brain

Why do people choose the easy option, fall for ads that don't work, and get persuaded more easily when overwhelmed? The evidence is stronger in some places than others — and some of the internet's favorite "facts" here do not hold up.

Childhood adversity does correlate with adult health and behavior: the CDC-Kaiser ACE Study (Felitti et al., 1998, *Am J Prev Med*, n>17,000) is solid, government-and-nonprofit-funded evidence, though critics warn the popular "ACE score" oversimplifies a genuinely complex picture (PMC6220625).

On advertising: the industry's own most-cited effectiveness data — the IPA Databank behind the "60/40" brand-building rule, and the corporate-sponsored Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (funded by Pepsi, Unilever, P&G and 60-plus other sponsors) — is not neutral. The strongest independent evidence, a randomized eBay field experiment (Blake, Nosko & Tadelis, 2015, *Econometrica*), found branded search ads produced close to zero measurable extra sales. A lot of ad spend appears to buy far less than the industry that sells it claims.

Preferring low-effort choices is a durable, well-tested pattern (Petty & Cacioppo's Elaboration Likelihood Model, 1986) — but be wary of adjacent "willpower is a depleting resource" claims: a 23-lab, 2,000-plus-participant preregistered replication (Hagger et al., 2016) found no ego-depletion effect at all.

Overload does make people easier to persuade, by pushing judgment onto mental shortcuts instead of scrutiny — and Facebook's own internally leaked research (2021 "Facebook Files") shows engagement-ranking choices that its own researchers warned amplified anger. But the popular "47-second attention span" and "35,000 decisions a day" statistics are unverified in this search and should not be repeated as settled fact.
