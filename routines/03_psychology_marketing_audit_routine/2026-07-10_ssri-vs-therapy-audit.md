# News Item: Do SSRIs Actually Work As Well As Therapy For Depression?

**Date:** 2026-07-10
**Category:** Mental Health Treatment / Research Audit
**Type:** Claim audit (micro-blog)

---

## The claim

"SSRIs are about as effective as psychotherapy (CBT) for depression, so medication should usually be the first-line treatment" — a claim repeated by prescribers, pharma marketing, and mental-health apps that steer users toward medication.

## What the primary research says

[Cuijpers et al. 2013, *World Psychiatry*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wps.20038) — a meta-analysis of direct head-to-head trials — found psychotherapy and antidepressants **roughly equally effective** in the short term overall, though SSRIs specifically showed a small edge over psychotherapy (tricyclic antidepressants did not). The larger [Cuijpers et al. 2020 network meta-analysis, *World Psychiatry*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wps.20701) confirmed short-term parity but found **psychotherapy's benefits last longer**, and that combining both beats either alone. A 2024 individual-participant-data meta-analysis of interpersonal psychotherapy vs. medication reached a similar "comparable" conclusion.

## Funding and conflicts of interest

Funding bias cuts **both ways** here, which is often left out of the popular version of this claim:

- [Turner et al. 2008, *NEJM*](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa065779) showed that FDA trial data on antidepressants was selectively published, inflating the apparent drug effect size by **~32%** across the drug class — meaning the medication side of these comparisons draws on a literature that overstates drug efficacy.
- A Cochrane-affiliated methodology review ([Lundh et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28207928/)) found industry-sponsored drug trials are roughly **4x more likely** (odds ratio 4.05) to report sponsor-favorable outcomes than independently funded trials.
- Less discussed: a [systematic review of NIH-funded psychotherapy trials](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4589340/) found publication bias **also inflates the apparent efficacy of psychotherapy** for major depressive disorder — this is not a pharma-only problem.
- The Cuijpers meta-analyses themselves come from a university researcher (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam) with no disclosed drug-industry funding, which limits — but doesn't eliminate — bias in the "roughly equal" conclusion, since the underlying drug trials it draws on are still mostly industry-run.

## Independent reanalysis / opposing angle

Because publication bias has been independently documented on **both** the drug-trial side (Turner 2008) and the psychotherapy-trial side (NIH-funded review above), any claim of clean parity between the two treatments is likely built on two separately inflated literatures rather than one unbiased comparison. The most robust finding across sources is not "they're equal" but that **combined treatment outperforms either alone**, and that psychotherapy's advantage grows over time — a nuance usually dropped from "just take the pill" messaging.

## Verdict

**Contested, not settled.** Short-term outcomes are genuinely close between SSRIs and CBT, but published effect sizes on both sides are probably each somewhat inflated by selective publication, so "equally effective" may really mean "similarly overstated." Longer-term, therapy holds up better on its own, and combined treatment beats either alone regardless of time horizon. Anyone told medication should simply replace therapy — or vice versa — is hearing simplified marketing, not the actual state of the evidence.

## Appendix: four open questions on decision-making, marketing, and cognitive overload

### 1. How do we actually make decisions? (childhood, parents, education, trauma, ideology, society)

Covered in depth in the prior audit in this routine (`2026-07-07_antidepressants-vs-placebo-audit.md`). In short: most decisions run on fast, intuitive, pattern-matched processing (Kahneman's "System 1"), shaped early by attachment style and parental modeling, narrowed by education into which *kind* of default reasoning gets used, biased toward threat-avoidance by trauma (via lasting HPA-axis/amygdala reactivity), and boxed into a socially acceptable menu of options by ideology and community. Deliberate, "System 2" reasoning mostly justifies a choice after the fact rather than generating it.

### 2. Marketers spend billions globally — what are they actually optimizing for?

Also covered previously: not rational persuasion, but **repeated low-effort exposure that sits below the threshold of scrutiny** — mere-exposure and brand-salience effects (Byron Sharp's "mental availability" research; [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/prof-byron-sharp-skewers-binet-tells-marketers-to-sack-agencies-preaching-share-of-voice)) show that familiarity and emotional association, not argument quality, drive purchase behavior. Binet & Field's long-running effectiveness studies found emotional brand-building advertising outperforms rational/informational campaigns for long-term growth, which is why so much ad spend looks "wasteful" if judged purely as information transfer — it isn't trying to inform, it's trying to become familiar.

### 3. Why do humans gravitate toward low-effort, easy options — evolution or something else?

This is well-supported by cognitive-psychology research, not just folk evolutionary story-telling. Kool & Botvinick's experiments (["Decision Making and the Avoidance of Cognitive Demand"](https://www.wouterkool.com/papers/KoolEtAl_2010_JEPG.pdf)) demonstrated a consistent, measurable bias toward the less mentally demanding of two equally rewarding options — dubbed the "law of least mental effort." The "cognitive miser" framing (Fiske & Taylor, 1980s) holds that brains default to automatic processing and only escalate to effortful, controlled processing when a task is novel, high-stakes, or when the automatic route visibly fails. The evolutionary logic — that unnecessary mental exertion was metabolically costly and selection favored organisms that conserved it for when it mattered — is a plausible account of *why* this bias exists, but the effort-avoidance bias itself is directly measured in the lab (Kool & Botvinick, and later work), not merely inferred. Some newer work (["Challenging the Law of Least Effort"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352250X24000940)) qualifies the finding — people do sometimes choose the harder path when it signals competence or is intrinsically engaging — so it's a strong default, not an absolute law.

### 4. Why are we easier to convince when the brain is overloaded — and what's overloading it now?

This is one of the best-replicated findings in social psychology. Petty & Cacioppo's Elaboration Likelihood Model shows persuasion runs through two routes: a "central" route (careful evaluation of arguments) used when people have both the *motivation* and *cognitive resources* to think it through, and a "peripheral" route (quick heuristics — "the speaker seems credible," "everyone else is doing it") that takes over when either is missing. Cognitive load — not enough spare mental bandwidth — pushes people from the central route to the peripheral one, which is exactly where persuasion by cues (authority, scarcity, social proof) rather than substance works best. Separately, research on decision fatigue and information overload documents an inverted-U relationship between information load and decision quality: performance rises with more information up to a point, then degrades, with people falling back on defaults, heuristics, and impulsive choices as load increases.

Current major sources of that overload are well documented: continuous notification streams and the attention economy's design for interruption; the sheer volume of choices and information in digital environments (choice overload); accumulated decision fatigue from the hundreds of small daily decisions modern life requires; and multitasking/context-switching, which keeps working memory chronically taxed rather than freed up for deliberate evaluation. In short — persuasion attempts (advertising, scams, high-pressure sales, political messaging) are frequently timed or designed to catch people in exactly these overloaded states, when the brain has already defaulted to the peripheral route.

*(This appendix synthesizes well-established psychology and behavioral-science frameworks — dual-process theory, cognitive-miser research, the Elaboration Likelihood Model, and cognitive-load/decision-fatigue research — rather than auditing one single study, and is not held to the same single-claim sourcing standard as the treatment-comparison audit above.)*
