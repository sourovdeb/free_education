# Claim Audit: Does "Power Posing" Change Your Hormones and Behavior?

**Claim:** Standing in an expansive "power pose" for two minutes raises testosterone, lowers cortisol, and makes you more risk-tolerant and confident — the basis of Amy Cuddy's TED talk (one of the most-viewed ever) and her bestselling book *Presence*, and a staple of corporate coaching and sales-training decks ever since.

**The source:** Carney, Cuddy & Yap, "Power Posing," *Psychological Science* (2010). Sample: **N = 42** (~21 per condition) — small enough that any true effect estimate is noisy and vulnerable to false positives.

**Funding:** The original work was backed by an NSF CAREER grant (#1056194) to Dana Carney — public science funding, not pharma or corporate money, so no direct funder conflict. The real conflict emerged *after* publication: Cuddy's TED talk (~47M views) and book made the effect personally and commercially valuable to defend, independent of the data.

**Does it hold up?** No, mostly. Ranehill et al. (2015, *Psych Science*, N=200) replicated the *feeling* of power but found zero hormonal or behavioral effects. Simmons & Simonsohn's 2017 p-curve of 34 studies found no evidential value beyond that subjective feeling. Co-author Dana Carney published a 2016 statement saying "I do not believe that 'power pose effects' are real." Cuddy's own 2018 reply (55 studies) still could only support the felt-power effect, not the hormone/behavior claims.

**Verdict:** Contested and largely falsified for the marketed claim (testosterone, cortisol, risk-taking). The one piece that survives — posing makes you *feel* more powerful — is a modest, ordinary psychological effect, not the neuroendocrine story sold in keynotes.
