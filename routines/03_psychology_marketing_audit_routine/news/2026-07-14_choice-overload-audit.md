# The Jam Study Isn't What You Think: Auditing "Choice Overload"

*News item — 2026-07-14*

Marketers routinely cite a single 2000 grocery-store experiment to justify shrinking product menus, checkout options, and subscription tiers: Iyengar & Lepper's "jam study." Shoppers who saw 6 jam flavors bought roughly 10x more often than those who saw 24 (about 30% vs. 3% of the ~249 people who stopped at the tasting table). It's one of the most-cited findings in consumer psychology, and the founding anecdote of Barry Schwartz's *The Paradox of Choice*.

Does "more options kill sales" actually hold up? A 2010 meta-analysis of 50 studies and over 5,000 participants (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, *Journal of Consumer Research*) found the average effect of assortment size on choice was essentially zero — some studies even found more options *helped*. A 2015 follow-up (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 99 observations) found the effect reappears reliably only when several conditions stack together: complex, hard-to-compare options, uncertain preferences, and a goal of minimizing effort.

**Verdict: real, but narrow.** No pharmaceutical or corporate funding was found behind any of the three papers — all three are university-affiliated (Stanford/Columbia, Basel/Mannheim/Indiana, Northwestern) — so this isn't a funding-bias story. It's a *vividness bias* story: one small, dramatic single-store result (n≈249) became gospel, while two much larger disconfirming meta-analyses get cited far less in marketing decks than the jam.

**Sources:**
- Iyengar & Lepper (2000), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 79(6), 995–1006.
- Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37(3), 409–425.
- Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015), *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 25(2), 333–358.
