# Research Notes — Decision-Making, Marketing Spend, Effort-Seeking, Overload
*2026-07-14 — supporting notes for this cycle's audit, not a single-claim audit itself*

These four questions are broader explainers, not one verified claim each — treat
them as a literature synthesis with citations, not an adversarially-checked audit
like the claim above. Two independent sources are given per topic where possible.

## 8. How do we actually make a decision? (childhood, parents, education, trauma, ideology, society)

Decisions are mostly System 1 pattern-matching, checked (sometimes) by slower
System 2 reasoning (Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011). What populates
System 1's defaults is built early and socially:

- **Childhood attachment** (Bowlby; Ainsworth's Strange Situation studies) calibrates
  a lifelong baseline for trust, risk-tolerance, and how threats are read.
- **Parents/social learning** (Bandura, Bobo doll studies, 1961) — children adopt
  decision heuristics by observing modeled behavior, not just instruction.
- **Trauma** — the ACE Study (Felitti et al. 1998, *American Journal of Preventive
  Medicine*, ~17,000 Kaiser patients) links adverse childhood experiences to
  altered stress response and higher present-bias/impulsivity in adulthood.
- **Ideology/society** — Moral Foundations Theory (Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*,
  2012) and cultural cognition research (Kahan et al.) argue political/ideological
  reasoning is intuition-first: identity picks the conclusion, reasoning supplies
  the justification afterward.
- **Education/development** — prefrontal cortex (deliberate control) matures later
  than the limbic system (Steinberg, 2008, "A Social Neuroscience Perspective on
  Adolescent Risk-Taking"), so impulse control capacity genuinely differs by age.

Net: a decision is the output of fast intuitive priors laid down by attachment,
modeling, and trauma, filtered through cultural/ideological identity, checked by
a slow deliberate system whose bandwidth is itself limited (see §11).

## 9. What are marketers actually buying with billions in ad spend?

Not rational persuasion — attention capture, habit formation, and heuristic
triggers that bypass deliberate evaluation:

- **Cialdini's influence principles** (*Influence*, 1984/2021: reciprocity, social
  proof, authority, scarcity, commitment, liking) are shortcuts, not arguments.
- **Habit loops** (Duhigg, *The Power of Habit*, 2012) — cue → routine → reward —
  are the actual target; once a purchase is habitual it no longer needs a reason
  each time.
- **Brand as a heuristic**: a trusted brand reduces the cost of evaluating a
  product, so people "buy the brand," not the product spec sheet.
- Purchases that feel like "spending for no reason" are usually driven by
  identity/emotion/social signaling, then rationalized after the fact — the
  confabulation effect documented by Nisbett & Wilson (1977, "Telling More Than
  We Can Know," *Psychological Review*): people confidently report reasons for
  choices that demonstrably were not the actual cause.

## 10. Why do humans default to low-effort options — evolution or something else?

Both, and they reinforce each other:

- **Cognitive miser model** (Fiske & Taylor, *Social Cognition*) and Kahneman's
  "law of least effort": System 2 (deliberate) is metabolically expensive, so the
  brain defaults to System 1 (heuristic) whenever possible.
- **Metabolic argument**: the brain consumes ~20% of resting energy at ~2% of
  body mass (Raichle & Gusnard, 2002, *PNAS*), so under ancestral caloric scarcity,
  minimizing unnecessary deliberate cognition was adaptive, not lazy.
- Modern environments (one-click purchase, autoplay, algorithmic feeds) are
  designed to reward that same low-effort circuitry, which is why it now looks
  like "laziness" rather than the resource-allocation strategy it evolved as.

## 11. Why is an overloaded brain easier to persuade — and what's overloading us now?

- **Cognitive load theory** (Sweller, 1988) and working-memory capacity limits
  (Cowan, 2001, ~4±1 chunks) mean a saturated System 2 has no spare capacity to
  scrutinize a claim, so System 1 defaults (including persuasion cues) win by
  default — this is the mechanism, not "willpower running out."
- Caveat: the older popular mechanism, **ego depletion** (Baumeister et al., 1998),
  did *not* survive its largest test — a 23-lab registered replication (Hagger et
  al., 2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) found no reliable depletion
  effect. So "decision fatigue" as a depletable willpower fuel is itself a
  contested claim; cognitive-load/attention-capacity is the better-supported
  account today.
- **Current overload sources**: constant notification interruption, infinite-scroll
  feeds engineered against natural stopping cues, habitual task-switching costs,
  rising information volume ("infobesity"), always-on work culture, and — note
  the irony — choice proliferation itself, the same mechanism audited above.
