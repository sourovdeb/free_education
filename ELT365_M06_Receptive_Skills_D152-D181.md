### D152 · What Are Receptive Skills?
**Idea —** Reading and listening are receptive skills — learners receive meaning without producing language. That doesn't make them passive; comprehension requires active mental work.
**Example —** A learner listens to a news report: they hear words, infer meaning from context, and construct understanding. None of that happens automatically.
**Try —** List three things a learner must do mentally to understand a short spoken text. Compare your list with a partner.

---

### D153 · Top-Down Processing
**Idea —** Top-down processing means using existing knowledge — of topics, genres, and the world — to predict and interpret incoming language. Schemata drive it.
**Example —** The title 'A Recipe for Disaster' activates cooking knowledge and idiom knowledge simultaneously. Both help before a single word is read.
**Try —** Give students a title only. Ask: what will this be about? Who wrote it? Who reads it? Then compare predictions with the actual text.

---

### D154 · Bottom-Up Processing
**Idea —** Bottom-up processing builds meaning word by word, sound by sound — decoding the signal before constructing sense. It dominates when language is unfamiliar.
**Example —** A learner meets an unknown phrase: they segment sounds, identify morphemes, then attempt a meaning. They work from signal to sense.
**Try —** Give learners a transcript with five words blanked. Ask them to predict each missing word using surrounding grammar and vocabulary alone.

---

### D155 · Why Gist Comes Before Detail
**Idea —** A gist task builds a global frame before the detail task fills it. Without that frame, learners try to process everything at once and process little.
**Example —** 'What is the speaker's main feeling?' anchors the listener. 'List five specific complaints' then has a framework to attach to.
**Try —** Play a short audio. Gist task: one-sentence summary. Pause, discuss. Play again for specific detail. Notice what changes on the second listen.

---

### D156 · Pre-Listening: Activating Schemata
**Idea —** A good pre-listening stage activates relevant knowledge and sets a purpose. Without it, the audio lands in a vacuum and comprehension drops.
**Example —** Before a podcast about remote work, ask: 'Do you prefer working from home or an office? Why?' The discussion primes vocabulary and topic.
**Try —** Design a two-minute pre-listening stage for a news report on climate change. Use one question — not a vocabulary list.

---

### D157 · Identifying Blocking Vocabulary
**Idea —** Blocking vocabulary is the small set of words that, if unknown, prevent comprehension entirely. Pre-teach only those — not every unfamiliar word.
**Example —** In a listening about a court case, 'verdict' may be blocking. 'Courtroom attire' probably isn't — context carries it.
**Try —** Read a text you plan to use. Circle every unknown word. Cross out all but the three that would truly block meaning. Pre-teach those three only.

---

### D158 · Designing a Gist Listening Task
**Idea —** A good gist task is one question, answerable after a single listen, that requires global understanding — not scanning for a specific detail.
**Example —** 'What does the speaker want the listener to do?' is gist. 'What time does the meeting start?' is a detail task in disguise.
**Try —** Write two gist tasks for any audio you use regularly. Test yourself: can each be answered without catching every word?

---

### D159 · Detailed Listening Tasks
**Idea —** Detailed tasks target specific information — facts, figures, sequences, names. They require a second or third listen and focus attention on precision.
**Example —** 'What three reasons does the speaker give?' demands close attention to sequencing and specific content, not just global meaning.
**Try —** Design a detailed task with exactly three target items for a two-minute audio. Write an answer key. Notice how precise your task language must be.

---

### D160 · Listening for Specific Information
**Idea —** Scanning in listening means ignoring most of the audio and catching only the target item — a number, a name, a date. It is a real-world skill.
**Example —** Catching a flight number from a departure announcement — everything else is noise. The skill is filtering, not processing the whole text.
**Try —** Play a weather forecast. Task one: find only temperatures. Task two: find only the days of the week. Notice how focus changes what you hear.

---

### D161 · Inference in Listening
**Idea —** Inferring means using what is said plus what is implied to reach a conclusion the speaker never stated. It is higher-order listening.
**Example —** 'She's been here since seven.' Nobody said she is tired — but the inference is available and likely intended.
**Try —** Write three statements that imply something without stating it. Ask learners what can be inferred. Discuss where each inference comes from.

---

### D162 · Using the Tapescript
**Idea —** A tapescript is a tool for reflection after listening, not a crutch during it. Showing it too early removes the listening task entirely.
**Example —** Play audio, set task, check answers, discuss difficulties — then show the tapescript. Learners now read it to confirm what they caught.
**Try —** Use a tapescript only after two listens and a full task cycle. Ask learners to underline the three hardest moments and explain why.

---

### D163 · Authentic Audio in Class
**Idea —** Authentic audio was made for real speakers. It exposes reduced speech, overlapping turns, and natural pace — all things graded recordings hide.
**Example —** A BBC Radio discussion moves fast, speakers interrupt, and words reduce. A coursebook audio says the same thing at two-thirds the speed.
**Try —** Choose one authentic clip of 90 seconds. Set a single achievable gist task. Reflect: what made it harder than graded audio, and was that useful?

---

### D164 · Grading Listening by Task, Not Text
**Idea —** You can grade a listening task without changing the audio — by adjusting task complexity, number of listens, and the support provided before and after.
**Example —** A C1 class and an A2 class hear the same clip. The C1 task asks for implied attitude; the A2 task asks for a single fact.
**Try —** Take one authentic clip. Write three tasks — easy, medium, hard — for the same recording. What makes each one harder?

---

### D165 · Listening Sub-Skills
**Idea —** Listening is not one skill but a cluster: global comprehension, local detail, inference, deduction, attitude recognition. Good tasks target one sub-skill explicitly.
**Example —** 'Understanding the speaker's attitude' is a sub-skill. Teaching it means tasks that require attitude recognition — not just fact retrieval.
**Try —** Choose a recording you use. Identify which sub-skill your usual task targets. Write a second task targeting a different sub-skill from the same audio.

---

### D166 · Reading: Scanning
**Idea —** Scanning means moving quickly over a text to find specific information without reading everything. It is purposeful and selective.
**Example —** Finding a name in a phone book or a price on a menu — the reader isn't reading; they are searching.
**Try —** Give learners a 400-word article and ask them to find five specific facts in 60 seconds. Time it. Discuss how their eyes moved.

---

### D167 · Reading: Skimming
**Idea —** Skimming means reading quickly for a general impression — what the text is about, its tone, its structure. Readers skip freely and skip often.
**Example —** Reading the first sentence of each paragraph to decide if an article is worth reading fully — that is skimming in action.
**Try —** Give a 500-word text. Task: read it in 90 seconds and write one sentence summarising it. No looking back. Discuss what was caught and what wasn't.

---

### D168 · Reading Intensively
**Idea —** Intensive reading means reading closely for accurate understanding of meaning, language, and implication. It is slow, deliberate, and cognitively demanding.
**Example —** Reading a contract clause or a grammar explanation — every word has weight and must be processed precisely.
**Try —** Give a 200-word paragraph. Ask learners to underline the single most important sentence, then justify the choice in one sentence. Discuss disagreements.

---

### D169 · Reading Extensively
**Idea —** Extensive reading means reading large quantities at or below learner level, for pleasure and fluency — not for close language study.
**Example —** A B1 learner reads a graded reader for the story, not to study the language. Volume and enjoyment are the goals.
**Try —** Ask learners to read one page of anything in English outside class this week. Report back: what did they notice, enjoy, or struggle with?

---

### D170 · Schema Theory in Reading
**Idea —** Schema theory holds that understanding depends on prior knowledge structures. A reader with relevant schemata comprehends faster and more accurately.
**Example —** A text about cricket is harder for a reader with no cricket schema, even if the vocabulary is simple. Knowledge drives reading, not just language.
**Try —** Choose a culturally specific text. Activate the schema first with a two-minute discussion. Compare comprehension with a version given cold.

---

### D171 · Text Types and Genre
**Idea —** Genre shapes reader expectations. Knowing a text is an editorial, a recipe, or a letter changes how readers process it from the first line.
**Example —** 'Dear Editor' activates different expectations than 'Method: combine the flour and butter.' Genre tells readers what is coming.
**Try —** Give three short texts with the headings removed. Ask learners to identify the genre from language and layout alone. Discuss the clues they used.

---

### D172 · Designing Pre-Reading Tasks
**Idea —** Pre-reading warms up the topic, activates vocabulary, and gives readers a reason to read. It should predict content, not pre-teach or spoil it.
**Example —** 'Look at the headline. What do you think happened? Who wrote this?' — purpose and prediction, not vocabulary explanation.
**Try —** Design a pre-reading stage for a text you use. Write two questions that activate knowledge without revealing content.

---

### D173 · Gist Tasks for Reading
**Idea —** A gist reading task asks one global question answerable after a single read — it trains learners to resist processing every word first.
**Example —** 'Is this text for or against the proposal?' is gist. 'How many people were affected?' is a detail task.
**Try —** Take a reading text you use. Write one genuine gist question. Check: could a learner answer it without reading the final paragraph?

---

### D174 · Detailed Reading Tasks
**Idea —** Detailed reading tasks require learners to locate, understand, and often infer specific information. They follow gist — they never precede it.
**Example —** 'Find three reasons the author believes public transport is underfunded' requires close reading, not just a general impression.
**Try —** Write five comprehension questions for a text. Classify each: gist, specific detail, or inference. Ensure at least one falls in each category.

---

### D175 · Inferring Vocabulary from Context
**Idea —** When learners meet unknown words, context is the first tool — using the surrounding sentence and paragraph to infer likely meaning.
**Example —** 'The price was exorbitant — she put it back immediately.' 'Exorbitant' can be inferred as 'too high' from the consequence alone.
**Try —** Mark five unknown words in a text. Cover definitions. Ask learners to guess meaning from context, then state the evidence they used.

---

### D176 · Reading Circles
**Idea —** Reading circles assign roles — summariser, questioner, connector, vocabulary detective — so every learner processes the same text differently and shares findings.
**Example —** The questioner writes three questions; the connector links to real life; the vocabulary detective finds three words worth knowing. All discuss.
**Try —** Assign roles for a text next lesson. Debrief: which role forced the deepest engagement?

---

### D177 · Jigsaw Reading
**Idea —** In jigsaw reading, different groups read different texts, then regroup to share information. Nobody has the full picture until everyone contributes.
**Example —** Three groups read three articles on the same topic, then form mixed groups of three to piece the full story together.
**Try —** Split a long text into three topic-based sections. Assign one per group. What information must learners get from each other to complete the task?

---

### D178 · Authentic vs. Graded Texts
**Idea —** Authentic texts carry real language — irregular syntax, colloquial lexis, cultural assumptions. Graded texts are written to level. Both have a place.
**Example —** A menu is authentic and often manageable at A2. A magazine feature may be authentic and genuinely C1. The label doesn't determine difficulty.
**Try —** Find one authentic text at your learners' level. What makes it manageable? What scaffolding would you add before, during, or after?

---

### D179 · Reading into Speaking
**Idea —** Integrating reading with speaking gives readers a real purpose — they read to have something to say. It is the most natural reading follow-up.
**Example —** 'Read the article. Then tell your partner: do you agree with the writer? Give one reason.' The reading produces a conversation.
**Try —** Take a reading text. Write one speaking task that follows from it naturally. Make sure it requires an opinion, not just information recall.

---

### D180 · Selecting a Listening or Reading Text
**Idea —** Text selection criteria: appropriate length and complexity for the level, genuine interest value, enough richness for a task. The best text sparks disagreement.
**Example —** A text that makes learners want to argue with the author — or with each other — is more valuable than a neutral, sanitised one.
**Try —** Choose your next listening or reading text by asking: will students have something to say about this after? If not, find a different text.
