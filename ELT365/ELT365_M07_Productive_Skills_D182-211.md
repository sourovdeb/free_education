### D182 · What Productive Skills Are
**Idea —** Productive skills — speaking and writing — require learners to generate language, not just receive it. They are demanding because accuracy, fluency, and appropriacy must align at the same moment.
**Example —** A learner who understands a grammar rule perfectly in a reading exercise may still fail to use it in spontaneous speech — production is a separate skill.
**Try —** Ask learners: which is harder for you — speaking or writing in English, and why? Collect reasons. The answers reveal their current gaps.

---

### D183 · Fluency vs Accuracy: The Balancing Act
**Idea —** Fluency means communicating without excessive hesitation; accuracy means using language correctly. Teachers must choose which to prioritise in each task — correcting mid-flow kills fluency.
**Example —** A discussion task aims for fluency: save corrections for the feedback slot. A gap-fill aims for accuracy: correct immediately and explicitly.
**Try —** In your next speaking task, pick fluency or accuracy — not both. Design your correction strategy around that single goal before the task begins.

---

### D184 · Controlled Practice: The Purpose of the Cage
**Idea —** Controlled practice limits what learners can say to focus attention on one language point. It is not natural language, but it reduces cognitive load while a new form is being automatised.
**Example —** Drill: "Where were you born?" — "I was born in…" Learners cannot vary the structure. The repetition builds procedural memory for the form.
**Try —** Write one controlled practice exercise for a grammar point you are teaching this week. Check: is there only one correct answer per item? If not, it is not controlled.

---

### D185 · Freer Practice: When Learners Fly
**Idea —** Freer practice gives learners a communicative goal and allows any language to reach it. The target structure may appear but is not forced — fluency and meaning come first.
**Example —** After drilling "I used to…", learners interview a partner about childhood. The structure is likely but not mandatory — communication is the aim.
**Try —** Design a freer practice task where the target structure will naturally arise but is not compulsory. Test it: could a learner complete the task without using it?

---

### D186 · Drilling: Choral → Individual → Substitution
**Idea —** Drilling is repetition for pronunciation and automatisation. Move from choral (everyone together) to individual (one learner) to substitution (swap one element). Never drill before meaning is clear.
**Example —** Choral: "She's been working here for five years." Individual: spot-check four learners. Substitution: "She's been living in Paris for two years."
**Try —** Take one target sentence from your next lesson. Write a 3-step drill sequence. Practise delivering it; keep total drill time under two minutes.

---

### D187 · Information-Gap Activities: Making the Gap Real
**Idea —** An information gap works only when each learner has information the other genuinely lacks — not when both could look at the same sheet. The communicative need must be real, not simulated.
**Example —** Student A has a timetable with Tuesday blank; Student B has Tuesday but not Thursday. They must speak to complete both — neither can see the other's paper.
**Try —** Check your last information-gap activity: could either student have completed the task without speaking? If yes, close the gap — separate the information genuinely.

---

### D188 · Role-Play: Context, Cards, Correction Window
**Idea —** Role-plays succeed when learners know their character, situation, and goal before they begin. Context cards do this work. Correction happens after — never interrupt a role-play mid-scene.
**Example —** Card: "You are a customer. Your order arrived damaged. You want a replacement, not a refund." That is character, situation, and goal — nothing more.
**Try —** Write one role-play context card for a B1 class. Include: who you are, what the situation is, what you want. Keep it to three sentences.

---

### D189 · Discussion Tasks: Grading the Demand
**Idea —** Discussion tasks vary in cognitive and linguistic difficulty. Match the demand to the level: A2 needs concrete familiar topics with scaffolding; C1 can handle abstract contested ideas without support.
**Example —** A2: "What is your favourite food and why?" C1: "To what extent does diet reflect identity?" Same topic — completely different linguistic demand.
**Try —** Take a discussion question you use at one level. Write two versions — one two levels lower, one two levels higher. What changes in each?

---

### D190 · Monologue Tasks: Describe, Narrate, Argue
**Idea —** Monologue tasks — timed descriptions, personal narratives, mini-presentations — build fluency and the ability to sustain extended turns. Most classroom speaking is dialogic; monologue needs deliberate practice.
**Example —** "Describe a place you know well for one minute without stopping." No interruptions, no questions — sustained speech is the aim.
**Try —** Give learners a photo and 30 seconds to prepare a one-minute description. Record or peer-count the seconds of silence. Aim to reduce them.

---

### D191 · The Task-Based Speaking Cycle (Willis)
**Idea —** Willis's TBL cycle: pre-task (introduce topic, activate language) → task (meaning first) → planning and report (prepare to present) → language focus (reflect on what was used).
**Example —** Task: plan a surprise party on a budget. Learners use any language, then present their plan, then the teacher highlights emerging language patterns.
**Try —** Design a 15-minute TBL cycle for a B1 class. Which stage do you usually skip? Plan to include it this time.

---

### D192 · On-the-Spot Error Correction: When and How
**Idea —** Immediate correction suits accuracy-focused tasks — drills, written exercises. During fluency tasks, it breaks flow and discourages speaking. Decide the task aim first, then set the correction policy.
**Example —** During a grammar drill, correct immediately: "I go there yesterday → I went there yesterday." During a discussion, note the error; correct in the feedback slot.
**Try —** In your next class, announce your correction policy before the task begins: "I'll correct as we go" or "I'll note and we'll look after." Stick to it.

---

### D193 · Delayed Feedback: The Hot Correction Slot
**Idea —** Delayed feedback collects learner errors during a fluency task and addresses them immediately after — while the language is still warm. It protects flow and still targets accuracy.
**Example —** Teacher monitors a discussion, noting three recurring errors on a slip. After five minutes: "Someone said… What would you say instead?" — anonymous, reformulation-focused.
**Try —** In your next speaking task, note exactly three error types — not individual mistakes. Address them on the board at the end. Ask learners to correct; do not tell them.

---

### D194 · Process Writing: Four Stages Every Teacher Needs
**Idea —** Process writing treats writing as recursive, not linear: planning → drafting → revising → editing. Each stage is teachable, and each improves the final text more than a one-shot task does.
**Example —** Giving learners 40 minutes to "write an essay" skips planning, revision, and editing — three of the four stages that make writing improve.
**Try —** For your next writing task, dedicate 5 minutes to planning before learners write a word. Does their draft reflect their plan? Discuss where plans changed and why.

---

### D195 · Guided Writing: Models, Not Molds
**Idea —** A model text shows learners what the target genre looks like — its structure, register, and key phrases. Guided writing uses the model as a resource to analyse, not a template to copy.
**Example —** Learners analyse a formal email for greeting, purpose, request, and close. They then write their own on a different scenario — same structure, original content.
**Try —** Take a model text you use. Ask learners to label its stages before they write. Do they understand why each stage is there?

---

### D196 · Paragraph Structure: Topic Sentence First
**Idea —** A paragraph works when its topic sentence signals what follows and every subsequent sentence develops that one idea. Teaching this explicitly reduces the "list of sentences" problem in B1–B2 writing.
**Example —** Topic sentence: "Exercise improves sleep quality." Development: evidence, mechanism, personal relevance — all serving that one claim, not switching to a new topic.
**Try —** Give learners a paragraph with the topic sentence removed. Can they write it? Compare their version to the original. What differs?

---

### D197 · Cohesion Devices: The Linkers Learners Actually Use
**Idea —** Cohesion is achieved through reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, and lexical chains — not just "however" and "furthermore." Over-teaching connectors creates mechanical, unnatural writing.
**Example —** "She bought a coat. It was expensive." The pronoun creates cohesion — no connector needed. Many learners would write "In addition, the coat was expensive."
**Try —** Take a learner essay. Highlight every connector. Then highlight every pronoun used for reference. Which type of cohesion dominates? Which is absent?

---

### D198 · Peer Editing: What to Train Learners to Notice
**Idea —** Untrained peer editors either praise everything or correct spelling. Trained peer editors follow a checklist: Is the aim clear? Does each paragraph develop one idea? Does the ending follow from the body?
**Example —** A peer-editing checklist with three questions produces more useful feedback than "correct your partner's work" — which invites surface edits, not structural thinking.
**Try —** Write a 3-item peer-editing checklist for a writing task you assign regularly. Give it to learners before they write so they know what reviewers will look for.

---

### D199 · Teacher Feedback on Writing: Code, Don't Correct
**Idea —** Error-coded feedback (WO = word order, T = tense, VF = verb form) returns responsibility for correction to the learner. Research (Ferris) suggests it produces more durable learning than direct correction.
**Example —** Instead of crossing out "He go to school yesterday" and writing "went," underline it and write "T" in the margin. The learner identifies, explains, and corrects.
**Try —** Design a 6-code error key for the most common errors in your learners' writing. Use it on the next batch. Measure how many they self-correct correctly.

---

### D200 · Writing for Real Audiences: Authentic Tasks
**Idea —** Authentic writing tasks — real emails, review posts, complaint letters — motivate because the audience is real. The writing must work, not just be correct. This shifts the frame from accuracy to communication.
**Example —** Learners write a genuine review of a local café and post it. The public audience changes how they draft and proofread — far more than a teacher marking it ever does.
**Try —** Design one authentic writing task for your current class. Who is the real audience? Where will the text actually go? Make the purpose concrete.

---

### D201 · Genre Awareness: What the Text Type Demands
**Idea —** Different genres carry different expectations for structure, register, and purpose. Teaching learners to read genre before writing it reduces the "wrong shape" problem in exam and professional writing.
**Example —** A formal complaint letter and an informal email about the same problem use different openings, vocabulary, and tone — same facts, different genre conventions entirely.
**Try —** Collect three texts of the same genre (e.g. three formal emails). Ask learners to find three features they all share. Those features define the genre.

---

### D202 · Academic Writing at B2+: Hedging Language
**Idea —** Academic writing requires hedging — language that marks claims as tentative rather than absolute: "suggests," "appears to," "it is possible that." Over-claiming is a register error, not just style.
**Example —** "Exercise cures depression" is an over-claim. "Research suggests exercise may reduce symptoms of depression" hedges accurately and reflects the evidence.
**Try —** Give learners three absolute statements on a familiar topic. Ask them to rewrite each as a hedged academic claim using modal verbs or reporting verbs.

---

### D203 · Personal and Informal Writing: Register Markers
**Idea —** Informal register in writing shows through contractions, ellipsis, discourse markers ("anyway," "so"), and first-person directness. Teaching register means comparing formal and informal versions of the same content.
**Example —** Formal: "I am writing to enquire about the position advertised." Informal: "Just dropping you a line about that job you mentioned." Same move, opposite register.
**Try —** Take a formal sentence from a text. Ask learners to rewrite it informally. Then reverse: informal sentence goes formal. Discuss what changed and why.

---

### D204 · Email Writing: The Professional Register in Practice
**Idea —** Professional emails follow a predictable structure: greeting → purpose → body → request or next step → close. Teaching the moves explicitly saves learners from burying the request or omitting the purpose.
**Example —** Learners often write: "Hello, I hope you are well, I wanted to know about…" — the purpose arrives late. Teach: purpose in sentence one, always.
**Try —** Give learners an effective professional email. Ask them to label each move. Then give a scenario and ask them to write only the opening move — one sentence.

---

### D205 · Narrative Writing: Past Tenses in Context
**Idea —** Narrative writing requires coordinating past simple (sequence), past continuous (background), and past perfect (earlier events). Teaching these forms through narrative gives context isolated exercises cannot.
**Example —** "I was walking home (past cont.) when I saw (past simple) a wallet. Someone had dropped it (past perfect)." The three forms earn their place in the story.
**Try —** Give learners a four-panel picture sequence. Ask them to write the story using all three past forms. Collect: which form causes most errors?

---

### D206 · Opinion Essays: Beyond the Five-Paragraph Formula
**Idea —** The five-paragraph essay formula produces predictable, weak writing at B2+. Teaching learners to argue — claim, evidence, concession, rebuttal — produces stronger thinking and stronger prose.
**Example —** A body paragraph saying "Some people think X, but I disagree" is weaker than one that says "X holds in limited cases; however, when Y, it fails because…"
**Try —** Take a learner's opinion paragraph. Identify: is there a claim? Evidence? Concession? Rebuttal? Add whichever is missing in the next draft.

---

### D207 · Summary Writing: Selection and Compression
**Idea —** Summary writing requires selecting main ideas and compressing them — cutting examples, repetition, and hedging. It is a separate skill from both reading comprehension and extended writing.
**Example —** A learner who answers comprehension questions correctly on a text may still write a summary that lists details rather than synthesising the main argument.
**Try —** Take a 200-word paragraph. Ask learners to write a 40-word summary. Are they the right 40? Compare summaries in pairs.

---

### D208 · Dictation as a Productive Task: A Reappraisal
**Idea —** Dictation is often dismissed as old-fashioned, but it activates listening, spelling, punctuation, and grammatical knowledge simultaneously. Dictogloss makes it genuinely productive.
**Example —** In dictogloss, learners hear a short text twice, note key words only, then reconstruct the whole text in pairs. Their version need not be identical — accuracy and meaning are both assessed.
**Try —** Run a 60-word dictogloss next class. Read the text twice at natural speed. Give pairs 5 minutes to reconstruct. Compare versions on the board.

---

### D209 · Collaborative Writing: Group Tasks That Work
**Idea —** Collaborative writing tasks require genuine interdependence — each writer has a role (planner, drafter, editor) that the group needs. When any learner can write everything alone, collaboration is redundant.
**Example —** Jigsaw writing: Group A writes the introduction, Group B the argument, Group C the counterargument, Group D the conclusion. Each group reads only what precedes their section.
**Try —** Assign roles before your next group writing task: planner, drafter, editor. Keep the roles for the duration. Debrief: which role felt hardest?

---

### D210 · Pronunciation in Production: The Intelligibility Principle
**Idea —** The goal of pronunciation in production is intelligibility — being understood by the intended listener — not native-speaker accuracy. Many native-speaker features are unnecessary for communication (Levis).
**Example —** A learner who adds a vowel after final consonants is perfectly intelligible; targeting this error wastes time better spent on nuclear stress or weak forms.
**Try —** Listen to a learner recording. Identify one feature that causes a listener to mishear, and one that is simply "non-native." Address only the first.

---

### D211 · Designing a Productive-Skills Lesson from Scratch
**Idea —** A productive-skills lesson follows: lead-in → model and analyse → prepare → task → content feedback → language feedback. Skip language focus and learners repeat errors; skip content feedback and they feel unheard.
**Example —** A writing lesson that jumps from model to task skips the "prepare" stage — learners write without planning, which is the habit the lesson should break.
**Try —** Take a productive-skills lesson you teach regularly. Map it against the sequence above. Which stage is shortest? Extend it by two minutes next time.
