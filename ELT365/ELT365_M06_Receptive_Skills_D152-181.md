### D152 · What Receptive Skills Actually Are
**Idea —** Receptive skills — listening and reading — involve taking language in, not producing it. Learners receive far more than they produce, so these skills build the base for everything else.
**Example —** A beginner who watches one English sitcom episode daily builds an ear for rhythm and chunking long before they can name a grammar rule.
**Try —** Ask learners: last 24 hours — how much English did you hear or read? How much did you speak or write? Discuss.

---

### D153 · Top-Down Processing: Meaning Comes First
**Idea —** Top-down processing means using background knowledge, context, and expectation to interpret a text before analysing individual words. The brain predicts before it decodes.
**Example —** When you see a menu, you already know roughly what language to expect — prices, dish names, descriptions. That expectation speeds reading before a word is processed.
**Try —** Show learners a title and image only. Ask them to predict three words they will find in the text. Then check.

---

### D154 · Bottom-Up Processing: Sounds Build Meaning
**Idea —** Bottom-up processing starts with the signal — letters, phonemes, words — and builds up to meaning. It is essential when context is unclear or vocabulary is unknown.
**Example —** Learners decoding a medical form must process every word carefully; background knowledge about health does not compensate for unknown technical terms.
**Try —** Read a short sentence aloud very fast, then at natural speed, then slowly. Ask learners what they caught each time. Compare — this shows the processing load shift.

---

### D155 · Gist Listening: The One-Question Rule
**Idea —** A gist task asks one broad question answered by the whole text. Its purpose is to give listening a reason before the detail work begins — not to test memory.
**Example —** "What is the general topic of the conversation?" beats six true/false questions on a first listen; learners cannot attend to everything simultaneously.
**Try —** Set one gist question, play an audio clip, take hands-up answers. Resist replaying until the gist is agreed.

---

### D156 · Scanning: Purposeful Speed in Reading
**Idea —** Scanning means moving quickly over a text to locate specific information — a date, a name, a price — without reading everything. It is purposeful speed, not careless reading.
**Example —** Finding a departure time on a timetable: eyes skip the irrelevant columns and land on the one that answers the question.
**Try —** Give learners a short article and five specific-information questions. Time them at 90 seconds. Scanning improves with repeated timed practice.

---

### D157 · Skimming: Getting the Gist in Print
**Idea —** Skimming means reading quickly for overall meaning — not every word. Readers use titles, headings, first and last sentences, and key words to build a mental outline.
**Example —** Before buying a book, most people read the back cover, a chapter title, and the first paragraph. That is skimming in action.
**Try —** Give learners a one-page article. Allow 60 seconds. Ask: what is it about, and what is the writer's main point? Check accuracy.

---

### D158 · Pre-Teaching Vocabulary: What to Select
**Idea —** Pre-teach only the words that block comprehension of the task, not every unknown word. Over-pre-teaching removes the productive struggle that builds incidental vocabulary.
**Example —** For a gist task on climate change, pre-teach "drought" — it blocks the main idea. Leave "meteorological" — learners can infer it from context.
**Try —** Read your text; identify two genuinely blocking words. Pre-teach those, elicit the rest from context during or after the task.

---

### D159 · Schema Theory: Why Background Knowledge Beats Vocabulary
**Idea —** Schema theory (Bartlett) holds that we interpret new information through existing mental frameworks. A learner with rich background knowledge outperforms one with more vocabulary but no schema.
**Example —** Cricket fans decode a match report faster than fluent English speakers who have never seen the game, despite equal reading ability.
**Try —** Before a reading, give learners two minutes to write what they already know about the topic. Activated schema reduces processing load measurably.

---

### D160 · Authentic vs Adapted Texts: The Trade-Off
**Idea —** Authentic texts are designed for native speakers; adapted texts are simplified for learners. Neither is always right — the choice depends on level, task, and learning aim.
**Example —** A B1 class reading a newspaper editorial unedited will hit too many unknown words. A graded version reduces load but removes natural discourse features.
**Try —** Take one authentic sentence and rewrite it at A2, B1, and B2. Compare what each version gains and loses in authenticity and accessibility.

---

### D161 · Graded Readers: The Input Hypothesis Made Practical
**Idea —** Krashen's Input Hypothesis suggests we acquire language from input slightly above our current level (i+1). Graded readers operationalise this — they are calibrated to that zone.
**Example —** An A2 reader on a familiar topic lets a learner read 15,000 words fluently in an hour, encountering known structures in new combinations.
**Try —** Ask learners to find a graded reader at their level on a topic they enjoy. Set a weekly reading goal and ask them to report back.

---

### D162 · Intensive Reading: When Every Word Matters
**Idea —** Intensive reading means careful, slow reading for precise understanding — examining vocabulary, grammar, and cohesion closely. It suits short texts and language-focused aims.
**Example —** Analysing the grammar of a business email before learners write one of their own: that is intensive reading feeding productive work.
**Try —** Choose a paragraph. Ask learners to underline every verb and identify the tense. Then ask: why did the writer use this form here?

---

### D163 · Extensive Reading: Quantity Over Analysis
**Idea —** Extensive reading means reading large amounts at a comfortable level for pleasure and fluency — not stopping to analyse. Volume drives vocabulary growth and reading speed.
**Example —** ER programmes with 20-minute silent reading slots show measurable gains in fluency over a term when learners choose their own books.
**Try —** Introduce a 10-minute silent reading slot. Learners choose their own text. No tasks, no testing — just reading. Track pages per week.

---

### D164 · The Lead-In: Activating Schema Before the Text
**Idea —** A lead-in activates learners' prior knowledge before they encounter the text. It does not preview the content — it warms the relevant schema so processing is faster.
**Example —** Before a listening about job interviews, ask: "What questions do employers often ask?" Learners recall the topic, set expectations, and listen with purpose.
**Try —** For your next receptive-skills lesson, design a 3-minute lead-in — one question or image only. No text, no spoilers. Check it activates rather than reveals.

---

### D165 · Gist Tasks: One Question, Whole Text
**Idea —** A gist task gives learners one reason to process the whole text — not individual facts. It trains tolerance of ambiguity and listening for direction.
**Example —** "Is this conversation formal or informal?" cannot be answered from one sentence; learners must attend to the whole to decide.
**Try —** Write a gist question for a text you use regularly. Test it: can it be answered without hearing or reading past the first sentence? If yes, rewrite it.

---

### D166 · Detailed Comprehension: Going Deeper
**Idea —** Detailed comprehension tasks target specific information — facts, figures, sequences, or attitudes — and require a second or third pass. They follow gist; they never precede it.
**Example —** After establishing that a text is about a delayed flight (gist), learners find the departure time, the reason for delay, and the new gate number (detail).
**Try —** Check your existing comprehension questions: are any gist tasks disguised as detail tasks? Reorder them — gist always comes first.

---

### D167 · Inference: Reading What Isn't Written
**Idea —** Inference means deriving meaning that the writer implies but does not state. It requires combining text signals with background knowledge.
**Example —** "She put her coat back on." In context, this implies the room became cold or a decision was reversed — nothing is stated directly.
**Try —** Write three sentences with implied meanings. Ask learners: what can we infer? What in the text tells us that?

---

### D168 · Critical Reading: Fact vs Opinion
**Idea —** Critical reading tasks ask learners to evaluate a text — to judge bias, distinguish fact from opinion, or assess the writer's purpose. This is reading as active dialogue.
**Example —** A newspaper headline using "crumbles" and "soars" is evaluative where a neutral report gives data. Spotting this is critical reading in practice.
**Try —** Take any short article. Ask learners to underline facts in blue, opinions in red. Discuss what the ratio reveals about the text type.

---

### D169 · Listening for Specific Information
**Idea —** Listening for specific information means focusing on targeted details — names, numbers, times — while letting irrelevant content pass. Learners hold a question in mind before the text plays.
**Example —** At an airport, a traveller tunes out announcements until their flight number appears. That selective attention is the skill in action.
**Try —** Set three target questions before playing a clip. Stress that learners will not catch everything — and do not need to.

---

### D170 · Note-Taking During Listening: A Trainable Skill
**Idea —** Note-taking while listening is cognitively demanding because decoding and writing compete for working-memory resources. Training learners to use symbols and abbreviations reduces that load.
**Example —** Instead of writing sentences, a learner uses "→" for "causes," "≠" for "differs from," and "govt" for "government."
**Try —** Teach five abbreviation symbols. Play a 90-second clip. Learners take notes using only those symbols. Compare notes in pairs.

---

### D171 · Jigsaw Listening: Information-Gap in Reception
**Idea —** In jigsaw listening, different groups hear different parts of a text, then pool information. The information gap is real — no group can answer all questions alone.
**Example —** Group A hears a job interview from the employer's perspective; Group B hears it from the candidate's. Together they reconstruct what happened.
**Try —** Split a two-track dialogue: half the class hears Speaker A's audio only, half Speaker B's. They reconstruct the conversation together.

---

### D172 · Multiple Choice: What Makes a Good Distractor
**Idea —** In multiple-choice listening, a good distractor is plausible — same topic area — but wrong for a specific, testable reason. Vague or unrelated options do not discriminate.
**Example —** Q: Why was the meeting cancelled? A) poor attendance B) a scheduling conflict C) lack of preparation. B is the answer; A and C are plausible distractors from the same domain.
**Try —** Take an existing multiple-choice task. Remove the correct answer. Could learners identify and dismiss each distractor and explain why?

---

### D173 · True / False / Not Given: The Exact Distinction
**Idea —** "True" = confirmed by the text. "False" = contradicted by the text. "Not given" = neither confirmed nor contradicted. Conflating false and not given is the most common test error.
**Example —** Text: "The meeting was on Tuesday." Q: "The meeting lasted two hours." Answer: Not Given — the text does not address duration at all.
**Try —** Write three statements per category from a short text. Ask learners to justify each answer by citing the exact line that proves it.

---

### D174 · Ordering Tasks: Sequence and Cohesion
**Idea —** Ordering tasks ask learners to reconstruct the sequence of events, arguments, or steps from a text — testing comprehension of discourse structure, not just isolated facts.
**Example —** Learners hear five sentences from a process (how bread is made) in random order and arrange them logically. The task reveals whether they followed the whole text.
**Try —** Take four consecutive sentences from a text, scramble them, and ask learners to reorder them. Ask: what cohesion device gave each position away?

---

### D175 · Gap-Fill from Audio: Lighter Than Dictation
**Idea —** A listening gap-fill focuses attention on specific language points — function words, collocations, key content. It is lighter than full dictation but heavier than a gist task.
**Example —** A transcript with every fifth word removed directs learners to process grammar and collocational patterns, not just general meaning.
**Try —** Remove 8 words from a 60-word transcript, targeting function words and strong collocations. Play twice. Check together. Discuss why each word fits.

---

### D176 · Discourse Markers as Listening Anchors
**Idea —** Discourse markers — "however," "the key point is," "moving on" — signal shifts in topic, contrast, and emphasis. Training learners to hear them reveals text structure in real time.
**Example —** A lecturer says "Now, this is crucial." Trained listeners stop and attend; untrained listeners miss the signal and the content that follows.
**Try —** Give learners a list of ten discourse markers before a clip plays. They tick each one they hear. Debrief: what follows each marker?

---

### D177 · Listening to Accents: Exposure, Not Drilling
**Idea —** Research (Derwing & Munro) shows that listeners adapt to unfamiliar accents through exposure, not accent-drilling exercises. Variety builds tolerance; correction builds anxiety.
**Example —** Learners who hear one unfamiliar non-native accent weekly for a term understand it significantly better by the end — without a single pronunciation lesson targeting it.
**Try —** Check your audio materials for this term. Are learners hearing only one English variety? Introduce one new variety next lesson.

---

### D178 · Extensive Listening Outside the Classroom
**Idea —** Extensive listening — podcasts, series, audiobooks at a comfortable level — builds fluency and vocabulary without direct instruction. Volume matters more than difficulty.
**Example —** A B1 learner who listens to a scripted podcast for 20 minutes daily gains more listening hours in a month than a year of weekly classes.
**Try —** Ask learners to commit to 15 minutes of English listening outside class this week — any genre, any topic they enjoy. Report back next lesson.

---

### D179 · Vocabulary from Reading: Incidental Acquisition
**Idea —** Incidental vocabulary acquisition happens when learners meet a word in context repeatedly without deliberately studying it. Nation's research suggests 10–20 encounters produce productive acquisition.
**Example —** A learner reading extensively at the right level meets "inevitable" in different contexts across several chapters and infers, then absorbs, its meaning.
**Try —** Ask learners to flag one unknown word per extensive reading session. Track it for a month. How many encounters before they feel confident using it?

---

### D180 · Designing a Receptive-Skills Lesson from Scratch
**Idea —** A receptive-skills lesson follows: lead-in → pre-teach blocking vocabulary → gist task → detailed task → response or production. Each stage has a specific cognitive purpose — skip one and the sequence breaks.
**Example —** Jumping from pre-teaching directly to detailed questions removes the gist stage: learners search for facts before they have a mental frame for the whole text.
**Try —** Take a text you use. Map each stage against the sequence above. Which stage is missing or conflated with another? Redesign that stage only.

---

### D181 · Month 6 Review + Preview of Month 7
**Idea —** Month 6 covered the foundations of receptive-skills teaching: top-down and bottom-up processing, task sequencing, text types, and techniques from lead-ins to jigsaw listening.
**Example —** The lesson sequence from Day 180 — lead-in → vocabulary → gist → detail → response — is the backbone of every reading and listening lesson you will plan.
**Try —** Choose one lesson from Days 152–179 that changed something in your practice. Write one sentence on what changed and why. Keep it.
