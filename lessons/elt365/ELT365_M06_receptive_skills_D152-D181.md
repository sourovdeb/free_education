**Format:** 5-minute bite-size lessons (Idea / Example / Try)

---

### D152 · Why listening is harder than it looks
**Idea —** Listening in a second language demands simultaneous decoding of sound, parsing of grammar, and construction of meaning — all in real time, with no rewind button.
**Example —** A learner who reads *I should have gone* easily may miss it in speech as /aɪ ʃədəv gɒn/ — four words compressed into a blur.
**Try —** Record yourself saying three natural phrases. Play them back. How much did you compress?

---

### D153 · Bottom-up vs top-down listening
**Idea —** Bottom-up processing builds meaning from individual sounds upward; top-down uses prior knowledge and context to predict. Skilled listeners use both simultaneously.
**Example —** Hearing a weather forecast, you already expect "temperature", "rain", "degrees" — top-down. Catching the actual number spoken is bottom-up.
**Try —** Ask a learner to predict five words before playing a short news clip. Which predictions were right?

---

### D154 · Gist tasks — one question, whole text
**Idea —** A gist task poses one global question answered by hearing the whole text. It prevents learners from switching off once they catch a single familiar word.
**Example —** 'What is the main topic of this conversation?' is a gist task. 'What time does the train leave?' is a detail task — do not start there.
**Try —** Write one gist question for a two-minute audio clip you use in class.

---

### D155 · Listening for detail
**Idea —** Detail tasks ask learners to extract specific information. Set them after the gist task; front-loading detail questions causes panic and learners miss everything.
**Example —** 'Which city does the speaker prefer?' or 'How much does the flight cost?' — one answer, locatable in the text.
**Try —** Write three detail questions at different difficulty levels for the same listening text.

---

### D156 · Predicting before you listen
**Idea —** Prediction activates schema, focuses attention, and gives learners a genuine reason to listen. It is the most underused pre-listening technique.
**Example —** Show a photo of a busy airport and ask 'What might these people be talking about?' before playing a dialogue set there.
**Try —** Write one prediction task for a listening text you teach this week.

---

### D157 · Schema theory in listening
**Idea —** Schema are mental frameworks built from experience. Learners with relevant cultural schema comprehend far more, even at lower language levels.
**Example —** A learner who has visited a doctor understands a medical dialogue more easily — even without all the vocabulary — because the script is familiar.
**Try —** Identify one listening text where cultural knowledge gives native speakers an unfair advantage. Adjust your lead-in to bridge that gap.

---

### D158 · Authentic vs graded listening
**Idea —** Authentic recordings expose learners to real connected speech; graded recordings control load. The right choice depends on level and lesson stage.
**Example —** At A2, a slowed podcast with clear speakers builds confidence. At B2, the same unedited podcast challenges and prepares for the real world.
**Try —** Review one graded text you use. What authentic alternative exists, and at what level would you switch?

---

### D159 · How accents affect comprehension
**Idea —** Learners tuned to one accent — often British or American — struggle with Scottish, Irish, Indian, or Australian speakers. Accent exposure is a teaching goal, not a side-effect.
**Example —** A learner who scores 90% on a BBC listening task may score 40% lower hearing the same information from a Glaswegian speaker.
**Try —** Find one listening resource with a non-standard English accent and use it next week.

---

### D160 · Note-taking while listening
**Idea —** Note-taking is a productive sub-skill, not a shortcut. Teach learners to write key words and numbers, not full sentences — full sentences cost them the next sentence.
**Example —** 'Company profits rose in Q3' → notes: *profits ↑ Q3*. Full-sentence note: 'Company prof—' and Q3 is gone.
**Try —** Give learners a two-minute spoken text. Tell them to note only nouns and numbers. Review what they caught.

---

### D161 · Dictogloss — the collaborative listening task
**Idea —** Dictogloss is a reconstruction task: play a text at natural speed, learners note key words, then rebuild the meaning in groups. Grammar emerges from communicative need.
**Example —** Play a news paragraph twice. Learners write individually, then compare notes and rebuild the text in pairs. No transcript yet — that comes after.
**Try —** Write a 60-word dictogloss paragraph on a topic your class has studied this month.

---

### D162 · The listen-and-repeat trap
**Idea —** Drilling isolated sentences builds pronunciation patterns but not real listening. Learners who only ever repeat a model cannot handle unpredictable connected speech.
**Example —** A learner drills 'Can I help you?' perfectly, then cannot parse 'Cannahelp cha?' from a fast American cashier.
**Try —** Add one unpredictable spontaneous question to any drilling activity this week. Note what happens.

---

### D163 · Why learners miss words: connected speech
**Idea —** In fast speech, words link, reduce, and disappear. Learners taught vocabulary in isolation cannot recognise those words in the natural stream.
**Example —** 'Did you eat yet?' → /dɪdjʊiːtjet/ — two words missing, two merged.
**Try —** Choose five common questions from your course book. Write the connected-speech form of each. Teach both versions.

---

### D164 · Elision and assimilation
**Idea —** Elision means sounds drop out (*next door* → /neks dɔː/). Assimilation means a sound changes to match its neighbour (*ten minutes* → /tem mɪnɪts/). Both are normal spoken English, not lazy speech.
**Example —** 'Good morning' → /gʊm mɔːnɪŋ/ — the /d/ assimilates to the following /m/.
**Try —** Find three examples of assimilation in a course-book dialogue. Mark them for learners before they listen.

---

### D165 · Linking in fast speech
**Idea —** When a word ends in a consonant and the next begins with a vowel, the sounds link. This is the single biggest cause of missed words at A2–B1.
**Example —** 'Turn it off' → /tɜːnɪtɒf/ — one continuous sound, not three separate words.
**Try —** Dictate five linked phrases to your class at natural speed. How many do they hear as one word?

---

### D166 · Weak forms in the stream of speech
**Idea —** Function words — articles, prepositions, auxiliaries — reduce to a schwa or disappear in natural speech. Learners trained on strong forms are unprepared for real listening.
**Example —** 'I was at the station' → /aɪ wəz ət ðə steɪʃən/ — three function words weakened.
**Try —** Mark the weak forms in three sentences from tomorrow's listening. Pre-teach the reduced versions.

---

### D167 · Extensive listening — low-stress input
**Idea —** Extensive listening is high-volume, self-chosen, largely understood content. Comprehensible input applied to listening. Fluency grows from quantity, not from intensive drilling alone.
**Example —** A learner who watches one subtitled English comedy series per week, without pausing to look words up, builds speed and pattern recognition over time.
**Try —** Recommend one free listening source at your learners' level this week. No tasks. Just listen.

---

### D168 · Staging a listening lesson
**Idea —** Three stages, always in this order: pre-listening (activate schema, remove blockers) → while-listening (task) → post-listening (exploit the text, produce something). Skipping pre is the most common lesson-design error.
**Example —** Pre: show a hospital photo, elicit vocabulary. While: gist then detail task. Post: role play using the language just heard.
**Try —** Audit your next listening lesson against this three-stage sequence. Which stage do you usually cut short?

---

### D169 · Post-listening tasks that develop the text
**Idea —** Post-listening is where language learning deepens. The text becomes a springboard: discuss opinions, analyse the grammar, role-play a follow-up. 'Discuss in pairs' is not a post-listening task.
**Example —** After a job interview dialogue, learners analyse the interviewer's questions, then conduct their own interview using the same structure.
**Try —** Replace an open 'discuss in pairs' with a structured post-listening task for your next lesson.

---

### D170 · Reading vs listening — different processing demands
**Idea —** Reading allows re-reading; listening does not. But reading requires decoding written symbols; listening already knows the sound. Both skills need explicit teaching.
**Example —** An oral learner may speak fluently but read slowly — decoding words on the page is a separate skill from understanding spoken language.
**Try —** Identify one learner who performs very differently on reading versus listening tasks. What does the gap tell you about their needs?

---

### D171 · Top-down reading — what readers bring
**Idea —** Top-down reading means using title, headings, layout, and prior knowledge to predict and check meaning before decoding every word.
**Example —** A reader seeing the headline *Markets fall on rate fears* already expects vocabulary like 'investors', 'central bank', 'percent'.
**Try —** Before your next reading lesson, ask learners to predict five words the text contains. Tick them as they find each one.

---

### D172 · Bottom-up reading — decoding the page
**Idea —** Bottom-up reading processes phoneme to word, word to clause. It is essential for new scripts, low-frequency vocabulary, or technical precision where context cannot compensate.
**Example —** A nurse reading a drug dosage must decode bottom-up — every milligram matters; '0.5' misread as '5' cannot be corrected by context.
**Try —** Choose a short paragraph with three technical terms. Remove context clues. Can learners still decode the meaning accurately?

---

### D173 · Gist reading — skimming and scanning
**Idea —** Skimming = read fast for the general idea. Scanning = search for one specific item. Both require learners to ignore most of the text — that is the skill, not a shortcut.
**Example —** Skimming: 'What is this article mainly about?' Scanning: 'Find the date the law was passed.'
**Try —** Set a 90-second skimming task before any detailed reading this week. Time it strictly.

---

### D174 · Reading for detail — intensive skills
**Idea —** Intensive reading demands close, careful attention to a short text. It builds accuracy and language awareness, not reading speed. Use it after gist work.
**Example —** 'Find three reasons the author gives for the argument' requires learners to read every sentence, not skim or scan.
**Try —** Write one detail question per paragraph of a text you use. Map each question to its paragraph before the lesson.

---

### D175 · Inferring meaning from context
**Idea —** Guessing unknown words from context is a core reading strategy. Teach learners to use part of speech, surrounding sentence, and topic before reaching for a dictionary.
**Example —** 'The hidalgo strode into the room, his cloak billowing.' Hidalgo is a person — male, formal, historical. Enough meaning to continue reading.
**Try —** Blank out five words from a paragraph. Learners guess from context, then check. Discuss how they guessed each word.

---

### D176 · Jigsaw reading
**Idea —** Jigsaw reading gives different groups different texts, then mixes groups so each learner teaches their part to the others. This creates a genuine communicative purpose for reading.
**Example —** Three groups read three news articles on the same event. Regrouped, one person from each shares their story. What information differs? What is missing from each source?
**Try —** Find three short texts on one topic and design a jigsaw reading task for your next class.

---

### D177 · Grading reading texts
**Idea —** A text is appropriately graded when learners understand roughly 95% of the words — enough challenge without overload. Below that threshold, learners stop reading for meaning.
**Example —** Nation's vocabulary research suggests one unknown word per 100 known words for fluency reading; up to five per 100 for vocabulary growth.
**Try —** Count unknown words in a text you plan to use. Is it within the 95% comprehensible zone?

---

### D178 · Pre-teaching vocabulary — the gating problem
**Idea —** Pre-teaching every unknown word defeats the purpose of reading. Only pre-teach words that would block all understanding — 'gate' vocabulary that unlocks the whole text.
**Example —** Pre-teach *embargo* before a trade-war text. Leave *tariff* and *export* — context and cognates carry those.
**Try —** From your next reading text, identify two truly gating words. Pre-teach only those.

---

### D179 · Extensive reading — building fluency
**Idea —** Extensive reading means large volumes of self-chosen, largely understood text read for pleasure. It is the most reliable route to reading fluency (Nation's research). No tasks, no comprehension checks.
**Example —** A B1 learner reading one graded reader per week for a school year gains measurably in vocabulary size, grammar accuracy, and reading speed.
**Try —** Start a class reading log. Learners record title, pages read, and one sentence about it each week.

---

### D180 · Reading aloud — when it helps and when it does not
**Idea —** Reading aloud focuses attention on decoding — pronunciation, not meaning. Use it for phonics with young learners or dialogue performance; avoid it as a comprehension tool.
**Example —** Round-robin reading (each learner reads a paragraph aloud) reduces comprehension: the next reader is rehearsing their turn, not listening to the current one.
**Try —** Replace round-robin reading with silent reading followed by one comprehension question. Compare the quality of answers.

---

### D181 · Month 6 review — receptive skills
**Idea —** Month 6 covered listening (top-down vs bottom-up, staging, connected speech) and reading (skimming, scanning, grading texts, pre-teaching vocabulary). The thread: input must be comprehensible, purposeful, and staged.
**Example —** Listening: lead-in → gist task → detail task → post-listening production. Reading: predict → skim → scan → intensive → respond.
**Try —** Audit one recent receptive skills lesson against either model. What stage did you cut? Plan to restore it. Month 7 ahead: productive skills.
