---

### D182 · Speaking vs talking — what classroom tasks do
**Idea —** Learners talk all day. Speaking tasks give them specific language to practise in a communicative context. A task without a communicative goal is a drill, not a speaking activity.
**Example —** 'Discuss your weekend' = talking. 'Persuade your partner to visit your hometown, giving three concrete reasons' = a speaking task with a goal.
**Try —** Rewrite one discussion prompt from your course book to include a clear communicative goal and a measurable outcome.

---

### D183 · Accuracy vs fluency — the teacher's choice
**Idea —** Accuracy activities focus on correct form; fluency activities build speed and confidence. Every speaking task sits on a spectrum. Name your aim before you design the task.
**Example —** Drilling 'I used to + verb' = accuracy. 'Tell your partner about your childhood for two minutes without stopping' = fluency.
**Try —** Label three speaking activities in your course book A (accuracy), F (fluency), or A+F. Were they designed with that distinction in mind?

---

### D184 · Drilling — why, when, and how
**Idea —** Drilling fixes the sound and rhythm of new language in muscle memory. Use it after presentation, before freer practice — never as a lesson in itself.
**Example —** Choral drill → individual drill → back-chain drill for long chunks: *haven't I been waiting for this*.
**Try —** Drill one target structure using all three types: choral, individual, back-chain. Note which learners still need correction at the end.

---

### D185 · Controlled to free practice — bridging the gap
**Idea —** Controlled practice constrains form (fill the gap, substitute one word). Free practice gives full communicative choice. Jumping straight from a drill to a role play leaves the gap too large.
**Example —** Controlled: complete the dialogue with a modal verb. Semi-controlled: choose which modal fits your situation. Free: negotiate a real decision using modals naturally.
**Try —** Add a semi-controlled stage to your next grammar lesson, between the drill and the free production task.

---

### D186 · The information gap task
**Idea —** An information gap is the simplest communicative speaking task: student A holds information that B needs, and vice versa. Speaking happens because it must.
**Example —** Student A has a train timetable with three times missing. Student B has those missing times but different gaps. They ask and answer until both tables are complete.
**Try —** Design one information gap task using a topic from your current unit. Write what each student knows that the other needs.

---

### D187 · Role play — three reasons it fails
**Idea —** Role play fails when learners do not understand the situation, lack the language for it, or care too much about accuracy to speak at all. Fix the scenario card, not the activity.
**Example —** 'Be a customer in a shop' fails at A2. 'You want to return a blue jacket bought yesterday — it has a broken zip' gives learners something specific to say.
**Try —** Take a role play that went badly in your class. Rewrite the scenario card to be more concrete and contain the key vocabulary.

---

### D188 · Discussion tasks — the problem of unequal turns
**Idea —** In open discussion, confident speakers dominate and quieter learners produce nothing. Structured turns, written preparation, and talking tokens level the participation.
**Example —** 'Discuss' with six learners = two speak, four wait. 'Each person states one point using the prompt card, then pass' = all six contribute.
**Try —** Add a turn structure or a talking token rule to your next discussion task.

---

### D189 · Monitoring — what to do while learners speak
**Idea —** While learners do a speaking task, the teacher's job is to listen, note language, and not interrupt — unless a learner is completely stuck. Facilitate, do not teach.
**Example —** Move quietly around the room. Note three strong uses of target language and two errors. Use both in your delayed feedback, not during the task.
**Try —** Prepare a monitoring grid: one column for strong language, one for errors. Use it in your next speaking lesson.

---

### D190 · Content feedback vs language feedback
**Idea —** Give content feedback first — validate the communicative act — before correcting language. This tells learners the message matters, not only the form.
**Example —** After a learner explains a plan: 'Your three steps were clear. I also noticed *I am agree* — the correct form is *I agree*.'
**Try —** In your next speaking lesson, give one content comment before any language correction for each learner.

---

### D191 · Delayed error correction — the board trick
**Idea —** Write anonymised errors on the board during a fluency task. After the task, learners correct them together. Class-built correction; no individual embarrassment.
**Example —** Board during task: *I enjoyed to swim.* After task: 'Who can spot the error? What is the correct form and why?'
**Try —** During a five-minute speaking task, note four errors on a clipboard. After the task, put them on the board for peer correction.

---

### D192 · Writing as process vs product
**Idea —** Product writing aims at a finished text. Process writing stages the journey: brainstorm → draft → peer review → revise → final. Process builds transferable skills; product builds text-type knowledge.
**Example —** Product task: 'Write a formal email.' Process task: plan audience and purpose → draft → swap with a partner → revise → final version.
**Try —** Take a product writing task and add two stages: peer feedback and a revision pass.

---

### D193 · Process writing — plan, draft, revise, edit
**Idea —** Four stages, four cognitive tasks. Planning builds ideas; drafting releases them; revision improves content and structure; editing fixes surface form. Never mix revision and editing.
**Example —** At the revision stage, learners ask 'Is the message clear?' not 'Is the spelling right?' — that question belongs to editing.
**Try —** Give learners a colour-coding task: mark each section of their draft with the stage it represents (plan / draft / revision / edit).

---

### D194 · Genre in writing — text types matter
**Idea —** Each text type has a predictable structure, register, and social purpose. Teaching the genre explicitly gives learners the skeleton before they fill it with language.
**Example —** A complaint email follows: opening reference → specific problem → impact → requested action → polite close. Once learners see the pattern, the language fills itself.
**Try —** Choose one writing task you teach. Map the genre structure explicitly with your class before they write a word.

---

### D195 · Peer correction in writing
**Idea —** Peer correction builds editing skills, reduces teacher marking load, and creates a real audience for writing. It only works with a structured checklist — not 'check your partner's work'.
**Example —** Checklist: (1) Does the text have an opening, middle, and close? (2) Is the register consistent? (3) Circle two phrases you found effective.
**Try —** Write a three-item peer feedback checklist tailored to your next writing task.

---

### D196 · Teacher feedback on writing — less is more
**Idea —** Marking every error overwhelms learners and trains them to hunt for red pen, not to read for meaning. Target feedback to the lesson aim only.
**Example —** A B1 essay task aimed at argument structure: comment on structure and one language pattern. Leave spelling for the learner to self-correct.
**Try —** On your next marking batch, limit yourself to two feedback points per script, both tied directly to the lesson aim.

---

### D197 · Dictation — a neglected productive task
**Idea —** Dictation integrates listening, spelling, punctuation, and grammar in a single task. It is not a test — it is focused practice. Adjust speed and repetitions to control difficulty.
**Example —** Read a sentence at natural speed, pause, repeat. Learners write, compare with a partner, then see the original — three types of noticing in one pass.
**Try —** Use a dictation for the first five minutes of your next class, drawing on target language from the previous lesson.

---

### D198 · Collaborative writing tasks
**Idea —** Writing together removes blank-page panic, promotes discussion about language choices, and produces a shared text learners feel invested in.
**Example —** Groups of three write one sentence each in rotation, building a story. Or co-author a class guide to their city for an imaginary new student.
**Try —** Design one collaborative writing task where every learner contributes at least two sentences.

---

### D199 · The learner journal — extensive writing at low stakes
**Idea —** A learner journal is regular, personal, ungraded writing. It builds writing habit, fluency, and vocabulary without product pressure. Teachers respond to the content, not the errors.
**Example —** 'Write five sentences about your week, your opinion, or a question you have' — weekly, five minutes, no red pen.
**Try —** Introduce a journal task this week. Respond to two learners' content with your own view. Do not correct grammar.

---

### D200 · Mixed-ability speaking groups — roles that equalise
**Idea —** Mixed-ability speaking groups fail when stronger learners dominate. Role cards that assign specific responsibilities force every learner to contribute regardless of level.
**Example —** Role cards: Timekeeper (controls who speaks how long), Reporter (shares the group's conclusion), Devil's Advocate (must challenge at least one point).
**Try —** Assign one structured role per learner in your next group speaking task.

---

### D201 · Receptive to productive — the skills bridge
**Idea —** Receptive input feeds productive output. Learners cannot speak or write language they have never encountered in listening or reading. Plan a reading-to-speaking or listening-to-writing bridge.
**Example —** Learners read three opinions on a topic → choose one they agree or disagree with → write a response → share it with the group.
**Try —** In your next unit, trace one language item from input (listening or reading) to output (speaking or writing). Show learners the journey.

## Specialist Extension: Young Learners

### D202 · Teaching young learners — what is different
**Idea —** Young learners have shorter attention spans, learn through play and movement, and acquire language holistically. They need variety, physical activity, and clear routines — not condensed adult lessons.
**Example —** An adult may focus on a grammar explanation for 20 minutes. A seven-year-old needs a task change every 8–10 minutes to stay engaged.
**Try —** Count the task changes in your next young learner lesson. Aim for at least one every 10 minutes.

---

### D203 · Phonics for young learners — the basics
**Idea —** Phonics teaches the relationship between letters and sounds systematically. It is the most evidence-based approach to building reading in young learners of English.
**Example —** Teaching that *sh* makes one sound /ʃ/ (ship, shop, she) before learners try to read connected text prevents guessing and builds decoding confidence.
**Try —** Find one phonics activity targeting a sound your young learners regularly confuse in their written work.

---

### D204 · Songs and chants in the young learner classroom
**Idea —** Repetition in songs and chants drills rhythm, pronunciation, and vocabulary with zero tedium. Young learners do not tire of a song they enjoy.
**Example —** *Head, shoulders, knees and toes* teaches body parts through kinaesthetic reinforcement. The ABC song makes sequence memorable.
**Try —** Find one chant that practises a grammar point — not just vocabulary — and use it this week.

---

## Specialist Extension: Business English / Professional Learners

### D205 · Business English — needs analysis first
**Idea —** Professional learners have specific, job-related language needs. A needs analysis interview before teaching identifies what they must actually do in English: meetings, reports, emails, calls.
**Example —** A financial analyst presenting quarterly data has very different language needs from a receptionist handling enquiries. Same CEFR level; entirely different content.
**Try —** Write five needs analysis questions you would ask a new professional learner before planning a single lesson.

---

### D206 · Business English — email register
**Idea —** Register in professional writing means matching tone to the relationship and purpose: formal, semi-formal, or informal. Wrong register signals cultural mismatch, not just grammar error.
**Example —** 'Per my last email…' reads as passive-aggressive in many professional cultures. 'As I mentioned on Monday…' achieves the same reference with a neutral tone.
**Try —** Give learners three versions of the same email at different register levels. They decide which to send and to whom, then justify the choice.

---

## Specialist Extension: Mixed Ability & Differentiation

### D207 · Mixed ability — the reality
**Idea —** A mixed-ability class is not a problem to solve; it is the normal classroom. The goal is not to standardise outcomes but to give every learner an appropriate challenge.
**Example —** One reading task, three question sets: A (simpler, more contextual clues), B (standard), C (evaluative, open-ended). Same text; different entry points.
**Try —** Take one task you teach and write a simplified version and an extension. Use all three versions next week.

---

### D208 · Differentiation — the open task
**Idea —** An open task has no fixed right answer and can be completed at any level of depth. It differentiates naturally without requiring the teacher to write three separate versions.
**Example —** 'Write a description of your neighbourhood.' A1 writes three sentences; B2 writes a structured paragraph with contrast and opinion. Same task; different outcomes.
**Try —** Find one closed task in your current materials and make it open by removing the fixed answer constraint.

---

### D209 · Mixed levels together — pairing strategies
**Idea —** Pair stronger learners with weaker ones for production tasks; pair same-level learners for accuracy tasks. The goal determines the grouping, not class convention.
**Example —** Accuracy drill: pair by level so feedback is appropriate. Discussion task: pair mixed so weaker learners gain richer input and stronger learners consolidate.
**Try —** Deliberately vary your pairing strategy in your next lesson: same-level for controlled practice, mixed-level for freer production.

---

### D210 · Assessing mixed-ability classes fairly
**Idea —** Standardised tests measure all learners against the same benchmark. In mixed-ability classes, progress-based assessment — measuring individual gain — is more motivating and more honest.
**Example —** A learner who moves from A1 to A2 in one term has made outstanding progress, even if the class average is B1. Document that gain explicitly.
**Try —** Write one progress-based feedback comment per learner this week, focused on what each can do now that they could not do one month ago.

## Error Correction + Month Review

### D211 · Error correction — when and why
**Idea —** Immediate error correction interrupts fluency and can inhibit risk-taking. Delayed correction collects errors during the task and addresses them after. Match the type to the aim.
**Example —** Accuracy task: correct immediately. Fluency discussion: note errors quietly, correct after. Never interrupt a fluency task with form focus.
**Try —** At the start of your next lesson, decide: immediate or delayed correction. Commit to it for the whole session.

---

### D212 · Month 7 review — productive skills
**Idea —** Month 7 covered speaking (accuracy vs fluency, drilling, monitoring, feedback) and writing (process, genre, peer correction, journals). Core principle: production needs preparation and a communicative purpose.
**Example —** Speaking: model → controlled → semi-controlled → free task → content feedback → delayed language feedback. Writing: model → genre analysis → draft → peer review → revise.
**Try —** Map your next speaking and writing lessons to these sequences. Identify the missing stage and plan to add it.
