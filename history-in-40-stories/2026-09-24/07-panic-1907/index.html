07 — When private rescue hit its limit

The Panic of 1907

Imagine a town without firefighters.
A shop catches fire.
Other shopkeepers bring buckets.
That may save it.

But many fires start.

That is imagined comparison.

In October 1907, speculators tried cornering United Copper shares. The attempt failed. Runs then hit connected banks. Soon depositors rushed from New York trust companies. These trusts held lower cash reserves than national banks. Most also stood outside the New York Clearing House’s strongest support network. Knickerbocker Trust suspended payments on October 22 after nearly $8 million left.[1]

J. P. Morgan became crisis coordinator. He gathered bankers and arranged cash for the stock exchange. He also helped institutions he judged supportable. The U.S. Treasury deposited public money into banks.[1]

Private rescue had limits. Morgan could choose recipients. He lacked guaranteed public resources. No nationwide lender existed. Clearing-house banks issued loan certificates. They also restricted cash payments. Gold imports later eased shortages.[1][3]

Federal Reserve, 1913

The crisis reached businesses. Research links trust-company runs with worse outcomes for firms tied to affected trusts.[4] Congress created the National Monetary Commission in 1908. Its work shaped later reform. Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act in 1913.[2]

Who gained?
Some supported firms survived.

Who paid?
Depositors lost access.
Borrowers lost credit.
Businesses faced contraction.

Evidence limit: scholars debate how much the failed stock scheme mattered. Deeper funding weaknesses also mattered.[1][4]

Why speculate thoughtfully?

Consider two possibilities.

Private coordination might suffice.
Public liquidity might help.

Then seek contrary evidence.

Speculation explores possible outcomes.
Skepticism checks those outcomes.
Neither guarantees investment profits.

Question:
Who should rescue whom?
