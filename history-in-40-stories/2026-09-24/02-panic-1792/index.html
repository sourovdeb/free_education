When credit disappeared

Imagine a new market.
Everyone wants new bonds.
Banks make borrowing easier.
Prices keep climbing.
Then credit suddenly shrinks.

America's national bank opened.
That happened in 1791.
It expanded lending quickly.
Securities prices rose too. [1]

William Duer borrowed heavily.
He bought federal securities.
His plans needed credit. [2]

Then banks changed course.
Gold reserves faced pressure.
The national bank tightened.
Borrowers needed cash quickly.
They sold securities instead. [1]

Duer defaulted that March.
His network started failing.
Fear spread through markets.
Prices fell about 25%. [2]

Hamilton sought debt support. [3]
Treasury purchases supported prices.
Banks kept lending selectively.
Good collateral remained acceptable.
The panic eased quickly. [2][4]

Several forces interacted together.

Who gained?
Borrowers first gained credit.

Who bore losses?
Creditors faced losses.
Other investors lost wealth. [2]

What changed?
The panic exposed fragility.
Credit itself can vanish.
Broker cooperation soon followed. [4]

One limit matters.
No single cause suffices.
Duer's scheme mattered greatly.
Bank tightening mattered too.
Historians weigh them differently. [1][2]

Consider two possibilities.
Continued lending might stabilize.
It might deepen losses.
Earlier tightening might restrain.
It might trigger panic.

What evidence separates possibilities?
Check loans and reserves.
Check prices and defaults.
Then revise your explanation.

That is thoughtful speculation.
Skepticism checks the evidence.
Neither requires betting money.

Question for your child:
What breaks first?
Prices, trust, or credit?
