04 — When railways outran cash

British Railway Mania and the 1847 crisis

Imagine a new road changing every town. Trains really could do that. So investors imagined real possibilities.

Between 1843 and 1845, British railway shares rose sharply. Hundreds of proposed lines followed. Low rates and partly paid shares let buyers take large positions with small first payments. [1]

This shows sequence, not causation.

Railway companies later demanded more money. Those shareholder “calls” arrived while prices fell. Railway building also absorbed capital. [1]

Railways were not everything. Poor harvests raised food imports. Gold left Britain. Corn traders then suffered when prices fell. Commercial failures hit lenders. [2][3]

The Bank of England reacted. It raised rates and reduced lending. Panic returned in autumn. The 1844 Bank Charter Act constrained note issuance. On 25 October, ministers allowed extra lending if needed. Confidence returned before the legal limit was breached. [3]

Early sellers could profit. Later investors faced calls. Traders faced scarce credit. Bankruptcies and unemployment followed the wider downturn. [2]

Parliament debated railway borrowing. Speakers disagreed about blame. One member called railways only one cause. That disagreement matters. [4]

Evidence limit: historians dispute weighting. Railway speculation mattered. Harvests, trade, bank policy, corn speculation, and credit rules mattered too.

Thoughtful speculation: compare two explanations. “Railways caused everything.” Or, “several shocks collided.” Ask what evidence separates them. Speculation explores possibilities. Skepticism tests them. Neither requires betting money.
