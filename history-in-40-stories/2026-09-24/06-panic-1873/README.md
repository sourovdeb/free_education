# 06 — When railroad promises broke

*The Panic of 1873 and railway finance*

Imagine building a railway.
The tracks cost money now.
The passengers arrive later.

That is an imagined analogy.
It shows the timing problem.

After the Civil War,
American railroads expanded fast.
Investors bought railroad bonds.
Banks helped sell them.
Jay Cooke & Company
financed Northern Pacific.[1]

But financing outran demand.
Projects needed refinancing.
Then Vienna markets crashed.
European investors sold securities,
including American railroad bonds.[1][3]

Money became harder to raise.
Bond prices fell.
Railroads lost funding.
On September 18, 1873,
Jay Cooke & Company failed.[1]

Two days later,
New York's exchange closed.
Trading stopped ten days.
The panic spread nationally.
At least 100 banks failed.[1]

The New York Clearing House
pooled bank liquidity.
Archival research finds
clearinghouse loan certificates
helped reallocate scarce funds.[2]

Investors lost money.
Railroad building slowed.
Businesses lost credit access.
Workers lost demand.[1]

NBER's historical chronology
dates a U.S. contraction
from October 1873
through March 1879.[4]

But labels need caution.
"Long Depression" can mislead.
Countries followed different paths.
Britain was less affected
than Austria, Germany, America.[3]

## Why speculate?

Test two explanations.

**A:** rail finance broke first.

**B:** global credit withdrawal
amplified existing weakness.

Ask what disproves each.
Check bond flows.
Check failure timing.
Check bank balance sheets.

Speculation explores alternatives.
Skepticism tests evidence.
Neither predicts prices safely.

**Discuss together:**  
Can useful projects fail
because financing arrives wrong?
