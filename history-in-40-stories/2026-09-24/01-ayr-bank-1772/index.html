The bank that ran out of time

01 / 40 · Financial histories 1 / 20 · Scotland, 1769–1773

Imagine borrowing coins, promising repayment tomorrow, then lending them to a friend building a shop. The shop might earn money next year. But next year cannot pay tomorrow’s bill. This is an imagined comparison, not a historical scene.

In 1769, Ayr Bank opened in Scotland, backed by wealthy landowners. It offered generous loans, including money for projects whose returns would take years. To keep going, it relied heavily on repeatedly renewed short-term borrowing. Rapid expansion and questionable lending added danger. [1,4]

In June 1772, a connected London bank failed. Borrowing became harder and demands for cash intensified. Ayr Bank suspended payments on 25 June. After briefly reopening, it finally closed in 1773. [1]

The timing trap · one vulnerability, not the whole cause [1,4]

BANK'S PROMISES          BANK'S LOANS
Repay cash soon          Earn cash later
       |                       |
       +----- timing gap ------+
                  |
New borrowing stops -> cannot pay

What happened next [1,2]

1769            June 1772          1773
Bank opens ---> Payments stop ---> Closes
                    |
                    +--> Decades of unwinding

Borrowers initially gained access to credit. Bank partners later bore heavy losses. Selling assets in distress worsened losses, and unwinding the bank took decades. Its experience also helped shape Adam Smith’s analysis of banking. [1,2,4]

A caution: historian Paul Kosmetatos challenges the familiar idea that a broad speculative bubble explains this crisis. A memorable story is not the whole explanation. [3]

Why speculate thoughtfully? Imagine two futures: lenders renew their loans, or refuse. Then ask what evidence supports each possibility. Looking only at the happy future hides danger. Skepticism checks the evidence; speculation explores possibilities. Neither requires betting money.

Ask together: “Could something valuable still leave us unable to pay on time?”
