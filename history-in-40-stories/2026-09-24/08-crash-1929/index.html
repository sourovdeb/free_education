08 — When a crash became depression

The 1929 crash and the Great Depression

Imagine a crowded theatre. Someone shouts, “Fire!” People rush toward one exit. This is an imagined analogy. It is not history. A crash can start panic. It cannot explain everything.

In the 1920s, US shares climbed. Margin accounts let investors borrow. The Dow peaked on 3 September 1929. On 28 October it fell nearly 13 percent. The next day brought another fall. By mid-November, almost half the peak value had vanished. [1]

1929                 1930-31           1933
Stocks fall  --->  Bank panics  --->  Bank holiday
     |                |                   |
spending falls     credit shrinks     reforms begin

Visual meaning: the crash came first. Banking failures, deflation, credit damage, and policy choices deepened the downturn. [2][3]

What did people expect? Many believed prosperity would continue. New technologies fed confidence. After the crash, uncertainty changed behaviour. Christina Romer argues that fear about future income pushed households to delay durable purchases. Factories then faced weaker demand. [2]

Visual meaning: several feedback loops mattered. No single arrow explains everything.

Investors lost wealth first. Workers, borrowers, depositors, farmers, and businesses later carried wider costs. By late 1930, bank panics spread. Bank failures damaged lending. Cash hoarding increased. Deflation raised debt burdens. Federal Reserve responses differed. Gold-standard pressures also constrained policy. [3][4]

What changed? Emergency banking measures came in 1933. Deposit insurance followed. Banking regulation changed. [4]

Evidence limit: historians debate weights. The crash mattered. Banking failures mattered. Monetary policy mattered. International pressures mattered. Their relative importance remains contested.

Why speculate carefully? Build two explanations. Seek contrary evidence. Revise when facts change. That is speculation with skepticism. It is not gambling.
