12/40 — When selling triggered more selling

On October 19, 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6%. It remains the largest one-day percentage drop. [1]

Investors had reasons to worry. Interest rates were rising. The dollar had weakened. A large trade deficit surprised markets. Stocks had fallen during the prior week. [2][4]

Many institutions used “portfolio insurance.” Its rule was simple: reduce stock exposure as prices fall. That could protect one portfolio. But many firms used similar rules. Falling prices triggered more selling. Futures fell. Arbitrage linked futures back to stocks. Trading systems lagged. Margin calls drained liquidity. The loop amplified stress. It was not the sole cause. [2]

This diagram shows a feedback loop. It is not a single-cause claim. [2]

Investors forced to sell bore losses. Brokers faced funding strain. Cash holders had more choices later. Profit was never guaranteed. [2]

The Federal Reserve supplied liquidity. Regulators later added circuit breakers. Market plumbing drew scrutiny. [1][4]

Evidence has limits. Researchers debate causation. Robert Shiller’s survey found no single weekend news story explained the crash. Many investors reacted to price moves. [3]

Two hypotheses: Less portfolio insurance might have slowed selling. Order data could test that. Worse payment failures might have spread the crisis. Settlement records could test that. [2]

Thoughtful speculation explores paths. Skepticism checks those paths. Price betting is different. History should change when evidence changes.

Question: Which mattered more: fear, or market design?
