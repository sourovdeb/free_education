10 — When the gold window closed

The dollar–gold break, 1971

Imagine a shopkeeper promising:
“Bring me thirty-five dollars.
I will give gold.”

That analogy is imagined.
The system was different.

Bretton Woods linked currencies.
Most linked toward dollars.
Foreign central banks mattered.
Their dollars could reach gold.
Gold cost $35. [1][3]

For years, dollars spread abroad.
Trade needed dollar reserves.
American deficits supplied them.
Trust slowly faced pressure.

By the 1960s, strains grew.
Foreign dollar claims expanded.
American gold stayed limited.
Inflation also rose. [1][3]

Visual meaning: promises stretched.

Officials tried several repairs.
Central banks used swaps.
The London Gold Pool intervened.
That pool failed in 1968. [1]

Then came August 1971.
Pressure intensified that summer.
On August 15, Nixon spoke.
He suspended gold conversion.
Foreign authorities lost access. [2][4]

Visual meaning: change continued.
One speech wasn't everything.

Nixon also froze prices.
That freeze lasted ninety days.
He added import surcharges. [2]

The suspension mattered greatly.
But causes were multiple.
Inflation mattered.
Payments deficits mattered.
Capital flows mattered. [1][3]

The suspension seemed temporary.
Officials tried new parities.
The Smithsonian Agreement followed.
It did not last.
Major currencies floated by 1973. [3][4]

Who gained?
Policymakers gained more flexibility.
Who lost?
Official gold conversion vanished.
Exchange certainty also weakened.

Evidence limit: causes overlap.
Sources stress different mechanisms.
No single cause suffices.

Why speculate carefully?
Imagine keeping convertibility.
Would gold reserves survive?
Imagine changing exchange rates.
Would confidence return?

Check reserve data.
Check inflation data.
Check policy records.
Then revise your view.

Speculation explores alternatives.
Skepticism checks evidence.
Neither means betting money.
