05 — When one panic crossed oceans

The international Panic of 1857

Imagine a railway town booming. Land rises. Tracks spread. Banks lend. Then several links break.

During the 1850s, western American land and railroad investment expanded. When those investments weakened, losses reached eastern brokers and banks. State banking systems handled the shock differently. [1]

western land + railways
          |
       weaken
          v
brokers -> banks -> depositors
   |          |          |
 losses   less credit    runs
          |
          +----> other regions

This shows transmission. It proves no single cause.

On 24 August 1857, the New York office of Ohio Life Insurance and Trust suspended payments. Reports of misconduct and losses damaged confidence. Banks restricted credit. By October, runs intensified. New York banks suspended specie payments. [2]

Savings-account research adds nuance. Runs began among businessmen and informed depositors. Less-informed depositors followed. Information problems mattered alongside contagion. [3]

Aug 24      October       November
Ohio Life -> NY panic -> Britain strains
 fails        peaks        |
                            v
                    Bank lending rises

The crisis crossed borders. Britain suffered failures. Bank of England records show crisis lending increased. Later research found its lending broadly resembled what became “lender of last resort” practice. [4]

Some buyers gained cheap assets. Investors and borrowers lost. Workers faced unemployment. Merchants faced frozen credit.

Evidence limit: “global crisis” hides differences. Countries had different banks, trade exposures, and responses. One American failure did not cause every foreign failure.

Thoughtful speculation: test two ideas. “One failure caused everything.” Or, “many weaknesses connected.” Ask what evidence separates them. Speculation explores possibilities. Skepticism tests evidence. Neither requires risking money.

Question: When does connection spread danger?
