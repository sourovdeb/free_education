11 — When cheap loans turned heavy

The Latin American debt crisis, 1982

Imagine borrowing during sunshine.
The interest looks manageable.
Then storms change everything.
Rates rise.
Sales weaken.
Lenders disappear.

That comparison is imagined.

During the 1970s, banks expanded lending.
Oil exporters held surpluses.
Banks recycled those funds.
Latin American borrowing surged.[1]
Debt reached $327 billion.
That was by 1982.[1]

Much debt used dollars.
Many loans used floating rates.
Then rich-country policy tightened.
World interest rates climbed.
A recession followed in 1981.[1][2]
Export earnings also weakened.[2][3]

The arithmetic changed quickly.

Visual meaning: several shocks connected.

Mexico became the trigger.
Officials warned creditors first.
On 20 August 1982,
Mexico announced a moratorium.
It covered principal payments.
Public bank loans were affected.[2]
Other countries soon struggled.
Banks cut new lending.[1]

1970s        Aug 1982        1989
loans grow -> Mexico pauses -> Brady Plan
                   |
             crisis spreads

Visual meaning: resolution took years.

Emergency lending prevented collapse.
Banks restructured existing claims.
The IMF supplied support.[1]
Debtor governments cut spending.
Workers faced lost jobs.
Investment also fell.[1][3]
Creditors avoided immediate losses.
Later, write-downs became unavoidable.
The Brady Plan followed.[1]

No single cause explains this.
Domestic policies mattered.[1][3]
External shocks mattered too.[2]
Creditor behaviour also mattered.[4]

Thoughtful speculation helps here.
Suppose rates stayed low.
Would debts remain payable?
Suppose banks lent less.
Would growth slow earlier?
Evidence should decide.
Predictions are not investments.

Evidence limit: historians disagree.
They weight causes differently.
Mexico was not everyone.
Regional experiences varied.

Question: Who should adjust first?
Borrowers, lenders, or both?
