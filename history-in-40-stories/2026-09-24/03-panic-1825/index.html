When London bought promises

Imagine London in 1824.
Peace had returned.
Savings sought new returns.
New republics needed money.
Mining stories filled newspapers.

Investors bought foreign bonds.
They also bought shares.
Latin American mines attracted cash.
Some ventures were sound.
Others were poorly understood. [1]

Prices climbed through 1824.
Mining shares rose fastest.
Part-paid shares increased leverage.
Information travelled slowly.
Promoters knew much more. [1]

1822–24   Foreign loans grow
    |
1824      Share boom expands
    |
Feb 1825  Prices near peak
    |
Dec 1825  Banking panic
    |
1826–29   Defaults spread

This shows event order.
It proves no cause.

Then prices started falling.
Credit became harder.
Banks faced withdrawals.
Some banks failed.
Pole, Thornton collapsed.
Panic peaked that December. [2]

easy credit + stories
          |

The Bank hesitated first.
Then lending expanded sharply.
Small notes returned briefly.
The panic finally eased. [2]

Borrowers gained early funding.
Promoters earned fees.
Investors lost wealth.
Businesses lost credit access.

Latin American governments suffered.
Many later suspended payments.
Wars also drained budgets.
Defaults differed by country. [3]

What changed?
Britain changed banking rules.
Bank branches followed quickly.
The first opened in 1826. [4]

One limit matters.
No single trigger suffices.
Historians stress several mechanisms.
Speculation clearly mattered.
Monetary conditions mattered too.
Bank structure mattered too. [1][2]

Consider two possibilities.
Falling prices caused panic.
Credit withdrawal worsened falls.
Both can reinforce themselves.

What evidence separates them?
Check prices first.
Check bank failures next.
Check lending conditions too.
Then revise your explanation.

That is thoughtful speculation.
Skepticism tests each story.
It seeks contrary evidence.
It never guarantees forecasts.
It need not risk money.

Question for your child:
What makes promises believable?
