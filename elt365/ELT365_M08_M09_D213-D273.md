## MONTH 8 · CLASSROOM MANAGEMENT (Days 213–243)

### D213 · What Is Classroom Management?
**Idea —** Classroom management is everything a teacher does to create and maintain conditions for learning. It is not about control; it is about enabling learning to happen.
**Example —** A teacher who gives clear instructions, positions herself where all learners can see her, and moves smoothly between stages is managing the classroom well.
**Try —** List three things you do before learners arrive that shape the lesson before a word is spoken.

---

### D214 · Arranging the Room
**Idea —** The physical layout of the room shapes interaction patterns before you open your mouth. Rows favour teacher-fronted work; clusters favour collaboration.
**Example —** A horseshoe arrangement lets the teacher monitor all learners without anyone sitting with their back to the board.
**Try —** Sketch three room layouts. Next to each, write one activity it suits best.

---

### D215 · Seating Plans — Horseshoe, Rows, Clusters
**Idea —** Each seating arrangement carries a message about who talks to whom. Change the furniture and you change the default interaction pattern.
**Example —** Clusters encourage peer discussion but make it harder for the teacher to address the group. Rows make monitoring writing tasks easier.
**Try —** For your next lesson, decide the layout before you plan the first activity — then check they match.

---

### D216 · Managing Noise Levels
**Idea —** Noise in a language classroom is not a problem by default — productive noise (learners speaking in the target language) is the goal. Manage type, not volume.
**Example —** Raise your hand silently to signal wind-down. Learners copy; noise drops without a word from you.
**Try —** Observe your next class. Count seconds of productive learner speech vs seconds of off-task chat.

---

### D217 · Using Silence Deliberately
**Idea —** Wait time — the pause after a question — directly increases the quality of learner responses. Most teachers wait fewer than two seconds before reformulating.
**Example —** After asking 'What do you think the text is about?', count silently to five before taking answers. More learners contribute.
**Try —** Time your wait after your next three questions. Aim for at least three seconds each time.

---

### D218 · Giving Clear Instructions
**Idea —** Instruction-giving is a teaching skill, not an administrative chore. Vague instructions waste lesson time and demotivate learners.
**Example —** Instead of 'Work in pairs on the exercise', say: 'Work with your partner. Read each sentence. Choose the correct verb form. You have three minutes.'
**Try —** Write out the exact words you will use for your next task instruction — then count the steps.

---

### D219 · Checking Instructions — ICQs
**Idea —** Instruction-checking questions (ICQs) confirm learners have understood the task before they start — not what they should produce, but how they should work.
**Example —** 'Are you writing or speaking?' / 'Are you working alone or with a partner?' / 'How much time do you have?'
**Try —** Write two ICQs for a speaking task in your next lesson. Make each one answerable in one or two words.

---

### D220 · Starting the Lesson Well
**Idea —** The first three minutes set the pace and signal what kind of lesson this will be. A strong start narrows the gap between what learners expect and what you deliver.
**Example —** A brief warmer that activates relevant vocabulary signals: today's lesson has a clear purpose. No long recap; move fast into content.
**Try —** Plan exactly how you will spend the first three minutes of your next lesson. Write it out stage by stage.

---

### D221 · Ending the Lesson Well
**Idea —** A strong close recycles the lesson's aim and gives learners something to take away. Trailing off or running over time undermines the lesson's impact.
**Example —** 'Today we practised disagreeing politely. What's one phrase you'll use this week?' One learner answers; class ends on their words, not yours.
**Try —** Plan a 90-second close for your next lesson that names the aim, elicits one example, and sets a micro-task for outside class.

---

### D222 · Keeping Time in Class
**Idea —** Timing is a craft. Under-time and learners are idle; over-time and the lesson loses structure. Planned timings are estimates, not contracts.
**Example —** If a speaking task runs two minutes long, cut the following controlled practice by two minutes — preserve the free-production stage above all.
**Try —** In your next lesson, note the planned time and the actual time for each stage. Analyse where and why they diverged.

---

### D223 · Transitions Between Stages
**Idea —** The moment between stages — ending pair work, moving to feedback — is where lessons lose momentum. Prepare signalling language in advance.
**Example —** 'Good. Let's get some answers on the board.' Pause. Wait for pens down. Then: 'Who had something interesting?' — not 'Did anyone get it right?'
**Try —** Write the transition sentence you will use to move from your next task into whole-class feedback.

---

### D224 · Managing Mixed-Level Classes
**Idea —** Mixed ability is the norm, not the exception. The question is not how to remove the gap but how to use it — faster learners support slower ones when tasks allow.
**Example —** An open information-gap task lets A2 learners communicate at their level while B1 learners use more complex structures to fill the same information hole.
**Try —** Take one task from your coursebook. Write a simpler version and a more challenging extension for the same core activity.

---

### D225 · Working with Large Classes
**Idea —** In large classes, whole-class activities eat time; pair and group work multiply speaking opportunities. Monitoring must be systematic, not random.
**Example —** With 30 students, a speaking task gives 15 simultaneous conversations instead of one. Move through the room in a fixed pattern so no pair is skipped.
**Try —** Plan a route around the room for your next monitoring stage. Decide in advance which three pairs you will focus on for feedback.

---

### D226 · Working with Very Small Classes
**Idea —** Classes of two to five learners change the social dynamic — there is no crowd to hide in. Build in solo preparation time before pair or group tasks.
**Example —** With three learners, a roleplay can rotate: A speaks to B while C prepares; then A speaks to C while B reflects. Each person is active.
**Try —** Plan a five-minute speaking task that works without any group larger than two learners.

---

### D227 · Managing Disruptive Behaviour
**Idea —** Most classroom disruption is unmet need: boredom, confusion, anxiety, or social pressure. Address the cause before the behaviour.
**Example —** A learner who talks over others may need a higher-challenge task or a different seat. Change the condition before raising the issue publicly.
**Try —** Think of one recurring disruption you face. Write one environmental or task-design change that might remove the trigger.

---

### D228 · Keeping Learners on Task
**Idea —** Task design drives on-task behaviour. If learners go off-task consistently, the task may be too easy, too hard, or the outcome too unclear.
**Example —** Adding a visible product — 'Write your three answers on the card' — keeps a group task anchored. Remove the product and drift follows.
**Try —** Review your last lesson plan. Does each task have a clear, observable outcome? Revise any that do not.

---

### D229 · Using Learner Names
**Idea —** Using names personalises attention and signals you know who is in the room. Directing questions to named learners also distributes participation.
**Example —** 'What do you think, Fatima?' distributes the floor. 'What does everyone think?' gives it to whoever speaks loudest.
**Try —** Make a seating plan for your next class. Aim to use every learner's name at least once in the lesson.

---

### D230 · Rapport in the Classroom
**Idea —** Rapport is the working relationship between teacher and learner — not friendship, but trust. Learners learn more when they feel seen and safe to make mistakes.
**Example —** Acknowledging a learner's answer before evaluating it — 'Good idea — let's look at the form' — builds rapport without empty praise.
**Try —** In your next lesson, count evaluative responses ('Good', 'Wrong') vs acknowledgement responses ('Interesting — tell me more').

---

### D231 · Teacher Proximity — Moving Around the Room
**Idea —** Where you stand sends a signal. Standing at the front during pair work keeps the focus on you, not the task. Move into the room to monitor.
**Example —** During a writing task, crouch to eye-level next to a learner. This says: I am interested in your work, not policing you.
**Try —** In your next lesson, identify two points where you tend to stay fixed. Plan to move away from those points deliberately.

---

### D232 · Teacher Talk Time — Cutting It Down
**Idea —** Teacher talk time (TTT) includes instructions, explanations, modelling, and filler. High TTT crowds out learner speaking practice — the goal of the lesson.
**Example —** Recording yourself for ten minutes reveals how much you explain versus elicit. Most teachers are surprised. Eliciting takes longer but produces more learning.
**Try —** In your next lesson, choose one explanation you planned to give and convert it to an elicitation sequence instead.

---

### D233 · Student Talk Time — Building It Up
**Idea —** Student talk time (STT) is not just speaking-skill practice — it is thinking time in the target language. Every minute of productive STT is a minute of acquisition.
**Example —** A five-minute pair discussion, well-monitored and feedback-given, produces more usable output than a ten-minute teacher explanation.
**Try —** Calculate the STT in your next lesson plan. If it is less than 50% of lesson time, add one more speaking stage.

---

### D234 · Wait Time — The Power of the Pause
**Idea —** Wait time is the deliberate pause after a teacher question. Extending it beyond three seconds increases both the length and quality of learner responses.
**Example —** Ask a question, then look around the room in silence. Resist the urge to rephrase. Let the silence do the work.
**Try —** Set a timer after your next three questions. Do not speak until three seconds have passed. Note the difference in responses.

---

### D235 · Using the Board Effectively
**Idea —** The board is a shared, permanent record of the lesson. A clear, organised board plan — written before the lesson — prevents mid-lesson confusion and supports visual learners.
**Example —** Divide the board into three columns: vocabulary (left), target language (centre), student output (right). Stick to the zones throughout.
**Try —** Sketch your board plan for your next lesson before you walk into the room.

---

### D236 · Pairing and Grouping Learners
**Idea —** Who works with whom changes what learners produce. Teacher-assigned pairs are not arbitrary — they are a pedagogical decision.
**Example —** Pairing a stronger and a weaker learner on a fluency task scaffolds the weaker; pairing two similar-level learners on a negotiation task produces richer language.
**Try —** Plan three different grouping arrangements for three stages in your next lesson. State the reason for each.

---

### D237 · Monitoring Pair and Group Work
**Idea —** Monitoring is purposeful observation: you are listening for content (task completion) and language (for feedback). It is not tidying the desk or waiting.
**Example —** Carry a notepad. Note two or three language points you hear — accurate examples to highlight and errors to address in delayed feedback.
**Try —** In your next monitoring stage, write down one thing a learner said well and one error that was common. Plan how to use both.

---

### D238 · Managing Feedback Stages
**Idea —** Whole-class feedback after a task has two parts: content feedback (did they complete the task?) and language feedback (how did they say it?). Do content first.
**Example —** 'What did your partner recommend?' (content) — then later: 'I heard someone say "I advice you…" — what's the correct form?' (language).
**Try —** Plan the exact sequence of your next feedback stage. Write the first content question and the first language point you will address.

---

### D239 · Handling Learner Errors in the Moment
**Idea —** On-the-spot correction interrupts fluency tasks — use it sparingly. Delayed correction preserves flow and lets you address the most important patterns.
**Example —** During a role play, note errors on your notepad. Afterwards: 'I heard a few interesting forms — let's look at them on the board.'
**Try —** In your next fluency task, practise not interrupting. Note five errors. Decide which two deserve whole-class attention.

---

### D240 · Motivation in the Classroom
**Idea —** Motivation is not a learner trait you inherit — it is partly shaped by task design, feedback quality, and how visible learner progress is.
**Example —** Setting a task with a real-world outcome (a menu to write, a complaint to draft) raises intrinsic motivation because the purpose is clear.
**Try —** Identify one task in your next lesson that has no visible real-world purpose. Add one.

---

### D241 · Using Humour Well
**Idea —** Shared laughter builds rapport and lowers the affective filter. The target is inclusive humour — based on ideas, not people. Never at a learner's expense.
**Example —** A playful example sentence ('My cat is applying for a mortgage') engages learners in grammar practice without singling anyone out.
**Try —** Write three example sentences for a target language point that are factually absurd but grammatically perfect.

---

### D242 · Energy Management Across a Lesson
**Idea —** Lesson energy follows a shape: engage learners early, hold attention through varied tasks, and close before momentum drops. Pace is a design choice.
**Example —** If the middle of your lesson is three written exercises in a row, the energy will dip. Insert one brief speaking task to reset engagement.
**Try —** Map the energy curve of your last lesson: high, medium, or low at each stage. Identify the dip and plan one change.

---

### D243 · Month 8 Review — Classroom Management
**Idea —** Classroom management is a system of small, purposeful decisions: room layout, timing, instructions, monitoring, and feedback — all chosen to maximise learner engagement and language use.
**Example —** A teacher who prepares her board plan, writes her ICQs, and routes her monitoring before the lesson is managing the classroom before she enters it.
**Try —** Write five classroom management principles you will apply consistently from this month. Keep the list visible when you plan.

## MONTH 9 · LESSON PLANNING (Days 244–273)

### D244 · Why Plan Lessons?
**Idea —** A lesson plan is a tool for thinking — not a script. It forces you to articulate what you want learners to achieve and how you will get them there.
**Example —** The plan you write on paper and the lesson you teach will differ. The plan's value is not prediction; it is the thinking it produces beforehand.
**Try —** Find a lesson you taught recently with no written plan. Write the plan retrospectively. Note what was missing.

---

### D245 · The Lesson Plan vs the Lesson
**Idea —** A written plan is a hypothesis; the lesson is the experiment. Experienced teachers deviate from plans when learners need something different — but only because they planned well.
**Example —** If a vocabulary task takes twice as long as planned, the plan tells you what to cut. Without it, you have no anchor.
**Try —** After your next lesson, annotate your plan with what actually happened. Use a different colour. Compare them.

---

### D246 · Aims — Main and Subsidiary
**Idea —** Every lesson needs one main aim — the primary thing learners will be better able to do by the end. Subsidiary aims are language or skills practised along the way.
**Example —** Main aim: learners practise making polite requests using 'could you…'. Subsidiary: reading for gist; pronunciation of weak forms.
**Try —** Write a main aim and two subsidiary aims for your next lesson. Check: is the main aim the biggest thing you are teaching?

---

### D247 · Writing a Good Lesson Aim
**Idea —** A good lesson aim is learner-facing and observable: 'By the end, learners will be better able to…' Avoid teacher behaviour ('I will teach…') as the aim.
**Example —** Weak: 'To teach the present perfect.' Strong: 'By the end, learners will be better able to use the present perfect to describe recent experiences.'
**Try —** Take the aim of your last three lessons and rewrite each one in SWBAT (Students Will Be Able To) format.

---

### D248 · Selecting Target Language
**Idea —** Target language is the specific item you are teaching: a structure, a set of lexical chunks, a functional exponent. Analyse it before you plan any stages.
**Example —** If you are teaching 'used to', write out its form, meaning, and pronunciation before writing a single stage of the plan.
**Try —** Choose the target language for your next lesson. Write MFPA (Meaning, Form, Pronunciation, Appropriacy) before opening the coursebook.

---

### D249 · Assumed Knowledge
**Idea —** Lesson plans should state what learners are expected to already know. This shapes your starting point and helps you avoid teaching what they already have.
**Example —** 'Learners can use the past simple for finished actions' is assumed knowledge for a present perfect lesson. State it explicitly.
**Try —** For your next lesson, write one sentence of assumed knowledge. Then design a brief diagnostic activity to check it holds.

---

### D250 · Anticipated Problems — Language
**Idea —** Before teaching, predict where learners will struggle with the target language: meaning confusion, form errors, or pronunciation difficulty.
**Example —** For 'used to': learners may confuse it with 'be used to doing'. Anticipate this and prepare a contrast example before the lesson.
**Try —** For your next target language item, write three anticipated problems — one for meaning, one for form, one for pronunciation.

---

### D251 · Anticipated Problems — Learners and Tasks
**Idea —** Beyond language, plan for procedural problems: unclear task design, grouping that doesn't work, or a text too long for the time available.
**Example —** 'The reading text has twelve unfamiliar words — I may need to pre-teach three blocking ones or the gist task will fail.'
**Try —** Read through your next lesson plan. Identify one task that could go wrong and write a concrete solution beside it.

---

### D252 · Solutions — Planning for Problems
**Idea —** For every anticipated problem, write a solution — not a worry. The plan is your back-up strategy. A problem without a solution is just an anxiety on paper.
**Example —** Problem: learners may not have enough vocabulary to complete the speaking task. Solution: provide a word-bank on the board before they start.
**Try —** Go back to the problems you wrote in D250 and D251. Write one specific solution for each.

---

### D253 · Staging a Lesson — What Is a Stage?
**Idea —** A stage is a discrete part of the lesson with its own aim, activity, interaction pattern, and timing. Most 60-minute lessons have four to seven stages.
**Example —** Lead-in (5 min) → pre-teach vocabulary (8 min) → gist reading (7 min) → detail task (10 min) → speaking response (15 min) → feedback (10 min) → close (5 min).
**Try —** Count the stages in your last lesson plan. Does each have a clear aim distinct from the others?

---

### D254 · Stage Aims vs Lesson Aims
**Idea —** Each stage has its own micro-aim that serves the overall lesson aim. The lead-in does not practise the target language — it activates interest and prior knowledge.
**Example —** Stage aim for a lead-in: 'To engage learners and activate schema about the topic.' Not: 'To practise vocabulary.'
**Try —** Write a stage aim for each stage in your next lesson. Check that each one uses a different verb from the others.

---

### D255 · PPP in a Plan
**Idea —** A PPP plan moves through Presentation (teacher introduces language in context), Practice (controlled activities), and Production (freer use). It is transparent and suits lower levels.
**Example —** Present 'going to' with a contextualised dialogue. Practise with gap-fills. Produce in a short speaking task about weekend plans.
**Try —** Write a PPP lesson plan for a B1 grammar point. Keep each stage aim distinct and your stage timing realistic.

---

### D256 · TTT in a Plan
**Idea —** Test → Teach → Test plans diagnose what learners already know before committing teaching time. The first 'test' is a diagnostic task, not an assessment.
**Example —** Set a short writing task using conditionals. Note errors. Teach the specific gap. Set a similar task. Compare.
**Try —** Design a two-minute diagnostic task that would reveal whether your learners already control your planned target language.

---

### D257 · TBL in a Plan
**Idea —** Task-Based Learning plans lead with a communicative task; language work comes after. The task creates a felt need for the language.
**Example —** Learners plan a class trip together (task), then examine the language they used and needed (analysis), then practise target forms (focus on form).
**Try —** Write a task-based lesson plan for B2 learners. Name the task, the language that will emerge, and the focus-on-form stage.

---

### D258 · ESA in a Plan
**Idea —** Engage → Study → Activate (Harmer) is a flexible framework where the three phases can appear in any order. It suits varied lesson types and mixed aims.
**Example —** A 'Boomerang' ESA: Engage (warmer) → Activate (free task: discover the gap) → Study (focus on language) → Activate again (new task).
**Try —** Plan an ESA lesson that uses the Boomerang pattern. Write the activate stage before the study stage and check the logic holds.

---

### D259 · Timing Stages Realistically
**Idea —** Planned timings are starting estimates. Under-timing is a common error — novice teachers often plan more content than a lesson can hold.
**Example —** A 60-minute lesson with seven stages of 8–10 minutes each works on paper. In practice, transitions and feedback eat the margins.
**Try —** Take your next lesson plan. Add 20% to every stage timing. Does it still fit? If not, cut a stage.

---

### D260 · Interaction Patterns in Plans
**Idea —** Interaction patterns describe who talks to whom: T→S (teacher to student), S→S (pair), S↔S↔S (group), S→T (student reporting). Vary them across a lesson.
**Example —** A lesson that is 90% T→S is a lecture. Plan for each stage to specify a different pattern.
**Try —** Write the interaction pattern next to every stage in your next lesson plan. Count how many minutes are genuinely S→S.

---

### D261 · Materials in the Plan
**Idea —** List every material in your lesson plan: the coursebook page, the handout, the board diagram, the audio file. Prepare, check, and sequence everything before the lesson.
**Example —** 'Handout 1 (gap-fill, one per learner) · Handout 2 (answer key, face-down on desk) · slide 3 (board plan, projected).'
**Try —** Write your materials list for your next lesson. Check each item: can it be prepared 24 hours in advance?

---

### D262 · Board Plans
**Idea —** A board plan shows what will be on the board at each key stage. Deciding it before the lesson prevents mid-lesson thinking about layout and ensures clarity.
**Example —** Left column: target language and form. Centre: a model sentence. Right: error correction space. All decided before learners arrive.
**Try —** Draw your board plan for your next lesson. Use the same column structure every week — consistency helps learners navigate.

---

### D263 · Personal Aims for Teachers
**Idea —** A personal aim is one teaching skill you are working on in a particular lesson. It makes observation feedback targeted rather than general.
**Example —** 'My personal aim is to elicit the target language from learners rather than presenting it myself.' This gives the observer something specific to watch.
**Try —** Identify one teaching habit you want to change. Write it as a personal aim for your next three lessons.

---

### D264 · Lesson Plans for One-to-One Teaching
**Idea —** One-to-one lessons remove the safety of pair and group work. Every interaction is between teacher and learner — so planning for variety and pacing is critical.
**Example —** In a 60-minute one-to-one, plan at least one stage where the learner works independently so they are not always performing for the teacher.
**Try —** Plan a 45-minute one-to-one lesson with four distinct stages. Include at least one independent task.

---

### D265 · Planning for Pronunciation
**Idea —** Pronunciation is often the first thing cut when lesson time is short. Build it into the plan as a stage aim, not an afterthought.
**Example —** 'At the presentation stage, drill the stress pattern of the new chunks: /ˈkuːd ju ˈmaɪnd/ — choral then individual.'
**Try —** Take the target language from your next lesson. Mark the stress. Plan one drilling activity of 90 seconds maximum.

---

### D266 · Planning Lexis into Lessons
**Idea —** Vocabulary is not filler — it needs staging: encounter, notice, use. Pre-teaching blocks meaning; recycling builds retention.
**Example —** Pre-teach two blocking words before a reading text (not ten). After the text, recycle them in a speaking task.
**Try —** Identify the vocabulary load in your next reading text. Choose the two words that would block comprehension. Pre-teach only those.

---

### D267 · Sequencing Tasks — Controlled to Free
**Idea —** Tasks should move from high support to low support. Controlled practice is about accuracy; free practice is about fluency. The sequence builds learner confidence.
**Example —** Gap-fill (controlled) → sentence completion with own words (semi-controlled) → open role play (free). Each step reduces the scaffold.
**Try —** Design a three-task sequence for a grammar point, moving from controlled to free. Check each task is genuinely different in support.

---

### D268 · Adapting Published Materials
**Idea —** Coursebook materials are a starting point, not a contract. Adding, cutting, replacing, or reordering activities to fit your class is the professional norm.
**Example —** A reading text written for British teenagers may need a new context question before it engages adult learners elsewhere.
**Try —** Take one coursebook exercise. Change the context or names while keeping the target language the same. Time yourself — aim for under five minutes.

---

### D269 · Planning a Skills Lesson
**Idea —** A skills lesson (reading, listening, speaking, writing) follows a different shape from a language-systems lesson. Lead-in → task stages → productive follow-up — not PPP.
**Example —** Reading lesson: lead-in → pre-teach two blocking words → gist task → detail task → speaking response to the text.
**Try —** Plan a 50-minute reading lesson. Write explicit task rubrics for the gist and detail stages before you look at the text.

---

### D270 · Planning for Mixed-Ability Groups
**Idea —** Mixed-ability planning means building in choice: an extension task for faster finishers, a support scaffold for those who need it. One plan, two pathways.
**Example —** A writing task gives all learners the same rubric. The support scaffold is a sentence starter; the extension is an additional paragraph prompt.
**Try —** Take your next writing task. Write one support scaffold and one extension. Make both available without announcing who you expect to use which.

---

### D271 · Post-Lesson Reflection
**Idea —** A written reflection after the lesson closes the planning cycle. Without it, the same mistakes repeat. Three questions are enough: What worked? What didn't? What would I change?
**Example —** 'The speaking task ran five minutes over because the prompt was too open. Next time: add a word limit and a timekeeper role.'
**Try —** After your next lesson, take three minutes to write one sentence answering each of the three reflection questions.

---

### D272 · Planning in Series — Units of Work
**Idea —** Individual lesson plans exist inside a longer sequence. A unit of work plans the arc across several lessons: recycling, progression, and cumulative practice.
**Example —** A three-lesson unit on narrative tenses: L1 introduces past simple and continuous, L2 practises them in a reading, L3 uses them in a speaking and writing task.
**Try —** Plan a three-lesson unit around one language area. Map how the target language builds across the three lessons.

---

### D273 · Month 9 Review — Lesson Planning
**Idea —** A lesson plan is a thinking tool: it forces you to name what you want to achieve, anticipate problems, choose a framework, and sequence tasks from controlled to free.
**Example —** The teacher who plans well is flexible in class — not because she ignores the plan, but because she has already thought through the alternatives.
**Try —** Review your last five lesson plans. Identify one element (aim, timing, anticipated problem) that was consistently underdeveloped. Target it next month.
